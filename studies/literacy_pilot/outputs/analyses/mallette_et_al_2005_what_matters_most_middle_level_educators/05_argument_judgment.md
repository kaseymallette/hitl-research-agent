Article: `article.pdf` — Marla H. Mallette, William A. Henk, Jan E. Waggoner, and Carol J. DeLaney, “What Matters Most? A Survey of Accomplished Middle-Level Educators’ Beliefs and Values About Literacy,” *Action in Teacher Education* 27(2), Summer 2005, pp. 33–42.

All page references below use the article’s printed pagination. I read the article using Ghostscript’s `txtwrite` text extraction, including its tables, concluding discussion, references, and author biographies. This judgment uses only the article, the rubric, the evaluation instructions, and the evaluation; no claims reference list or outside sources were consulted.

## B1. Premise acceptability — 2

The evaluation weighs the evidence behind both the descriptive findings and the recommendations, with specific passages. It checks the sampling provenance against pp. 35–37: school-level recognition, seven participating schools out of 12 invited, 90 returns from 345 surveys, and school return rates of 11%–50%. It explains why volunteering and school recognition limit what can be inferred about teachers generally. It also correctly distinguishes nonsignificant demographic comparisons from evidence that nonresponse is harmless or subgroups equivalent.

Its treatment of measurement is substantive. It retains the favorable absolute ratings of both scales, compares the 1.53 and 1.72 means with the response anchors, and checks the item overlap in Tables 2–3 (pp. 37–38). It gives credit to open questions preceding scaled questions (p. 36), to concrete cross-curricular examples (p. 38), and to the authors’ explicit distinction between values and actual instruction (p. 40). These are judgments about evidence quality, rather than acceptance or rejection of the findings on their face.

## B2. Relevance of critique — 1

Most criticisms address what the authors actually claim. The challenge to “little support” in the abstract is grounded in the half-favorable distribution on p. 39. The criticism of interpreting skepticism as disregard attaches to the authors’ actual language about teachers regarding students’ literacies as habits needing repair (p. 40). The objection to state-test reform addresses an explicit recommendation on p. 41. The evaluation also preserves the article’s acknowledgment that beliefs do not establish classroom practice, and recognizes that the authors themselves question the educational significance of the small mean difference (pp. 37, 40).

Two ancillary objections extend beyond the stated claims. The historical critique invokes the absence of comparable earlier measurements, but the discussion’s qualified claim is that, after a history of resistance, teachers in successful schools “do indeed place value” on cross-curricular literacy (p. 40). It does not claim that this survey measures historical change. Similarly, p. 37 uses alpha and the interscale correlation to discuss consistency and distinct aspects of literacy; it does not claim that the categories exhaust literacy or are competing curricular choices. Those are legitimate methodological cautions, but they are not established defects in claims the article makes. The main critique remains relevant, so this is not a central straw man warranting 0.

## B3. Sufficiency — 2

The evaluation consistently distinguishes what the evidence supports from what remains open. It accepts shared responsibility for literacy as a conclusion about respondents’ commitments, while withholding conclusions about enacted teaching. It distinguishes relative scale priorities from rejection of New Literacies, and divided support for integration from a general lack of support. It also distinguishes a literature-based possibility of academic benefit from demonstrated instructional superiority.

For the central recommendations, it identifies what is missing: learning-outcome evidence that could adjudicate competing instructional judgments, an account of how integration develops formal competence and fits limited instructional time, and investigation of whether assessment alignment enables the desired teaching (pp. 34–41). These requests are framed as limits on the reform inference, not as prerequisites for conducting a descriptive survey. The evaluation expressly recognizes that a purposive practitioner sample can serve the original course-design purpose.

## B4. Unstated warrants and missing qualifiers — 2

The evaluation identifies consequential inferential bridges and explains their importance. School achievement and innovation are explicitly offered as reasons to expect unusually strong support for broader literacies (p. 35); the evaluation questions the unstated bridge from those attributes to that particular pedagogical orientation, especially given the designation’s test-based criteria. It also identifies the missing bridge from literacy’s social importance to its appropriate curricular priority: the article establishes that informal practices are real communication, but that alone does not decide instructional allocation or effectiveness (pp. 39–41).

Its alternative reading of skeptical responses is especially useful. Concern about formal language could coexist with recognition of informal communicative competence; the quotations do not require the broader deficit interpretation (pp. 39–40). Finally, it exposes assumptions behind the state-test recommendation: that testing these practices would enable broader teaching and would preserve the values motivating their inclusion (p. 41). The latter is properly presented as a possibility to investigate, not a demonstrated adverse effect.

## B5. Self-attribution — 2

The evaluation clearly separates the authors’ claims, respondents’ beliefs, and its own judgments. Its final paragraph explicitly states its preference for distinguishing recognition from curricular priority and for requiring evidence of instructional benefit. It acknowledges that this framing could underweight the cultural consequences of treating students’ practices as defective. It relates the authors’ framing to their literacy and teacher-education interests, consistent with the course-development account (p. 35) and biographies (p. 42), without treating those interests as grounds for dismissing their work.

The unsupported chronology identified below is a correctness issue; it does not make the evaluator’s reform judgment indistinguishable from the authors’ position.

**Errors: 1 unsupported factual characterization; no numerical or quotation errors found.**

The opening statement that the preferred solution was “largely specified before the survey began” asserts more about the project’s sequence and settled content than p. 35 establishes. The text supports a prior theoretical orientation, but describes debate followed by consultation to inform course creation. This is the same issue as problem 1, not an additional problem count. It should be qualified as an inference about framing rather than reported as established project history.

The historical-transformation and exhaustive/competing-scale objections are relevance overextensions described above; the evaluation does not explicitly report those stronger propositions as quotations or declared author conclusions, so they are not counted again as factual errors. Its hypothetical concern about standardized assessment is likewise clearly attributed to its own reasoning.

The evaluation’s checked quantities match the article: 69/87 and 76/81 on pp. 38–39; seven of 12 schools, 90/345 returns, and 11%–50% return rates on pp. 36–37; scale means 1.53 and 1.72 on p. 37; item means 1.58 and 1.69 on p. 38; and the four response groups on p. 39. Thirty-five favorable responses is a faithful conversion of the article’s “half” of 70, rather than a number separately printed there.

## Assessment for the researcher

The evaluation offers a well-grounded distinction between a credible description of respondents’ priorities and a less fully established case for particular reforms. Its strongest features are close attention to response denominators, favorable absolute ratings, alternative interpretations of teacher comments, and its own evidentiary preferences. Its main weaknesses are an unsupported claim about when the preferred solution was settled and two peripheral critiques aimed beyond the authors’ actual claims. Those qualifications should accompany use of the evaluation; they do not undo its central, article-supported analysis.
