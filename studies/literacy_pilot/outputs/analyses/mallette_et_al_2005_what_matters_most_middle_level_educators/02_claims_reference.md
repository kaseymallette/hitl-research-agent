# Claims reference list: article.pdf

**Article:** Marla H. Mallette, William A. Henk, Jan E. Waggoner, and Carol J. DeLaney, “What Matters Most? A Survey of Accomplished Middle-Level Educators’ Beliefs and Values About Literacy,” *Action in Teacher Education*, 27(2), pp. 33–42 (2005).

## Abstract claims

- **A1 — Study and scope** (p. 33; PDF 1, Abstract). Ninety teachers in award-winning middle schools answered a survey exploring quantitatively and qualitatively how they defined themselves as literacy teachers, viewed multiliteracies in adolescents’ lives, and valued these literacies in the classroom.
- **A2 — Relative valuation** (same location). Mean scores favored Basic Literacies, such as comprehension, word identification, fluency, and writing, over New Literacies, such as media, Internet, critical, and out-of-school literacies.
- **A3 — Literacy across disciplines** (same location). Qualitative responses strongly supported literacy instruction in all disciplines, although interpretations varied.
- **A4 — Strongest agreement** (same location). The greatest positive agreement concerned the proposition that every teacher is a teacher of literacy.
- **A5 — Out-of-school literacies** (same location). There was little support for developing students’ out-of-school literacies in school.
- **A6 — Implications** (same location). The findings have strong implications for changing curricular emphases and bringing teacher practice together with adolescents’ needs and interests.

## Contributions and explicit purposes

The article does not present a numbered contribution list. The following are its explicit statements of purpose and intended contribution, not an invented enumeration of empirical discoveries.

- **P1 — Gap and contribution** (p. 34; PDF 2, end of unheaded introduction). Research on secondary teachers’ beliefs about traditional literacy is scarce, and still less examines middle-school teachers’ and administrators’ beliefs and values under the broader reconceptualization of adolescent literacy. The study examines accomplished middle-school teachers’ literacy-teaching roles, views of adolescents’ multiliteracies, and the classroom value they assign to them.
- **P2 — Three research questions** (p. 35; PDF 3, end of Adolescent Literacy). The study asks: (1) whether middle-grade educators recognize and value multiple literacies; (2) how they define their literacy-teaching roles; and (3) which aspects of literacy they value most. The motivating comparison is whether educators who spend time with adolescents share the beliefs of adolescent-literacy researchers.
- **P3 — Practical and research contributions** (p. 35; PDF 3, Genesis of the Survey; p. 41; PDF 9, final Discussion paragraph). The survey was intended to bring practicing educators’ perspectives into a new middle-grades literacy course and yield research in its own right. The study ultimately attempts to give adolescent literacy a sharper pragmatic focus by attending to the voices of people with a vested interest in adolescents’ education.

## Section and subsection conclusions and supporting claims

### Unheaded introduction (pp. 33–34; PDF 1–2)

- **S1 — Developmental need and historical problem.** Adolescence is a distinctive, important period of literacy development, requiring teachers to scaffold students toward more advanced literacy. Exemplary middle-grades programs exist but are often exceptions. Earlier attention to reading processes commonly gives way to subject-content acquisition; historically, many teachers assigned literacy instruction to English/language-arts teachers instead of sharing responsibility across subjects. The historical and developmental premises are supported with the article’s cited literature.
- **S2 — Broadened field and concluding rationale.** The late-1990s shift from secondary reading to adolescent literacy broadened the construct and was followed by attention to literacy’s social and political nature. The shortage of research into educators’ beliefs under that broader view motivates the study described in P1.

### Adolescent Literacy (pp. 34–35; PDF 2–3)

- **S3 — Meaning of multiple literacies** (p. 34; PDF 2, opening paragraphs). Drawing on Moje and colleagues and Gee, literacy extends beyond academic reading and writing to multiple ways of communicating, thinking, speaking, interacting, and valuing in particular social settings. Home-based ways of using language and making meaning need not coincide with academic literacy.
- **S4 — Social construction and classroom organization** (same location). The contemporary conception is socially constructed: learning occurs in situated contexts through interaction, dialogue, and negotiated meaning. People draw on diverse information sources in everyday learning communities, whereas subject divisions and allotted classroom periods discourage exchange of information and use of varied texts and media.
- **S5 — Technology, motivation, and changing demands** (p. 34; PDF 2, middle paragraphs). The reviewed constructivist and critical research examines both school and out-of-school print/media literacies. In the cited research, technological tools motivate students, and technology-mediated composing in social learning communities has the potential to promote academic interests. As information and communication technologies redefine literacy demands, classroom instruction ought to change. Citing Alvermann, the authors say adolescents’ cultural, linguistic, and critical literacies are not keeping pace with information-age demands or increased reading standards.
- **S6 — Student-centered instruction** (p. 34; PDF 2, final paragraph). Adolescent literacy requires placing adolescents at the center of instruction and moving beyond narrow definitions confined to basic decoding and factual comprehension. Those narrow definitions fail to provide motivation and meaning in adolescents’ media-rich lives and overlook their distinctive psychosocial needs; adolescents deserve comprehensive support for continued reading and writing development.
- **S7 — Concluding research rationale** (p. 35; PDF 3, before Method). Researchers have learned adolescents’ literacy roles and values, reading preferences, and reasons for reading by spending time with them, listening, and observing interactions—activities educators presumably do daily. This leads to the question of whether educators share researchers’ beliefs and to the three explicit questions in P2.

### Method (pp. 35–36; PDF 3–4)

Method is a parent heading whose account is completed by the following subsections; it has no separate concluding paragraph or finding.

#### Genesis of the Survey: TQE Initiative (p. 35; PDF 3)

- **S8 — Origin and course-design outcome.** A Teacher Quality Enhancement grant at a large Midwestern university prompted an eight-person group to design a literacy course for preparation of grades 5–8 teachers. The group included literacy faculty, writing-assessment specialists, an adolescent-literature specialist, a nationally board-certified middle-grades language-arts teacher, and a reading-education doctoral candidate. Debate about literacy topics and time allocation led to the decision that practicing middle-level educators’ perspectives should inform the course.
- **S9 — Subsection conclusion.** The resulting survey was designed to reflect adolescent-literacy research and authentic, field-based beliefs and practices in distinguished schools around the state. Its results would be incorporated into the course to increase real-world applicability and reported as research (P3).

#### Participants (p. 35; PDF 3)

- **S10 — Target population and designation.** The intended respondents were teachers and administrators in 12 Illinois middle-grades schools (grades 5–8) designated Blue Ribbon within the preceding five years. The authors describe the national designation as honoring academically superior schools or schools with dramatic achievement gains. They report that the criteria changed in 2002 in response to No Child Left Behind: either at least 40% of students from disadvantaged backgrounds with dramatic improvement on state assessments, or performance in the top 10% on state assessments.
- **S11 — Selection rationale and subsection conclusion.** The schools were in ethnically, culturally, and linguistically diverse areas. Blue Ribbon schools were selected because the authors expected schools demonstrating innovation and academic excellence to be most likely to embrace adolescent literacies. This is the authors’ sampling rationale, not a demonstrated result about all Blue Ribbon schools.

#### Data Collection (pp. 35–36; PDF 3–4)

This is a parent heading developed through Sampling and Instrumentation, without a separate conclusion.

##### Sampling (pp. 35–36; PDF 3–4)

- **S12 — Recruitment procedure.** Administrators of all 12 schools were telephoned to request their own and their teaching staffs’ participation. Respondents had two weeks to complete the survey, followed by another call asking principals to encourage all teachers to participate. Actual participation and returns are reported under Results (S16).

##### Instrumentation (p. 36; PDF 4)

- **S13 — Instrument content.** The survey collected demographic information on teaching experience, grades and subjects, education, certification/endorsements, professional development, and gender. Five open-ended questions asked whether literacy was a major teaching responsibility; how students used literacy personally; whether those uses should influence instruction; how literacy instruction occurred in the school; and reactions to every teacher being a literacy teacher.
- **S14 — Quantitative instrument and ordering rationale.** The final section used Likert-type items about literacy topics in preparation of middle-grades teachers across subjects and in instruction of middle-grade students. Topics included reading/writing teaching and assessment, struggling readers, integration into content areas, and media, Internet, critical, visual, global, popular-cultural, in/out-of-school, and culturally/linguistically diverse literacies. Coding was 1 = Essential, 2 = Very Important, 3 = Somewhat Important, and 4 = Not Important; lower scores therefore indicate greater importance. Likert items followed open-ended questions to reduce their influence on free-form answers.

#### Data Analyses (p. 36; PDF 4)

- **S15 — Analysis and coding outcome.** Quantitative analysis involved demographic frequency distributions, item means and standard deviations, correlations, and a t-test comparing scales. Concurrent qualitative content analysis used two researchers’ independent coding. Three questions’ responses converged into a literacy-across-the-curriculum category; category schemes for the remaining questions were similar. The only reported discrepancy concerned naming a category of out-of-school literacy uses, resolved by rereading the responses and agreeing on a broad label. The later Results also reports ANOVAs (S17).

### Results: participation and demographic analyses (pp. 36–37; PDF 4–5)

- **S16 — Returns and actual sample.** Seven of 12 administrators agreed to participate; the others declined to avoid burdening teachers with another survey. Of 345 surveys mailed to teachers and administrators in those seven schools, 90 were returned, a 26% return rate. All seven schools were represented; school-specific return rates ranged from 11% to 50%. No administrators returned surveys: all actual respondents were teachers.
- **S17 — Group comparisons and pooled reporting.** The authors report no statistically significant differences in analyses by years taught, education, school, or certification. Forty-nine respondents (54%) currently taught or had previously taught reading; 46% reported no reading-instruction experience. Likert-item analyses found no significant differences between these two groups either. The authors therefore report the teachers as one group. Teachers in the reading category had marked other subject areas and were not necessarily reading teachers at the time of the study. Table 1 supplies the teaching-background distribution.

#### Quantitative Findings (p. 37; PDF 5; Tables 2–3 on p. 38/PDF 6)

- **S18 — Basic Literacies.** Comprehension, vocabulary, word identification, fluency, writing, at-risk students, and literature/content-area literacy received favorable ratings. Item means ranged from 1.30 to 1.74, between Essential and Very Important; the narrative describes standard deviations as similar and around .70. Exact table values are preserved below.
- **S19 — New Literacies.** Media, Internet, visual, global, critical, out-of-school literacies, and diversity received item means of 1.58–1.81. They were regarded as somewhat less essential than Basic Literacies, while remaining between Essential and Very Important on the instrument.
- **S20 — Scale difference and qualification.** Basic and New Literacies grand means were 1.53 and 1.72 respectively. Both scales had alpha internal-consistency estimates of nearly .90. A correlated-samples t-test found a statistically significant difference, reported as t = 3.25, p < .0016. The authors acknowledge that the numerically small difference raises questions about educational significance and say the qualitative analysis clarifies it.
- **S21 — Subsection conclusion: related but distinct aspects.** The scale correlation was .52, interpreted by the authors as about 25% shared variance. They conclude that the scales were somewhat similar but measured decidedly different aspects of literacy.

#### Qualitative Findings (pp. 38–40; PDF 6–8)

- **S22 — Relationship to quantitative results** (p. 38; PDF 6, opening). Qualitative findings were similar to quantitative findings and added insight into the ratings. Three open-ended questions converged on literacy across the curriculum; teachers from all disciplines strongly endorsed this idea, which was becoming part of school improvement plans.
- **S23 — Major teaching responsibility** (p. 38; PDF 6). Of 87 respondents to this question, 69 (nearly 80%) considered literacy a major teaching responsibility, 10 (about 12%) did not, and eight considered it part of their teaching but not a major part. Examples linked literacy to curriculum-wide integration, mathematical vocabulary and inferencing, and historical primary sources, context, and vocabulary.
- **S24 — Reported school practices and varied interpretations** (p. 38; PDF 6). Of 67 respondents describing their schools’ literacy instruction, 47 said it occurred across the curriculum. Examples concerned subject-specific reading and shared reading/writing expectations across classes. The other 20 responses concerned particular methods or respondents’ personal instruction, including classroom libraries, reading strategies, response writing, and literature circles. Teachers interpreted the question in different ways.
- **S25 — Strongest positive agreement** (pp. 38–39; PDF 6–7). The proposition that every teacher is a literacy teacher elicited the greatest positive agreement: of 81 responses, 76 were favorable, three negative, and two conditional on an ideal world. Favorable comments stressed incorporating literacy in every curriculum, encouraging reading and understanding, and shared responsibility.
- **S26 — Conclusion of the three-question cluster** (p. 39; PDF 7, first full paragraph). Together these responses strongly support the conclusion that literacy was valued across the curriculum in the sampled Blue Ribbon schools, with teachers believing all teachers responsible for literacy instruction. The authors emphasize that these views occurred across grade levels and subject areas.
- **S27 — Four categories of perceived personal literacy uses** (p. 39; PDF 7). Responses concerning students’ out-of-school literacy uses produced four thematic categories: Personal Enjoyment (magazines, newspapers, novels, entertainment, diaries, journals, notes, family reading); New Literacies (video-game guides, Internet, email, instant messaging, media); School (homework, required independent reading, current events); and Survival/Functional (business communication, menus, functioning in society and daily activities). These are teachers’ perceptions of students’ uses.
- **S28 — Social and changing character of literacy** (p. 39; PDF 7). Numerous responses explicitly or implicitly recognized literacy’s social uses for communication and interaction. A teacher’s observation that students write much more through email, perhaps developing new linguistic ground despite perceived clumsiness, illustrates the changing character of out-of-school literacies; it is a respondent’s observation rather than a measured change in student reading/writing volume.
- **S29 — Weaker support for instructional influence** (p. 39; PDF 7). Of 70 responses on whether personal/out-of-school uses should influence instruction, only half were favorable; 19 were negative, 12 mixed, and four indicated uncertainty about what out-of-school literacies meant. The authors contrast this with the much stronger endorsement of literacy across subjects.
- **S30 — Favorable respondents’ rationale** (p. 39; PDF 7). Support for merging traditional and new literacies seemed based on connecting teaching with students’ interests, increasing enthusiasm, and making instruction relevant and effective.
- **S31 — Subsection’s closing finding: resistance and basic/formal literacy concerns** (pp. 39–40; PDF 7–8, ending just before Discussion). Opponents worried about future preparedness and basic language skills, emphasizing employment/life skills, appreciation of literacy, formal usage, and complete sentences. The authors interpret such teachers as apparently excluding students’ out-of-school literacies from being literate. Respondent examples criticize abbreviated messaging and resist building curricula around email and chat rooms.

### Discussion, including the article’s overall conclusions (pp. 40–41; PDF 8–9)

- **S32 — Convergent methods and positive Basic Literacy finding** (p. 40; PDF 8, opening). Quantitative and qualitative results mutually support different valuations of Basic and New Literacies. The authors regard Basic Literacy findings as positive: teachers in schools designated successful appear to value literacy teaching across all subjects after a long history of resistance.
- **S33 — Explicit limit on the practice claim** (same paragraph). The study does not establish how teachers actually teach literacy across the curriculum. Brief reports of particular strategies offer only small glimpses of practices teachers may or may not use. Endorsement of literacy across subjects must not be converted into an observed finding about instructional quality or implementation.
- **S34 — Lower valuation and limited breadth of recognition** (p. 40; PDF 8, second paragraph). Teachers appeared to value out-of-school literacies described in research less than Basic Literacies. They showed some knowledge and appreciation of students’ personal literacies but seldom mentioned sign systems, graffiti, drama, or music. The authors call the lukewarm support for bringing these literacies into school disappointing in light of research on their value in adolescents’ lives.
- **S35 — Proposed explanation for resistance** (p. 40; PDF 8, third paragraph). Although many educators wanted to connect with students, they did not seem to recognize out-of-school literacy as a means of doing so. The authors suggest, tentatively, that resistance may stem from treating out-of-school uses as extensions of traditional school goals rather than influences on instruction. They interpret teachers’ statements about success and adult survival as indicating that teachers do not believe classroom integration of out-of-school literacies will achieve those goals.
- **S36 — Missed opportunities and contextual values** (p. 40; PDF 8, fourth paragraph). This thinking makes sense within a standards-driven, high-stakes testing system, but overlooks students’ needs and interests and misses opportunities. Respondents seemed to regard multiple literacies as habits needing repair. Instant messaging and chat rooms nevertheless constitute real communication in students’ lives. The resulting disconnect illustrates the cited argument that a community’s values determine which literacies it values and uses.
- **S37 — Research and fundamental changes in practice** (pp. 40–41; PDF 8–9, paragraph beginning with Resnick). Drawing on Resnick, the article argues that school is only one of many forces shaping literacy and that research into practices both inside and outside school is necessary for understanding and responding to literacy problems. Without systematic out-of-school research, educators cannot successfully confront the problem. Because dominant school practices may conflict with students’ practical and pleasurable everyday literacies, fundamental changes would be necessary to address students’ distinctive needs relevantly.
- **S38 — Broaden texts rather than remediate presumed deficits** (p. 41; PDF 9, continuation of preceding paragraph). Requirements to learn standard English do not imply that students’ literacies need deficit remediation; drawing on Gallego and Hollingsworth, the authors instead call for expanding what counts as texts.
- **S39 — Implications for curriculum and teacher learning** (p. 41; PDF 9, penultimate Discussion paragraph). A challenging, responsive literacy curriculum should stretch adolescents’ thinking and ways of reading their worlds, as advocated by Moje and colleagues. Studying teachers’ values could be a starting point. This study would seem to have strong implications for preservice education and staff development that communicate the value of broadly and authentically defined literacy. Teachers must examine their values and practices as literacy changes, centering adolescents and their lives in that process.
- **S40 — Implication for assessment** (same paragraph). State tests should somehow reflect New Literacies to give teachers license to value and pursue a broader range of literacy instructional outcomes. This is a recommendation, not a tested assessment intervention.
- **S41 — Final conclusion and contribution** (p. 41; PDF 9, final Discussion paragraph). Adolescent literacy is an important component of lifelong literacy. Researchers studying it should learn from those who work most closely with adolescents, and researchers in all literacy areas can benefit from its distinctive and changing character. Successful changes to adolescent instruction require listening to stakeholders’ voices. The present study attempted to sharpen the construct’s pragmatic focus (P3).
