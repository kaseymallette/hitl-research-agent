Source title: What Matters Most? A Survey of Accomplished Middle-Level Educators' Beliefs and Values About Literacy
Source type: peer_reviewed_paper
Publisher: Action in Teacher Education
DOI: null

Mallette and colleagues offer a useful account of teachers' stated priorities, but their case for changing those priorities is less securely established than their account of what respondents believe. Published in Action in Teacher Education in summer 2005, the paper addresses readers positioned to influence teacher preparation and professional development. Its immediate origin makes that purpose concrete: a university team designing a middle-grades literacy course wanted practitioners' advice about content and instructional time. The article consequently does two jobs. It reports a survey and advocates a broader conception of literacy. Those purposes can support each other, but they create tension when teachers' answers become evidence of a problem whose preferred solution was largely specified before the survey began.

The argument rests on three main premises. Adolescents continue to need literacy instruction across subjects; literacy includes socially situated communication beyond academic reading and writing; and instruction should engage adolescents' existing communicative lives. The first premise receives substantial support from respondents. The second is developed through the literature review's social constructivist framework. The third combines an educational value judgment with claims about motivation and learning. The article's strongest inference is that these respondents accept shared responsibility for literacy. Its weakest transition runs from recognizing everyday communication as literacy to prescribing its curricular incorporation and eventual recognition in state tests. That transition requires judgments about educational benefit, priorities, and institutional effects that the survey cannot settle.

The cross-curricular finding is convincing within its proper scope. Sixty-nine of 87 respondents regarded literacy as a major teaching responsibility, and 76 of 81 endorsed the proposition that every teacher teaches literacy (pp. 38–39). Examples from mathematics and history explain what that endorsement can mean: interpreting vocabulary, making inferences, and understanding primary sources. These examples strengthen the evidence beyond agreement with an attractive slogan. The authors also explicitly acknowledge that reported values do not establish classroom practice (p. 40). That qualification matters: the study supports a finding about professional commitments, while leaving their implementation open. Its historical framing is less conclusive. Contrasting these responses with a century of resistance supplies perspective, but without comparable earlier measurements the survey cannot demonstrate a historical transformation.

Even the descriptive conclusions require a narrower population than the title's accomplished educators might suggest. Seven of 12 invited Illinois schools participated, and 90 of 345 surveys were returned; individual school return rates ranged from 11% to 50% (pp. 36–37). School recognition does not certify every respondent's instructional expertise, and the views of volunteers cannot establish the prevalence of beliefs among all teachers in those schools. The reported absence of significant demographic differences does not resolve nonresponse or demonstrate subgroup equivalence. Nevertheless, a purposive sample is reasonable for the original course-design problem: these are relevant practitioners whose perspectives deserve consideration. The difficulty arises when their responses carry implications for teachers generally.

The selection rationale also unsettles the paper's implicit expectation that even these teachers ought to embrace broader literacies. The authors chose Blue Ribbon schools because academic excellence and innovation supposedly made such acceptance especially likely (p. 35). Yet the designation, as they describe it, depends on achievement or gains on state assessments. That could instead select institutions particularly committed to tested academic outcomes. The paper later invokes testing pressures to explain teachers' reluctance, without adequately reconciling that explanation with its sampling premise. These schools are informative cases, but their recognition does not make them a demonstrated best case for adopting the authors' preferred conception.

The numerical results establish relative priorities more clearly than resistance. Basic Literacies average 1.53 and New Literacies 1.72 on a scale where 1 means essential and 2 very important (p. 37). Both receive favorable ratings. Individual items overlap: critical literacy, at 1.58, is rated more highly than word identification, at 1.69 (p. 38). The statistically significant aggregate difference therefore does not support a simple division between valued basics and disregarded alternatives. The authors recognize the small numerical gap and turn to qualitative findings for practical meaning. That is a sensible mixed-methods move, provided the qualitative evidence is allowed to complicate the numerical story. High internal consistency supports coherent response patterns within the scales; it does not establish that the two groupings exhaust literacy or represent competing curricular choices. Moreover, the instrument description distinguishes questions about teacher preparation from questions about student instruction (p. 36), leaving some uncertainty about how directly comparable the ratings are.

The open responses reveal a real disagreement about classroom integration, but they also expose overstatement. Of 70 respondents, 35 were favorable, 19 negative, 12 mixed, and four unclear about the relevant concept (p. 39). Calling this little support in the abstract encourages a stronger impression of rejection than the distribution warrants. Half favored integration, while mixed answers need not indicate hostility. The defensible finding is divided support, markedly weaker than endorsement of shared literacy responsibility. The authors' decision to place open questions before scaled items usefully reduces prompting, but it also makes respondents' own understandings especially important. Failure to mention graffiti, drama, or music cannot by itself demonstrate that a teacher discounts them; respondents were not supplying exhaustive inventories of literacy.

The interpretation of skeptical comments deserves particular scrutiny. One teacher resists constructing a curriculum around email and chat rooms; another emphasizes complete sentences (pp. 39–40). These statements support concern about formal competence and curricular emphasis. They do not necessarily show a failure to recognize informal communication as meaningful literacy. A teacher could acknowledge its sophistication while judging that school should prioritize capabilities students acquire less readily elsewhere. Conversely, the teachers favoring integration predict greater enthusiasm and effectiveness through students' interests. Those predictions are plausible, but they remain beliefs too. The study supplies no learning outcomes that would establish either side's instructional judgment. Treating enthusiastic responses as pedagogically insightful while treating skeptical ones chiefly as evidence of conceptual deficiency gives the preferred position an evidentiary advantage it has not earned.

The literature review supplies reasons to take that preferred position seriously. Its account of home language practices challenges the assumption that divergence from school conventions signifies absence of competence. Its cited example of technology-mediated composing identifies a potential connection between participation and academic interest (p. 34). But potential is not demonstrated superiority, and the value of a practice in adolescents' lives does not determine the best way to use it instructionally. The closing proposal to expand what counts as texts offers a constructive alternative to dismissing students' communication. What remains missing is an account of how such expansion would develop formal competence, address uneven skills, and justify its demands on limited instructional time. Those questions are especially pertinent because allocating course time generated the study.

The recommendation that state tests reflect New Literacies is further removed from the evidence (p. 41). It assumes that assessment alignment would enable broader teaching and that assessing these practices would serve the values motivating their inclusion. The survey does not investigate either assumption. If socially situated meaning matters, converting diverse practices into standardized assessment targets might also change what is being valued. That possibility deserves the same attention the paper gives the constraining effects of existing tests.

The authors' literacy and teacher-education backgrounds help explain their framing without discrediting it. They seek recognition for adolescent competence and stronger connections between school and everyday life. Their disappointment with teachers follows from those commitments. My own evaluation gives considerable weight to distinguishing recognition from curricular priority and to requiring evidence for claims of instructional benefit. That preference could underweight the cultural consequences of treating students' existing practices as defective. Even allowing for that, the survey earns a call for inquiry and professional dialogue more clearly than a mandate for particular reforms. Its most valuable contribution is the unresolved tension it documents: teachers may endorse literacy across subjects while disagreeing about which communicative practices schools should develop. Taking that disagreement seriously would make teacher preparation more responsive to the practitioners the study set out to hear.
