Source title: Exploring the Value of a Summer Literacy Program on the Learning of At-Risk Adolescents
Source type: peer_reviewed_paper
Publisher: Literacy Research and Instruction (Association of Literacy Educators and Researchers)
DOI: 10.1080/19388070802434907

Mallette and colleagues make a persuasive case that struggling adolescents’ educational experiences deserve attention beyond test scores. They make a less secure case that caring relationships explain the literacy gains they observed, or their apparent failure to carry into ordinary schooling. Published in 2009 in Literacy Research and Instruction, the article addresses literacy researchers, teacher educators, and practitioners. Its purpose combines an exploratory evaluation of a summer intervention with advocacy for a broader understanding of effective instruction. The concluding appeal to consider caring alongside scientifically based reading instruction responds explicitly to high-stakes testing pressures. That setting helps explain why an inconclusive evaluation becomes an argument about what educators should value.

The argument begins with national dropout and reading statistics, then moves through research on comprehension instruction, belonging, and caring to a small local intervention. The statistics establish urgency; they cannot establish that this particular program addresses the causes of dropout. More consequential is the premise that academic and affective dimensions must be considered together. This premise is plausible and supported by the literature the authors summarize, including associations between perceived teacher caring and motivation. However, the move from association to explanation remains unfinished. The authors’ ethical commitment to supporting adolescents also does work that empirical findings alone cannot: opportunities for success and respectful relationships are treated as educational goods even when their contribution to measured achievement remains uncertain.

The paper announces its contextual interpretation in the abstract and prepares it before presenting results. Its attention is revealing. Detailed clinical accounts show how tutors adapted instruction; extended student correspondence establishes affection and attachment. Later school achievement receives a comparatively compressed account. This allocation makes the summer experience vivid while leaving the contrast with regular school less fully investigated. The academic and affective lenses were planned from the beginning, so the relational interpretation is not simply an excuse invented after disappointing follow-up results. Nevertheless, advance commitment to that lens makes it especially important to distinguish evidence of relationships from evidence that relationships explain learning.

The strongest quantitative finding is a rise in mean normal curve equivalent scores from 21.70 to 31.03 among 27 completers, with a paired-test probability of .001 and a reported effect size of .43 (p. 177). This supports a clear descriptive conclusion: these students performed better at the end of the program. It does not identify the program’s effect relative to what would otherwise have happened. There is no comparison group, and repeated testing, changes in effort, and other influences remain possible explanations. Excluding the three students removed for behavioral incidents also means that the positive results describe completers rather than everyone initially enrolled. This matters particularly when the argument concerns whether a supportive environment can engage adolescents at risk of exclusion.

The authors deserve credit for questioning their own preferred reading of the score gains. On page 181, they suggest that belonging might have encouraged greater effort, making post-test performance a better expression of existing ability rather than solely evidence of newly acquired skill. That distinction is educationally valuable: a score reflects a performance under particular conditions. Yet caring is only one possible account of changed effort, and neither effort nor its causes were independently measured. The explanation remains permitted by the evidence, rather than required by it. Likewise, the reported effect size is standardized using the conventional NCE standard deviation; its moderate label does not itself demonstrate instructional importance or causation.

The clinical examples provide credible evidence that some students learned particular strategies and participated more fully. Andre’s increasingly meaningful questions offer a concrete account of change, while Marvin’s report explicitly notes that improved fluency on a practiced text did not transfer to other texts (pp. 175–177). That admission should constrain the whole argument. Denisha’s fluent performance after weeks of practice is a worthwhile accomplishment, but does not by itself establish generalized reading improvement. The authors also disclose ineffective strategies and an unfinished writing activity. These details strengthen the report’s credibility while supporting a bounded conclusion: students achieved some identifiable successes during intensive, tailored instruction. They do not show how widespread or durable those successes were.

The long-term evidence cannot resolve durability. Subsequent grades were mostly Ds and Fs, thirteen students were retained, and one dropped out (p. 178). These outcomes establish serious continuing difficulty. They do not demonstrate that summer learning disappeared. The later measures are grades, promotion decisions, and a state assessment rather than a repeated equivalent measure of the summer gains. Students could have retained improvements while remaining far below curricular demands; their average summer reading level was still fifth grade. Conversely, ten later promotions cannot demonstrate a program benefit without a credible comparison. The authors explicitly deny causal force to either the favorable or unfavorable framing, which is sound. Their statement that the summer gains were not realized in the following year is defensible as a claim about school success, but stronger than the evidence warrants if read as loss of reading ability.

The relational evidence is strongest when read as testimony about meaningful experience. Students wanted to return, missed their tutors, and maintained affectionate contact. Bianca’s voluntary rereading and pursuit of a trilogy provide a particularly concrete indication of continuing engagement with books (p. 179). However, she alone answered all three letters, and the researchers supplied the third book. Her story shows both the potential value of continued support and the difficulty of treating follow-up as observation after a completed intervention. Optional correspondence also privileges students willing to remain connected. Without response totals, a clear interview denominator, or fuller accounts of dissenting experiences, the reader cannot tell how representative the warmest testimony is. The brief coding description does little to show how the researchers tested interpretations against contrary cases.

Most importantly, affection for tutors cannot isolate caring as the active ingredient. Students also received unusually intensive staffing, individualized texts, recreation, meals, career exploration, and access to a university. These provisions could foster belonging, motivation, and learning through several interacting routes. The authors’ own account of reciprocity permits another sequence: successful instruction could build trust, which then improves engagement. Caring might be a condition, a consequence, or part of a reinforcing process. The final suggestion that it may be as important as scientifically based instruction is therefore an educational hypothesis, not a comparative finding. A reasonable alternative conclusion is that sustained access to the program’s combined resources matters more than any single relational quality.

The contrast with regular teachers requires the same caution. Students’ reports of incomprehensible science instruction and widespread failure deserve attention. But the researchers acknowledge having only a glimpse of school relationships (p. 180). The teachers’ bleak judgment about promotion is explained as an assessment of grades already too low to recover, rather than direct evidence of indifference. Comparing richly observed summer tutors with sparsely observed schoolteachers risks giving the former both a favorable setting and interpretive generosity. Poverty and the predominantly African American composition of the cohort locate the study socially, but the explanation gives much more attention to interpersonal care than to how material constraints or racialized expectations might shape schooling. Those possibilities remain unexamined rather than disproved.

My own judgment is shaped by two commitments that need separating: students’ accounts of belonging have value in themselves, and claims about instructional mechanisms require evidence beyond those accounts. A narrow demand for causal estimates would undervalue what this exploratory study reveals; sympathy with its humane purpose could equally turn a plausible story into a demonstrated explanation. I judge the paper successful as an account of specific learning opportunities and relationships that mattered to some adolescents, and as a challenge to equating low attainment with incapacity. It supports examining instruction and students’ experiences together. It leaves open whether caring produced the measured gains, whether those gains persisted, and how the program’s benefits could be sustained in ordinary classrooms. Creating opportunities for success is a defensible recommendation here; assigning comparative explanatory weight to caring outruns the study.
