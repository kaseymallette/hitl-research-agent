Source title: A Critical Analysis of Two Preservice Teachers' Knowledge of Struggling Readers: Raced, Classed, and Gendered?
Source type: peer_reviewed_paper
Publisher: Reading Research and Instruction (Taylor & Francis)
DOI: 10.1080/19388070009558323

Mallette and her colleagues make a persuasive argument that prospective teachers can mistake familiar ways of supporting literacy for universal explanations of children's difficulties. They make a less secure argument that their cases establish how specifically raced, classed, and gendered identities produce those explanations. Published in 2000 in a specialist reading research and instruction journal, the paper addresses researchers and teacher educators who can change preparation programs. Its purpose extends beyond describing two teachers: it seeks to redirect professional attention from instructional knowledge toward the cultural assumptions organizing that knowledge. I find that redirection justified, while treating the proposed identity-centered curriculum as a plausible response whose effectiveness remains unestablished here.

The paper's architecture makes its commitments clear. The authors revisit an earlier interpretive study because its account of experience-based meaning seemed insufficiently deep. Before presenting the cases, they introduce dominant-group ideology, unequal schooling, and the cultural alignment between school literacy and middle-class homes. These premises establish the categories through which the data will become intelligible. The reasoning is that teachers develop expectations through socially situated experience; schools privilege some experiences; therefore, teachers who universalize their expectations may reproduce those privileges. A further normative premise is that teacher education should interrupt this process. The abstract announces the finding and recommendation before either is earned; the case narratives subsequently provide the strongest support for the narrower claim about unexamined expectations. The broader structural interpretation depends substantially on the cited theoretical literature.

Kaley's case supplies the clearest connection between belief and consequential action. She attributes reading difficulties to insufficient early parental involvement, expects school to provide support unavailable at home, and advocates withholding a grade unless a parent signs an assignment slip (pp. 227–228). The signature requirement matters more than her initial discomfort with an unfamiliar household. It converts a belief about desirable adult behavior into a condition imposed on a child's academic recognition. Even if shared reading helps children, that does not justify making credit for their work depend on someone else's compliance. The authors' criticism succeeds through the contradiction between the intervention's supportive purpose and its allocation of responsibility. No comprehensive theory of hegemony is needed to recognize this problem, although that theory usefully asks why such a practice can appear reasonable.

The evidence also supports criticism of Kaley's inference about the home. A child reporting that nobody reads to her does not establish that her family provides no educational support. Kaley apparently neither investigates other literacy practices nor collaborates with the parents. Yet the authors have limited evidence of those practices themselves. Their conclusion is strongest as a demand to investigate before attributing deficiency. It would be weaker if taken to establish that the household already provided adequate support in forms Kaley failed to recognize. Likewise, Moesha's preference for recreational over school reading challenges Kaley's prediction, but does not by itself identify the cause of that preference. Difficulty with school tasks, classroom relationships, or greater freedom outside school could fit the result. The survey exposes a mistaken expectation more directly than it establishes a white middle-class ideology of schooling.

Gayle's case demonstrates a different, valuable process: revision in response to information her original account could not accommodate. Her assessments already help her understand Scott's frustration rather than merely judge his unfinished work. Later, learning that his brother has been shot leads her to recognize security and survival as conditions affecting learning (pp. 229–230). The authors deserve credit for preserving the incompleteness of this change. Gayle retains faith in education and reports modest improvement rather than claiming transformation. Her developing account accommodates instruction, emotion, and circumstances together. This supports the conclusion that working closely with a child can complicate a teacher's inherited explanations.

Whether that change demonstrates reflection on racial identity is less clear. Gayle's quoted statements address fear, safety, and her own limited power. They do not explicitly reconsider Whiteness or identify a racial mechanism producing Scott's circumstances. Calling her recognition of violence an encounter with a different culture risks making violence stand for the child's culture when it establishes an urgent condition of his life. Race and class may shape exposure to danger, but the case does not trace that relationship. Gender is thinner still: motherhood, the children's genders, and Scott's felt responsibility for female relatives offer possible avenues of analysis, yet the authors scarcely develop them. Their three-part identity vocabulary promises more differentiation than the evidence delivers. The warranted judgment is that Gayle recognizes circumstances unlike her own; the more specific interpretation remains possible rather than compelled.

The methodological limitations concern interpretive accountability more than sample size alone. Two purposively selected cases can illuminate a process without estimating its prevalence. Here, however, they were selected as opposite ends of a continuum already defined by the analysis, while the four intervening cases remain undescribed. This produces a legible contrast without letting readers assess how well the continuum organizes the whole set. The account explains that the researchers used an a priori coding system, but gives little detail about how competing readings were considered or what would have counted against the preferred explanation. Both persistence and change can be classified as relations to ideology. That flexibility can illuminate complexity, but also makes the framework difficult to challenge unless the interpretation's boundaries are specified.

Other differences between the cases deserve more explanatory weight. Gayle has parenting and preschool experience, is older, and responds positively to the preparation program; Kaley is described as uncertain about assignments and resistant to coursework. They also work in different schools with different children. These differences do not disprove an ideological interpretation. They do prevent the contrast from establishing which features explain their differing trajectories. The favorable introduction to Gayle and unfavorable introduction to Kaley also prepare readers to accept one as reflective and the other as rigid. Because the first author taught the course and supplied feedback on the written assignments, the records are products of an instructional relationship as well as expressions of belief. The authors acknowledge their own interpretive role and distinguish retrospective critical analysis from reciprocal, action-oriented critical research. Those are substantive strengths, though acknowledgment cannot substitute for testing alternative interpretations.

The treatment of meritocracy similarly needs a distinction between an unconditional promise and a practical possibility. Evidence that schools distribute opportunities unequally undermines the claim that effort guarantees success for everyone. It does not establish that good instruction, effort, or educational attainment cannot improve a particular child's prospects. Gayle's hope for Scott is therefore compatible with recognizing structural injustice. Indeed, the authors' own commitment to helping children become literate presupposes that educational action remains worthwhile. Their analysis would be sharper if it separated confidence that teaching can help from confidence that schooling neutralizes inequality. Otherwise, reasonable professional commitments risk becoming evidence of ideological captivity simply because they coexist with unequal institutions.

The recommendations follow with varying force. Requiring teachers to substantiate judgments, learn about families, and examine the effects of their instructional decisions responds directly to the cases. Revising the assignment to investigate how schools help or inhibit children also addresses an identifiable omission. Home visits and a culturally responsive curriculum integrated across a program are defensible proposals, but the study does not compare them with focused mentoring, explicit challenges to deficit explanations, or other ways of supporting revision. Neither case tests the recommended curriculum. Visits could provide missing knowledge, yet their value would depend on listening and collaboration; observation filtered through unchanged assumptions could reinforce the original judgments. The proposed solution needs the same scrutiny the paper gives to Kaley's parental-involvement intervention: an inclusive intention does not establish a beneficial effect.

My judgment is shaped by valuing both educational equity and explanations that remain open to correction. That makes the signature requirement troubling and the paper's invitation to self-examination persuasive. It also makes me reluctant to infer a specific identity mechanism whenever a teacher's expectations prove limited. My preference for distinguishing explanations may understate the authors' concern that ordinary professional reasoning already carries institutional power. Nevertheless, their own admission that they did not push Kaley to question her convictions points toward a concrete conclusion: teacher educators should actively examine unsupported assumptions with candidates. The paper earns that obligation convincingly. It offers a suggestive account of those assumptions' social formation, while leaving their precise identity mechanisms and the best programmatic remedy unresolved.
