## B1. Premise acceptability — 2

The evaluation weighs the evidence supporting the central conclusions rather than accepting the authors' interpretation as evidence in itself. It examines the provenance of the records as course assignments receiving feedback from the first author (p. 226), purposive selection of two contrasting cases from six (p. 225), and the limited account of analytic coding (p. 227). It correctly distinguishes what two cases can illuminate from what they cannot establish about prevalence or alternative explanations. Its treatment of the parental-signature requirement and attitude survey is grounded in the actual incidents on pp. 227–228. Its scrutiny of Gayle's quoted reflections against the authors' identity interpretation is likewise grounded in pp. 229–231. Some methodological references are by section or description rather than explicit page number, but the passages are identifiable.

## B2. Relevance of critique — 1

Most criticisms address the claims actually made. The article repeatedly attributes knowledge construction to raced, classed, and gendered identities (pp. 223, 227, 231), making the evaluation's concern about the specificity of that interpretation relevant. The authors also make sufficiently strong prospective claims about culturally responsive preparation on p. 232 to warrant questions about effectiveness, even though they introduce the proposals as suggestions and acknowledge complexity.

The meritocracy criticism partially targets a broader position. The evaluation argues that unequal schooling does not mean instruction cannot improve a particular child's prospects and says the authors should separate these propositions. But the article defines its target as success for all children (p. 223) and mobility working for everyone (p. 230). It explicitly preserves Gayle's belief in education while describing her questioning of environmental constraints (p. 231), and calls her pedagogical approach sound (p. 228). There is a legitimate question about whether her quotations establish belief in universal mobility; there is much less basis for implying that the article confuses all confidence in teaching with that belief. This is a local overreach, not the evaluation's main criticism.

## B3. Sufficiency — 2

The evaluation consistently distinguishes evidence that permits an interpretation from evidence that establishes it. It argues that the attitude survey establishes a mistaken expectation more directly than a particular ideology (p. 228), and that Gayle's recognition of insecurity supports a broader understanding of learning without independently establishing reflection on Whiteness (pp. 229–230). It also distinguishes recommendations responsive to the cases from demonstrated program effectiveness (pp. 232–233). It identifies what is missing: information about family practices, more differentiated identity analysis, competing interpretations and contextual explanations, and investigation of the proposed curriculum and visits. It does not insist that a two-case interpretive study should estimate population effects.

## B4. Unstated warrants and missing qualifiers — 2

The evaluation identifies consequential inferential bridges: absence of adults reading aloud is not absence of educational support; recognizing trauma is not automatically recognition of racial identity; divergent responses by two white middle-class teachers do not isolate which aspects of identity explain the difference; and an inclusive curricular intention does not establish its effects. These matter directly to the movement from the incidents on pp. 227–230 to the identity conclusions on pp. 230–231 and recommendations on pp. 232–233. The evaluation supplies narrower conclusions and explains why the stronger conclusions need qualification. Its meritocracy discussion is less secure for the reasons given under B2, but that does not negate its other substantive findings.

## B5. Self-attribution — 2

The evaluation plainly identifies its judgments as its own, beginning with its distinction between persuasive and less secure arguments. Its closing paragraph states its commitments to equity and revisable explanation and acknowledges that its preference for differentiated explanations may understate the authors' concern with institutional power. Alternative explanations and possible risks of home visits are presented as the evaluator's reasoning, not attributed to the authors. The interpretive overstatement concerning meritocracy is a correctness/relevance issue; it does not make the evaluator's voice indistinguishable from the authors'.

**Errors: 1 interpretive misdescription; no additional factual errors identified.** In its meritocracy paragraph, the evaluation says the analysis would be sharper if it separated confidence that teaching can help from confidence that schooling neutralizes inequality. This understates a distinction already present on pp. 230–231: the authors retain Gayle's faith in education while recognizing her questioning of environmental constraints. This overlaps problem 14 and the B2 deduction; it is not a fabricated number or quotation. The surrounding warning is partly conditional, so it should not be inflated into a claim that the evaluation falsely reports an explicit denial of education's benefits. Its other conditional cautions are not counted as factual errors. Names, ages, school differences, instructional roles, reported incidents, and the page references it supplies agree with the article. The 2000 publication date is supported by the issue information despite the later online-publication date on the PDF cover.

## Researcher-facing assessment

The evaluation offers substantial, article-grounded scrutiny of evidence, interpretive warrants, and recommendations, with unusually explicit acknowledgment of its own framing. Its strongest contribution is distinguishing documented changes in expectations from the more specific identity interpretation placed on them. Its main weakness is the meritocracy criticism, which undercredits qualifications already in the article. Readers should also preserve the conditional status of the family-adequacy and home-visit cautions and recognize that the authors already acknowledge life-stage differences. These are localized limits in an otherwise well-supported evaluation, not grounds for treating all of its criticisms as established defects.
