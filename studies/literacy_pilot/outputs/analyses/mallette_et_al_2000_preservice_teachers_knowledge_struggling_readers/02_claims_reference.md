## Abstract claims

- **A1 — Purpose and scope (p. 222, abstract):** The analysis explores how preservice teachers’ developing knowledge about struggling readers was located in their white, middle-class identities.
- **A2 — Approach (p. 222, abstract):** Previously collected data on two preservice teachers are reanalyzed within a critical paradigm to explore the extent to which they accepted, resisted, and negotiated hegemonic ideologies.
- **A3 — Finding (p. 222, abstract):** The two preservice teachers mediated knowledge about struggling readers differently through their own cultured identities.
- **A4 — Recommendation (p. 222, abstract):** Teacher educators should probe more deeply into prospective teachers’ formative discourse as one way of dealing with the subjective nature of preservice teachers’ knowledge.

## Section and subsection inventory, in article order

### Unheaded introduction (pp. 222–223)

- **S1 — Findings of the prior interpretive inquiry (p. 222):** In the original semester-long study of six preservice teachers in a reading-difficulties methods course, participants began with personal stances about reading difficulties that appeared across their data sources. Their interactions with the struggling readers they studied served to confirm, question, or refute those stances. These are findings of the earlier inquiry that motivate the present reanalysis.
- **S2 — Why reanalysis was needed (pp. 222–223):** Although the earlier findings encouraged reconsideration of reading-methods teaching, the authors found them shallow. Interpretivism masked the fact that the participants were constructing subjective literacy knowledge rooted in their identities as raced, classed, and gendered individuals, rather than merely making meaning from experience.
- **S3 — Teacher-education rationale (p. 223):** Preparing teachers to work with diverse students requires attention to the subjective nature of literacy knowledge. Drawing on cited scholarship, the authors say white preservice teachers often dissociate themselves from racism because they do not feel personally prejudiced, and often ignore Whiteness as a construct embedded in an ideology that maintains white power and control.
- **S4 — Introductory conclusion/purpose (p. 223, immediately before “A White Middle-Class Ideology of Reading”):** The paper critically analyzes two representative but contrasting cases to explore how literacy-knowledge development was located in white, middle-class identities. An additional theoretical lens offers another layer of interpretation beyond that afforded by a single lens.

### “A White Middle-Class Ideology of Reading” (pp. 223–224)

- **S5 — Operative conception of ideology (p. 223):** Ideology is more than a set of beliefs: it is embedded in hegemonic relations through which dominant-group norms become reified over time and promote false consciousness. It also encompasses people’s acceptance, negotiation, and resistance of those norms. This conception grounds the analysis.
- **S6 — Meritocracy as ideology (p. 223):** The belief that all children can succeed through hard work and perseverance is an ideology rather than a neutral description of schooling. Schools lack the neutrality and equal playing field needed to enact meritocracy, yet this false consciousness drives education and is central to the study.
- **S7 — Reading and class (pp. 223–224):** Reading instruction remains a commodity of the white middle class, and the belief that every child has a right to literacy is also an ideology. School reading instruction closely reflects the dominant group. The authors leave open the direction of influence between school reading practices and middle-class home literacy, while emphasizing cited research showing greater reading-instruction success among children from middle-class homes than among children from lower socioeconomic backgrounds.
- **S8 — Section conclusion/research question (p. 224):** The analysis asks in what ways the two participants’ constructed meanings of reading difficulties reflected acceptance, negotiation, or resistance of these hegemonic ideologies. The question concerns the enactment of the ideologies just described.

### “Method” — “Design” (pp. 224–225)

- **S9 — Methodological status and boundary (p. 224):** This is a critical analysis informed by the critical paradigm, but it does not constitute critical research: critical research is action-oriented and requires reciprocity between researchers and researched, whereas this analysis revisits data after the fact. The participants’ original teacher research focused on understanding reading difficulties, not race, class, or gender; the authors adopted those identity categories when rereading revealed relevant patterns.
- **S10 — Theoretical basis (p. 224):** Frankfurt School neo-Marxist theory informs the analysis. Its immanent critique seeks to expose social relations concealed by appearances and contradictions between conceptual ideals and historically situated reality.
- **S11 — Application of immanent critique (p. 224):** Where the earlier study described how participants constructed meanings of reading difficulties, this analysis examines underlying relations by asking how their thoughts and ideas were situated in their cultured identities.
- **S12 — Subject–object relationship and design conclusion (p. 225):** The adopted theory treats society as both subject (people create it) and object (it has underlying structures); the two are related but independent, and reducing one to the other produces a false identity. The present analysis considers how historically crystallized political, social, and cultural structures shape reality and how participants enact and give meaning to it. Thus, their meanings of reading difficulties exist in a dialectical relationship between their own constructions and their sociohistorical structure. This extends the earlier inquiry’s focus on the subject.

### “Method” — “The Two Cases” (pp. 225–226)

- **S13 — Selection and comparative scope (p. 225):** Kaley and Gayle were purposively selected from the original six cases because they contrasted in how race, class, and gender positioned their knowledge of struggling readers. The authors describe them as opposite ends of a continuum, with the other four cases between them.
- **S14 — Shared setting (p. 225):** Both were preservice elementary teachers in a diagnosis-and-correction-of-reading-difficulties course at an urban university in the Southwest and belonged to a cohort together for two consecutive semesters. Cohort placements involved one semester at each of two culturally dissimilar schools serving diverse students designated at risk by the district: Heather, a mathematics-and-technology magnet, and Medford, an Accelerated Schools Project school using multi-age classrooms.
- **S15 — Kaley’s starting position (p. 225):** Kaley was white, middle class, and in her early twenties. She appeared among the cohort members most resistant to teacher-education expectations; uncertainty about assignments often led her to question coursework’s value.
- **S16 — Gayle’s starting position/subsection ending (pp. 225–226):** Gayle was white, middle class, and in her late forties. She entered education after raising children, was committed to the cohort, and willingly helped peers. She felt her preschool-teaching and parenting experience made her comfortable around children, though not necessarily confident. Together these profiles establish the different starting positions for the two cases; this descriptive subsection offers no separate causal conclusion.

### “Data Sources” (p. 226)

- **S17 — Source base (p. 226, opening):** The critical analysis uses four kinds of written assignments completed by the participants during the semester: field notes, a case study, reflective writing, and a group lesson plan. The following subsections specify how these records were generated; they do not report separate outcome findings.

#### “Field Notes” (p. 226)

- **S18 — Assignment and subsection endpoint:** Participants were required to spend 30 minutes each week taking field notes on their students. Preparation included a Bogdan and Biklen chapter and in-class video-based practice. During the semester the first author, also the course instructor, gave feedback and asked for assertions about observations; participants also had opportunities to share notes with peers.

#### “Case Study” (p. 226)

- **S19 — Assignment and subsection endpoint:** At the end of the course participants were given two directions: write a narrative of what they had learned about one student’s reading difficulties, and support every assertion with data. Permitted evidence included field notes, administered assessments, student work, and other information obtained through direct work with the student.

#### “Reflective Writing” (p. 226)

- **S20 — Assignment and subsection endpoint:** At the semester’s start participants wrote about how children learn to read, what reading difficulties are, and why students have them. Midsemester they reconsidered what reading difficulties were using their experience with their student. They also wrote course goals on the first day and, on the last day, reflected on whether and how their semester experiences had fulfilled those goals.

#### “Group Lesson Plan” (pp. 226–227)

- **S21 — Assignment and subsection endpoint:** The final assignment shifted attention from a single child to whole-class instruction. Participants grouped by their students’ grade level planned a lesson to meet needs while building on strengths, then taught it to a small group including the case-study child in their practicum classroom.

### “Data Analysis” (p. 227)

- **S22 — Analytic procedure and section conclusion:** The authors noticed acceptance, resistance, and negotiation of ideologies embedded in participants’ assertions in the original results. They then undertook immanent critique using an a priori/analytic coding system to explore what was versus what seemed to be. Each data source was examined for indications that constructions of reading difficulties were situated in what the authors identified as a white, middle-class ideology of reading and reading difficulties.

### “Results and Discussion” (pp. 227–230)

- **S23 — Overall result and reporting scope (p. 227, opening):** Critical analysis enabled the authors to position the participants as raced, classed, and gendered individuals and understand how that positioning formed a basis for their construction of knowledge about reading difficulties. Findings are reported and discussed through the two cases; all participant and student names are pseudonyms. The Gayle subsection ends the section, whose comparative conclusions are developed further under “Summary and Conclusions.”

#### “Kaley” (pp. 227–228)

- **K1 — Case context (p. 227):** In a third-grade classroom at Heather, Kaley and her cooperating teacher selected Moesha, an eight-year-old African American girl living near the school.
- **K2 — Parental responsibility interpreted through identity (p. 227):** Kaley’s expectations for Moesha appeared rooted in her white, middle-class ideology. To the authors’ knowledge she had not encountered research treating parental responsibility for learning to read as culturally specific. She saw parental support as the key to success, believed children learned reading early from parents, and attributed difficulties to insufficient early parental involvement; the authors interpret the particular support she prescribed as grounded in her identity.
- **K3 — Family arrangements and literacy expectations (p. 227):** The authors also see Kaley imposing white, middle-class thinking about normal living arrangements. Kaley initially found Moesha’s household arrangement awkward, then valued the availability of four adults who could help with schoolwork. She was saddened when Moesha said no family member read to her.
- **K4 — Persistence and attempted enforcement of parental involvement (p. 228):** Kaley maintained her conviction throughout the study, identifying parental support and extension of classroom activities as needs. She insisted on a parent-signature component in the group lesson plan, with no grade unless the slip was signed, hoping to force involvement. The authors report that the requirement had no effect for Moesha.
- **K5 — Reading attitude evidence and interpretation (p. 228):** Kaley assumed school offered the support lacking at home and consequently expected Moesha to prefer school reading to recreational reading. The Elementary Reading Attitude Survey showed the opposite; Kaley reported school-reading responses at the lowest two scores, 1 or 2. The authors interpret her puzzlement as reflecting the ideology of school as the great equalizer and an expectation that Moesha would share her valuation of school.
- **K6 — Subsection conclusion, including the researchers’ role (p. 228):** Like many preservice teachers, Kaley believed students would be much like herself regardless of appearance. She never questioned her convictions about parental involvement or schools as extensions of her own culture during the semester, and the researchers did not push her to do so. The authors contrast this with Gayle’s beginning to question her own and her student’s raced, classed, and gendered identities.

#### “Gayle” (pp. 228–230)

- **G1 — Case context (p. 228):** Gayle worked in a team-taught, multi-age grades 1–2 classroom at Medford. Her cooperating teachers selected Scott, a seven-year-old African American second-grade boy having trouble keeping pace with the class and thought likely to benefit from extra attention.
- **G2 — Initial instructional orientation (pp. 228–229):** Gayle initially approached Scott within white, middle-class thinking, focusing on academics rather than his raced, classed, and gendered identity. The authors describe her pedagogy as theoretically sound: multiple assessments identified strengths and needs, and she provided individual instruction. She sincerely wanted to help him as a struggling reader.
- **G3 — Diagnosis-and-correction belief (p. 229):** Gayle initially located reading difficulties primarily in instruction, including poor teaching, missing key concepts, absence, and uninteresting materials. Drawing on her own childhood difficulties, which improved after vision correction, she believed diagnosis and good instruction could correct Scott’s problem. She continually assessed his progress to guide instruction.
- **G4 — From judging work to recognizing frustration (p. 229):** Gayle first described Scott as a normal second-grade boy and wondered why he failed to participate or finish work despite available time; she criticized his coloring and cutting. As she knew him better, she understood noncompletion as frustration. Assessments indicated preprimer reading, incomplete alphabet knowledge, and recognition of only three words. She became concerned about the prevalence and lifelong consequences of unresolved reading difficulties.
- **G5 — Limits of the initial development (p. 229):** Reading difficulties had become real for Gayle, but her understanding still centered on her own white, middle-class identity: she assumed reading’s importance in her life extended to Scott’s and still believed correct diagnosis and good instruction could overcome his difficulties.
- **G6 — Turning point (pp. 229–230):** Near semester’s end Scott told Gayle that his brother had been shot in a gang-related drive-by shooting. He feared further violence, worried about his mother and sister, and felt responsible for their safety. He reported being unable to stop thinking about the shooting at school. The authors say this incident forced Gayle to consider his identity and environmental influences, and to reexamine reading difficulties and teaching’s complexity.
- **G7 — Developing recognition of difference (p. 230):** Gayle began to see Scott as belonging to a culture very different from her own and questioned education more broadly. She recognized the need to prepare for such incidents and concluded that assessment and materials were insufficient: children need environmental security before they can engage in the “luxury of learning.”
- **G8 — Qualified subsection conclusion (p. 230):** Gayle remained optimistic and continued accepting the meritocratic belief that educational gains produce occupational gains for everyone. Nevertheless, she was developing subjective knowledge through experiences involving equity and justice. In her case-study conclusion she reported some reading improvement but less than hoped, acknowledged that she could not make everything right in Scott’s world, and hoped her caring might encourage his continued literacy learning. Her change is partial negotiation/resistance, not a complete rejection of meritocracy or a claim to have resolved his difficulties.

### “Summary and Conclusions” (pp. 230–231)

- **Q1 — Knowledge and researchers’ subjectivity (p. 230):** The analysis illustrates the proposition that knowledge reflects its creators’ values and interests for researchers as well as participants. The authors constructed knowledge about participants’ subjectivities by situating their thoughts in research and theory on white, middle-class ideology. They did not create those thoughts, but supplied an interpretive lens. Both participants’ knowledge of struggling readers reflected their identities.
- **Q2 — Shared assumption and Kaley’s persistence (pp. 230–231):** Both entered teacher education expecting students to be similar to themselves and to share their values. Kaley found no reason to question this, blamed reading difficulties on insufficient parental involvement, and did not recognize her definition of involvement as ideological. Her belief was reinforced by its alignment with the dominant ideology and its racial social history.
- **Q3 — What Kaley’s parental-support account excluded (p. 231):** Kaley’s conception was seemingly myopic: she did not consider ways Moesha’s parents might already support school, treated reading aloud and homework as parental support, and imposed her values rather than collaborating with the parents. Her storybook-centered literacy definition left no room for multiple literacies. Storybook reading is one literacy, more frequent in middle-class homes, that undoubtedly helps children from that socioeconomic group succeed at school.
- **Q4 — Gayle’s resistance and negotiation (p. 231):** Gayle eventually resisted and negotiated hegemonic ideology by recognizing that Scott’s experiences differed from her own. This produced conceptual and emotional disequilibrium conducive to reflection and questioning. She grappled with her belief that good teaching was the answer, considered experiences beyond her identity, and began questioning how environment affects meritocracy while retaining faith in education. Calling learning a luxury suggested emerging recognition that such access is typically afforded white, middle-class students but not necessarily all students.
- **Q5 — Overall theoretical conclusion (p. 231, final paragraph):** Both participants constructed meaning through experience, but their literacy knowledge was situated in ideology: what they learned was embedded in what they believed they already knew from life experience, itself positioned in raced, classed, and gendered identities. This makes visible both subject and object and their relationship. The participants’ different life stages and initial thinking about teaching seemed reflected in their different negotiations of ideology; the authors present this connection tentatively.

### “Implications” (pp. 231–233)

- **I1 — Teacher educators’ implication in inequality (p. 231):** Unquestioning endorsement of Kaley’s white, middle-class account of parental involvement would mean failing to resist teacher educators’ own role in imposing those values on children of all cultures. The authors argue that this reproduces school inequalities rather than challenging them and may constitute an unseen form of racism, possibly the worst kind. This is a conditional implication and qualified possibility, not a report that the study established all teacher educators to be racist.
- **I2 — Knowledge, ethnic identity, and deeper belief examination (pp. 231–232):** Teacher educators need to help preservice teachers understand how educational knowledge is rooted in ideologies reflecting raced, classed, and gendered identities. There is no easy way to bring them to consider dominant ideologies, but the authors suggest beginning with opportunities to explore their own ethnic identities. Beliefs need examination at a deeper level for the ideologies they reflect.
- **I3 — Culturally responsive teacher education across the curriculum (p. 232):** A culturally responsive curriculum is one way to address knowledge’s subjectivity. Programs should exceed minimum multicultural-accreditation requirements and develop critical, transformative teachers with a pedagogy of social action and advocacy celebrating diversity. The authors say this can be accomplished by integrating a culturally responsive perspective throughout pedagogy, rather than confining it to a separate course whose knowledge may be compartmentalized or discarded.
- **I4 — Values, modeling, and educator self-examination (p. 232):** Adopting cited recommendations, the authors call for appreciation of diversity, cooperation, and caring community throughout culturally responsive curricula. Teacher educators must model these beliefs and examine their own thinking for parochialism. Although complex and lengthy, this approach will empower preservice teachers to examine raced, classed, and gendered identities and confront the politics of social change.
- **I5 — Revised case-study focus (p. 232):** Case studies of the kind described can become a means for exploring both teachers’ and students’ raced, classed, and gendered identities. Struggling-reader cases are particularly appropriate, the authors argue, because minority and low-socioeconomic-status students are overrepresented in that population. Such cases must address not just why children struggle, but who they are and how schools enhance or inhibit their growth. This is a proposed use of case studies, not an intervention tested in this retrospective analysis.
- **I6 — Home and community visits (p. 232):** The proposed case study should include visits to homes and communities, giving preservice teachers opportunities to learn about diverse student backgrounds and a forum for reflecting on their own backgrounds and ethnicity.
- **I7 — Multiple literacies and questioning values (p. 233):** Perhaps most importantly, preservice teachers should consider literacy in home and school and experience the existence of multiple literacies. Home and community visits can help them recognize differing valuations of literacy and question their own values, opening more opportunities to examine hegemony and ideology.
- **I8 — Final conclusion of the section and article (p. 233, immediately before “References”):** Teachers need to acknowledge, rather than deny, how students’ race, class, and gender shape what they bring to classrooms. The authors extend this principle to teacher educators and conclude that teacher educators need to analyze all forms of discourse to resist the tendency to replicate existing patterns.
