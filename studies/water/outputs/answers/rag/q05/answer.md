# Desalination as freshwater substitution

The collection supports desalination as a technically developed way to supply data centers without directly drawing on existing freshwater sources, but it does not establish that desalination is generally preferable to reclaimed water or lower-water cooling. Its strongest contribution is to show why that judgment depends on the saline feed, freshwater recovery, concentrate management, cooling demand, and regional water stress. The most detailed desalination study offers a framework for comparing these factors, rather than a demonstrated ranking across all three approaches [E7, E9, E24].

The underlying problem is both water quantity and quality. Folger et al. (2026) report that most data centers obtain water from municipal systems, while Li et al. (2025) explain that cooling towers need continuing replenishment for evaporation and discharge, with clean freshwater needed to control mineral accumulation, clogs, and bacterial growth [E20, E1]. Katakam et al. (2027) identify a freshwater requirement below 500 ppm total dissolved solids, among other criteria, and argue that treating saline water to the required standard could alleviate water constraints on data center development [E5]. Desalination therefore changes the source supplying freshwater demand; it does not, by itself, eliminate that demand [E5, E9].

Katakam et al. (2027) give this substitution a substantial engineering basis. They model and optimize reverse osmosis, mechanical vapor compression, and hybrids for brackish water, seawater, and hypersaline produced water, incorporating pretreatment, post-treatment, and concentrate management costs. Their scenarios cover conventional disposal, minimal liquid discharge (MLD), and zero liquid discharge (ZLD), and quantify recovery, energy consumption, thermodynamic efficiency, and water cost [E7]. This establishes a broader analytical basis than an isolated proposal to use server waste heat: Kanbur et al. (2020) argue that data center waste heat can supply desalination energy, but the retrieved passages do not provide performance results that would establish the practical advantage of that integration [E17, E22].

Recovery is central because the volume of saline water withdrawn depends on both the cooling system's water demand and the fraction of feed converted into freshwater. Katakam et al. (2027) explicitly treat saline water as the resource extracted from the environment [E9]. Their case comparison indicates that conventional seawater desalination can require greater saline withdrawals than a higher-recovery produced-water configuration for the same freshwater output [E19]. They also report that adding thermal desalination to increase seawater recovery could reduce their Saline Water Utilization Intensity (SWUI) by about 40% [E15]. These results support evaluating complete treatment and disposal configurations, rather than treating all desalination as equivalent [E7, E19].

However, SWUI requires careful interpretation. Katakam et al. (2027) explicitly describe it as a first-order measure of utilization under regional water stress, not a measure of depletion or intrinsic sustainability of the saline source. Its scarcity component reflects the possibility that desalinated freshwater could instead serve municipal, agricultural, or industrial users [E24]. Thus, reducing direct dependence on existing freshwater supplies does not establish the absence of regional allocation trade-offs [E2, E24]. Produced-water reuse also retains a connection to the water-intensive hydraulic fracturing process that generates its feed, even while offering waste-management and resource-recovery benefits [E2].

# Comparison with reuse and reduced cooling demand

Reclaimed water provides another route away from municipal potable supplies. Folger et al. (2026) describe operating practices that include on-site wastewater treatment and connections to nearby wastewater treatment facilities [E14]. Their definition of reuse includes capturing and treating wastewater, stormwater, saltwater, or graywater for a specified purpose, so reuse and desalination are not entirely separate categories; produced-water desalination also illustrates this overlap [E10, E2]. Nevertheless, the supplied material does not compare the costs, energy requirements, treatment performance, or freshwater displacement of reclaimed municipal water against the desalination configurations modeled by Katakam et al. (2027) [E7, E14].

The collection instead identifies constraints on reuse: Barnett-Itzhaki (2026) notes regional variation in reclaimed-water availability and competition from agriculture, while Folger et al. (2026) report unclear permitting pathways for recycled-water cooling [E11, E14]. These constraints preclude assuming that reclaimed water is universally available, but they do not demonstrate desalination's superiority where both options are feasible [E11, E14].

Lower-water cooling addresses demand more directly. Folger et al. (2026) state that air-cooled chillers use no water but generally require more energy than evaporative systems [E25]. Barnett-Itzhaki (2026) adds that dry cooling performs less well in hot, dry climates, limiting its applicability precisely where scarcity can be acute [E11]. Additional electricity demand matters to a broader water comparison: James et al. (2026) distinguish electricity-generation systems with large withdrawals, evaporative consumption, or little operational water use [E4]. The retrieved material does not quantify whether the energy penalty of lower-water cooling outweighs the energy and water burdens of desalination at any particular site [E25, E7, E4].

Finally, freshwater withdrawals, consumption, and regional scarcity are different outcomes. Barnett-Itzhaki (2026) distinguishes withdrawal from water not returned to its originating watershed, and explains why scarcity weighting matters [E18]. A defensible comparison would therefore need consistent accounting of these outcomes, treatment and electricity requirements, and local alternative uses. The collection establishes mechanisms and constraints relevant to that comparison, but leaves unresolved which combination of desalination, reclamation, and reduced cooling demand delivers the greatest overall benefit at a given location [E7, E11, E14, E18, E24, E25].
