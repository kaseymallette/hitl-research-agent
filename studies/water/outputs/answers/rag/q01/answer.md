The collection explains freshwater use primarily through the requirements of evaporative heat rejection and the need to protect cooling equipment. Drinking-quality water is a readily usable supply that generally meets those requirements, rather than a universally necessary cooling medium. Folger et al. (2026) explicitly state that cooling technologies require water meeting their particular quality parameters, that public-system water generally suffices, and that direct surface-water, groundwater, or reclaimed supplies may need pretreatment. The distinction is therefore between a source’s origin and its suitability after treatment: the retrieved evidence supports alternatives where they can deliver the required water quality, but does not establish that untreated alternatives are interchangeable with drinking water. [E7]

### Why freshwater and drinking-quality water are used

Evaporative cooling creates a continuing need for replacement water. Li et al. (2025) describe cooling towers in which some water evaporates to release heat while the remainder recirculates to absorb more server heat. Evaporation and the discharge of recirculated water both require makeup supplies. Their account gives a typical reuse range of three to ten cycles, dependent on water quality, before discharge becomes necessary to prevent mineral and salt buildup. Clean freshwater, often potable, is used to avoid pipe blockages and bacterial growth. [E3] Folger et al. (2026) explain the underlying quality constraint: evaporation increases dissolved-solids concentrations until the water reaches a threshold beyond which it cannot continue to be used for cooling. Initial quality consequently affects how much reuse is possible and the total volume required. [E4]

Drinking-quality supplies help manage more than dissolved solids. Folger et al. (2026) connect the suitability of regulated public water to requirements concerning disinfection and corrosivity control in distribution systems. Such water may be less likely to cause corrosion, mineral scaling, or microbial growth that impairs cooling performance. [E7] This explains its practical attractiveness without establishing that cooling equipment needs every attribute required for human consumption. The retrieved material identifies equipment compatibility as the operative requirement and provides no universal rule that cooling water must be potable. [E7]

Potability also does not eliminate the need to manage water during operation. Folger et al. (2026) describe the concentration of source-water constituents during evaporation, potentially including chlorine from treated public supplies. Operators may discharge concentrated water as blowdown or add fresh water to dilute it and permit further circulation. [E15] Thus, the relevant quality is both the incoming water’s condition and its condition after repeated concentration. [E4, E15]

The articles are less consistent about the relationship between concentration cycles and makeup demand. Alissa et al. (2025) state that higher cycles of concentration require more fresh makeup water and increase overall use. [E5] Li et al. instead emphasize a water-quality-dependent limit on recirculation, while Folger et al. explain how sufficiently low initial dissolved solids permit repeated use before discharge. [E3, E4] These passages do not supply a common quantitative relationship that reconciles their formulations. They establish the importance of dissolved-solids management, but do not support a single numerical rule for how changing the cycle count changes freshwater demand. [E3, E4, E5]

### When other supplies could meet the requirements

Reclaimed water and directly withdrawn surface water or groundwater are conditional substitutes. Folger et al. (2026) identify pretreatment as a means of improving these supplies and limiting damage to cooling technologies. [E7] Herrera et al. (2025) recommend grey-water and non-potable cooling supplies, especially where municipal water is stressed, but the retrieved recommendation provides no treatment specifications or measured performance demonstrating equivalence. [E12] Read together, these sources support substitution where treatment delivers suitable quality; they do not establish that non-potable water can be introduced into an existing system without adjustment. [E7, E12]

Katakam et al. (2027) extend this argument to saline sources through desalination. They state that freshwater for cooling and power generation needs total dissolved solids below 500 ppm, alongside other criteria, and propose desalination to meet data-center standards. [E2] This provides a concrete treatment target within their account, but the unspecified additional criteria prevent treating that number as a complete specification. Nor does the passage establish that the same limit governs every cooling configuration in the collection. [E2, E7]

Other supplies can also serve a different role within the cooling architecture. Lei et al. (2025) describe waterside economizers using cool water from lakes, rivers, or the sea to reduce mechanical refrigeration, with supplementary chilling when necessary. [E20] This shows that seawater can provide a cooling resource without the passage establishing its suitability as untreated cooling-tower makeup. The same configuration may still consume substantial water in evaporative towers. [E20]

### Configuration limits the need for substitution

Water-source requirements cannot be inferred from chip-cooling labels alone. Alissa et al. (2025) model air-cooled, cold-plate, and immersion systems that all use evaporative air-handling units for server-room cooling. [E1] Folger et al. (2026) likewise explain that closed-loop systems separate fluids but can still use evaporative heat rejection. [E16] Consequently, a closed loop or immersion system does not by itself establish the absence of freshwater makeup demand. [E1, E16]

Avoiding evaporation changes the question from finding replacement water to reducing direct water demand. Folger et al. (2026) report that air-cooled chillers use no water but require comparatively more energy. [E11] Alston et al. (2026) show that the resulting indirect water consumption depends on the generators supplying that electricity. [E22] The collection therefore supports a conditional conclusion: alternatives can replace drinking-quality supplies when their treatment and cooling role satisfy equipment requirements, while reducing dependence on water altogether requires evaluating the full cooling configuration and its electricity supply. It does not establish a universally suitable alternative source or a complete treatment specification. [E7, E2, E20, E22]
