# Agreement and the independence of its evidence

The retrieved material supports a distinction between convergence in conclusions and independence of evidence. Several articles converge on the need for more geographically and temporally specific water accounting, but they support that position through different kinds of material: reporting-system descriptions, policy arguments, and a regional simulation. Conversely, repeated results within a single modeling exercise do not constitute independent confirmation. The excerpts reveal some data provenance and external references, but do not establish a collection-wide network of shared estimates. Agreement therefore needs to be assessed claim by claim, rather than counted by the number of articles expressing a similar concern. [E2, E4, E8, E10, E11, E16, E24]

## Convergence supported by different lines of evidence

The strongest convergence concerns the inadequacy of existing disclosure. Barnett-Itzhaki (2026) argues that commercially sensitive facility information is often withheld and that missing or aggregated information about locations, cooling configurations, and electricity sources prevents reliable footprint assessment. Folger et al. (2026), writing for the Congressional Research Service (CRS), describe concrete reporting mechanisms that make individual facilities difficult to assess: utility data may not be public, customer uses are aggregated, and self-supplied facilities can face fewer reporting requirements. These accounts reinforce one another at the level of the diagnosis, while supplying different explanations for the information deficit. Their independence is only partly demonstrable, however: the Barnett-Itzhaki passage cites Jain (2025), while the supplied CRS footnotes identify Luukkonen et al. and Medalie et al.; the excerpts do not trace these sources further. [E8, E10, E13]

Alston et al. (2026) provide a distinct, model-based reason why greater detail matters. Their Michigan simulation holds facility cooling configurations and load profiles constant while changing locations, thereby keeping total direct consumption and electricity load unchanged across the two siting cases. Yet the maximum subbasin depletion increase falls from 6.74% to 2.78% under dispersed siting. This supports the proposition that equal aggregate consumption can have different local consequences. It gives analytical support to Barnett-Itzhaki’s call for geographically granular disclosure, although a simulation of consequences does not independently establish how often operators withhold data. [E2, E6, E11, E15]

Agreement about accounting for electricity-related water use has a similar structure. Barnett-Itzhaki recommends including embedded water in electricity supply; James et al. (2026), in the Ceres report, call for a standardized methodology and database for purchased-electricity water use; and Alston et al. explain how facility location can alter indirect consumption through generation and transmission constraints. These contributions converge on an accounting requirement, but the Ceres recommendation is not itself an independent measurement of the footprint’s magnitude. Alston et al.’s recommendation for subannual, subbasin reporting likewise supplies a modeling rationale for finer accounting, rather than proof that the collection independently reproduces a particular footprint estimate. [E2, E11, E12, E16]

## Shared cases, inputs, and methodological limits

The Michigan findings illustrate how apparent corroboration can arise from repeated presentation of one case. Alston et al. explicitly introduce Michigan as a demonstration of their framework. The concentrated-siting result, relocated-siting result, and reporting recommendations are connected outputs and interpretations of that exercise. They should not be treated as separate empirical replications, nor do the supplied excerpts demonstrate that other articles reuse Michigan as an illustrative case. [E6, E15, E16, E22]

Alissa et al. (2025) offer clearer provenance for a different modeling exercise: Microsoft supplied primary data, environmental product declarations supplied secondary data where possible, and LCA FE and ecoinvent 3.6 supplied background data when those sources were unavailable. Their conclusion that advanced liquid cooling reduces environmental impacts thus rests on an identifiable combination of company information and background datasets. The excerpts do not establish that other studies use those same datasets, so this is evidence of this study’s dependencies, not demonstrated cross-article dependence. [E4, E7]

Their uncertainty claims also require careful weighting. Alissa et al. describe overall uncertainty as low because the dominant use phase has high-quality data. However, their assessment uses a pedigree matrix, and they explain that unavailable dataset standard deviations prevented a complete quantitative uncertainty assessment. Their confidence is therefore supported by a representativeness assessment rather than demonstrated statistical replication across independent datasets. [E1, E7, E14]

Herrera et al. (2025) disclose a different limitation: probabilistic inputs are treated as statistically independent despite possible technological and regional interrelationships. Agreement between their scenario results and another model would consequently need scrutiny of both input provenance and assumptions. Their references include Li et al. (2025) and Siddik et al. (2021), but bibliography entries alone do not show which estimates were adopted, how they entered the model, or whether another article reused them. [E9, E20, E24]

## What the collection does not establish

The excerpts expose possible foundations for dependence without establishing its extent. CRS describes a database of state-reported withdrawals and distinguishes it from USGS assessments that supplement reported values with modeled estimates. These are different data products, but the material does not show which other articles use either product or whether their inputs overlap. Barnett-Itzhaki’s statement that no data were used also cautions against counting that article as a new empirical dataset. [E5, E19]

The defensible conclusion is stronger for methodological convergence than for independent numerical confirmation. Different reporting accounts and a regional model support the need for finer water accounting. The material is insufficient to identify a widely recycled numerical estimate, demonstrate repeated use of a common illustrative case across articles, or establish independently replicated footprint totals. Those limits matter: agreement on what should be measured is better supported here than agreement on a separately verified magnitude. [E2, E6, E10, E12, E19, E24]
