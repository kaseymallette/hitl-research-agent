The collection explains freshwater use principally through the operating requirements of particular cooling systems, and drinking-quality water use through the suitability of regulated municipal supplies for meeting those requirements. It does not establish that potability itself is a universal technical requirement. Li et al. (2025) describe clean freshwater, often potable, as necessary makeup water in the cooling towers they discuss; Folger et al. (2026) explain that municipal water generally meets cooling-quality requirements, while other sources may need pretreatment. Together, these accounts support a distinction between the quality needed to protect equipment and the source through which that quality is obtained. [E6, E3, E10]

### Why water quality matters

In the cooling towers described by Li et al. (2025), some water evaporates to dissipate heat, while the remainder circulates through an open loop and absorbs further heat at a heat exchanger. Evaporation and discharge both require replacement water. The authors report that the remaining water typically undergoes only 3–10 cycles before discharge, depending on its quality, because continued reuse would promote mineral and salt accumulation, clogs, and bacterial growth. Freshwater demand therefore reflects both consumptive evaporation and the need to discharge circulating water before its condition becomes detrimental to operation. [E6, E17]

This mechanism makes supply quality consequential for how much reuse is possible. Li et al. (2025) explicitly condition the number of cycles on water quality and report that, with good-quality water, approximately 80% of withdrawal is consumed through evaporation. This is an operating example rather than a universal ratio: their account also makes evaporation dependent on weather and operating settings. [E6, E17] An evaluation of Folger et al. (2026) reinforces the connection between quantity and quality, assessing the report's explanation of dissolved-solid concentration as showing why reduced intake and the character of eventual discharges must be considered together. That is the evaluator's interpretation of the report, rather than an additional measured finding. [E5]

Drinking-quality municipal water offers a supply whose treatment addresses several of these equipment concerns. Folger et al. (2026) state that regulated public-system water generally satisfies cooling requirements and connect its suitability to disinfection and corrosivity controls. They contrast this with water that causes corrosion, mineral scaling, or microbial growth, and explain that directly sourced surface water, groundwater, and reclaimed water may require pretreatment. [E3, E10] Thus, the evidence explains potable-water use through an overlap between public-supply treatment and cooling-system needs. It does not demonstrate that all drinking-water criteria are necessary for cooling, or that all nonpotable water is unsuitable. [E3, E10]

### Conditions for alternative supplies

Reclaimed water can substitute where it meets the relevant cooling-quality requirements, with pretreatment where necessary. Folger et al. (2026) report that some facilities already recycle water on site or obtain reclaimed water from nearby treatment plants in response to freshwater concerns. Their account also identifies an institutional constraint: EPA reports operator interest in recycled cooling water alongside an unclear permitting pathway. Substitution therefore depends on both water quality and an implementable supply arrangement. [E10, E16] Recycling also does not, by itself, eliminate the evaporative losses or quality-limited recirculation described by Li et al. (2025). [E6]

Saline resources offer another route, but the retrieved proposal involves treatment rather than unrestricted use of saline water in the same equipment. Katakam et al. (2027) specify less than 500 ppm total dissolved solids, among other criteria, for the cooling and power-generation uses they discuss. They describe brackish supplies as generally containing 1,000–10,000 ppm and propose desalination to deliver water of the requisite quality. [E20] This would change the source of the freshwater supplied to cooling systems; it would not remove the stated quality requirement. Their system account includes energy inputs, treatment, delivery, and separate concentrate disposal or recovery, making these parts of the proposed substitution. [E20] The retrieved material does not provide the other quality criteria or establish that the 500 ppm threshold applies to every cooling configuration. [E20]

Natural water bodies can also serve a different function: receiving heat through heat exchangers rather than supplying potable-quality tower makeup water. Barnett-Itzhaki (2026) discusses lakes, rivers, and seawater as thermal sinks and identifies Google's Hamina seawater installation and Alibaba's Qiandao Lake facility as examples. [E15, E18] Such arrangements require suitable geography, water depth, and flow, while ecological impacts and thermal-discharge regulation can constrain operation. The article specifically associates heat-wave curtailment risk with once-through or open-loop systems; that qualification should not be extended indiscriminately to every heat-exchanger arrangement. [E18]

Finally, the need for continuous replacement water depends on cooling design. Folger et al. (2026) distinguish air, liquid, and hybrid systems, while Herrera et al. (2025) argue that closed loops replacing evaporation can substantially reduce water demand, subject to costs, integration complexity, and hardware compatibility. [E9, E11] These designs change the requirement rather than simply substitute another water source. Assessing their overall benefit also requires a wider boundary: an evaluation of Folger et al. notes that reduced on-site use can shift demand toward electricity-generation water use. [E2]

The collection therefore supports conditional substitution, not a universal alternative-water prescription. Supplies must satisfy the particular equipment's quality requirements, and treatment, permitting, discharge management, or site conditions may determine feasibility. It establishes several available pathways, but supplies neither a complete specification for cooling-water quality nor a comparative demonstration that any one alternative can replace municipal drinking water across facilities. [E3, E10, E16, E18, E20]
