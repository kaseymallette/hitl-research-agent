The collection supports desalination as a technically specified way to substitute saline resources for existing freshwater supplies, but it does not establish that desalination is generally preferable to reclaimed water or lower-water cooling. Its strongest evidence concerns treatment design and production costs; its weaker evidence concerns regional water-stress relief and comparative environmental performance. The supplied assessment of Katakam et al. (2027) makes this distinction explicitly: their engineering analysis establishes desalination as an option worth evaluating, while their siting analysis does not establish when planners should choose it. [E8, E10, E13]

### What desalination establishes

Desalination addresses the source of cooling water rather than necessarily reducing cooling demand. Barnett-Itzhaki (2026) distinguishes source substitution from changes in heat rejection: non-potable supplies retain water demand while changing its source, whereas eliminating site water use changes the cooling arrangement and its electricity requirements. [E4] This distinction matters because, as Li et al. (2025) explain, cooling towers require replacement water for both evaporation and discharge; repeated circulation is limited by water quality and the accumulation of dissolved material. Providing desalinated water could therefore replace an existing freshwater input without eliminating the evaporative consumption associated with that cooling process. [E6]

Katakam et al. (2027) give this substitution a concrete engineering basis. They identify water-quality requirements, including a stated threshold below 500 ppm total dissolved solids, and consider saline resources that require treatment before use. [E21] Their system-level evaluation includes pretreatment, product post-treatment, and concentrate management, with inflation-adjusted costs, and considers reverse osmosis, mechanical vapor compression, and hybrid arrangements. This is a broader production-cost framework than treating the separation equipment alone as the desalination system. [E8, E9]

The supplied evaluation identifies the salt balance as the study’s strongest starting point: for a fixed quantity of freshwater product, higher recovery reduces feed intake and residual brine volume while increasing brine concentration. It also shows why maximizing recovery is not equivalent to minimizing cost or energy. In one modeled seawater hybrid reaching a concentrate concentration of 130,000 ppm, mechanical vapor compression contributes 7.8 of the total 9.4 kWh and $2.60 of the total $3.20 per cubic metre of combined product, although reverse osmosis supplies most of the water. These are results for a particular modeled configuration, not general prices for desalinated cooling water. [E10] They establish that additional recovery has consequential energy and equipment costs, while concentrate management remains a separate problem from recovering usable water. [E10, E15]

Waste-heat integration offers another design rationale, but the retrieved evidence does not establish its overall environmental advantage. Kanbur et al. (2020) motivate desalination integration by analogy with other low-temperature heat-recovery applications. [E17] The supplied evaluation questions their exclusion of chemical exergy from a separation process and explains that their sustainability index does not independently demonstrate environmental benefits. Thus, those modeled thermodynamic indicators cannot settle the comparison with alternative water supplies or cooling arrangements. [E16]

### What the alternatives leave unresolved

Reclaimed water offers another source-substitution route. Folger et al. (2026) report that some data centers recycle water onsite or obtain reclaimed water from nearby treatment plants, while operators encounter uncertainty over permitting. [E2] However, reclaimed supply is not shown to be freely available without consequences for other users. The supplied assessment of their report emphasizes treatment requirements, competing demands, infrastructure costs, and the need to establish what would otherwise happen to that water. Reducing potable demand could divert reclaimed water from another beneficial use; administrative initiatives and partnerships alone do not demonstrate additional savings. [E11] The retrieved material therefore supports neither assuming reuse is always preferable nor dismissing it in favor of desalination. Its comparative value depends on availability, treatment and delivery requirements, and displaced uses. [E11, E13]

Lower-water cooling changes demand more directly, but the collection does not supply a common performance comparison. Herrera et al. (2025) describe substantial water reductions from liquid cooling, especially closed loops replacing evaporation, while identifying cost, integration, and hardware-compatibility barriers; immersion can require substantial retrofitting. [E14] Alston et al. (2026) identify a water–energy tradeoff across cooling configurations. [E19] The assessment of Folger et al. (2026) further explains that air-cooled systems generally require more electricity and that facility water-use effectiveness omits electricity-generation water. Lower onsite consumption can consequently shift demand elsewhere, with its significance depending on conditions at both supplies. [E7]

That wider boundary also matters for evaluating desalination’s electricity requirements. Lei et al. (2025) include onsite water, electricity-generation water, and upstream fuel-related water within a holistic assessment. [E20] Applying that scope to the modeled desalination costs would be necessary to determine whether freshwater substitution also reduces the wider water burden; the supplied desalination evaluations do not resolve that comparison. [E10, E13, E20]

Finally, reduced intake is not itself a validated measure of reduced regional stress. The assessment of Katakam et al. (2027) treats their siting results as conditional comparisons driven by assumed cooling demand, feed, recovery, and stress weights; robustness to selected weights does not validate the borrowed impact categories. [E18] Likewise, the assessment of James et al. (2026) finds that the collection supports source-specific assessment and separate reporting of withdrawal and consumption, rather than a universal technology ranking. [E3] Desalination is therefore established as a credible supply option, but choosing it over reuse or cooling redesign still requires comparable computing service, local supply counterfactuals, electricity-related water impacts, and treatment and residual-management costs—elements identified across the material but not brought together into a decisive comparison.
