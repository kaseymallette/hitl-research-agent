# Agreement and the independence of evidence

The retrieved material supports a distinction between agreement about how water impacts should be studied and independently corroborated estimates of those impacts. It shows convergence on data limitations, contextual variation, and the need for broader assessment, but provides little basis for tracing numerical agreement across articles to independent measurements. Several items are inventory instructions or section labels rather than substantive findings; others explicitly repeat an article’s conclusions. Counting retrieved items or agreeing publications would therefore overstate the evidence available here. [E1, E2, E4, E14, E18, E22, E25, E30]

## Convergence with different evidentiary foundations

The strongest convergence concerns the inadequacy of available information, although the articles approach that problem differently. James et al. (2026), in the Ceres report, identify limitations in the electricity-sector data used for their analysis: EIA water figures are self-reported and unverified, while national-average WRI factors cannot represent individual plants’ cooling systems, age, climate, or efficiency. Missing reports require substantial estimation, with the balance between reported and estimated inputs differing across states. These are specific limitations of an operational dataset and its supplementation. [E8]

Herrera et al. (2025) describe a different obstacle: commercial sensitivities and cybersecurity concerns restrict companies’ disclosure of detailed water-use statistics. Their response is a forecasting framework combining expert judgment and scenario assumptions without requiring comprehensive historical data. [E24, E27] The supplied evaluation of Folger et al. (2026), meanwhile, credits the CRS report with explaining how aggregated public-supply totals, reporting differences for self-suppliers, and statistical categories that do not isolate data centers obstruct policy-relevant measurement. This is the evaluator’s assessment of the report’s institutional explanation, rather than an additional empirical study. [E10]

These accounts offer complementary reasons to take the information problem seriously: limitations arise in power-plant reporting, corporate disclosure, and public statistical classification. Their distinct explanations strengthen the methodological diagnosis, but the retrieved passages do not establish that the underlying evidence was assembled independently or that the articles corroborate a particular water-use total. [E8, E10, E27]

Agreement also appears around the importance of context. Herrera et al. (2025) call for forecasts sensitive to hydrology, infrastructure, regulation, and vulnerability. Alston et al. (2026) recommend subannual, subbasin assessments and distinguish consumption volumes from risk, which also depends on existing infrastructure, competing uses, and hydrologic conditions. [E26, E12] James et al. (2026) supply a related methodological caution: their analysis assumes that water sources share a power plant’s location and represents a single year despite seasonal and interannual variation. [E8] This is convergence between a forecasting recommendation, an integrated-assessment prescription, and acknowledged limitations of a snapshot analysis. It supports attention to spatial and temporal resolution; the passages do not supply a common measured effect against which independent results can be compared. [E26, E12, E8]

## Repetition, shared inputs, and illustrative evidence

Some apparent agreement is demonstrably repetition within the same source. The Ceres close reading and claims reference both report the unverified EIA data, average WRI factors, and directional status of the estimates. These are two representations of James et al. (2026), not two confirmations. [E8, E25] Likewise, the claims reference and close reading of Herrera et al. (2025) repeat the same defense of scenario forecasting under disclosure constraints. [E24, E27] The inventories for Li et al. (2025), Folger et al. (2026), and James et al. (2026) explicitly warn that recurring claims should not be treated as independent findings. [E2, E4, E30]

Shared estimation is clearest within the Ceres analysis: national-average factors fill gaps in plant reporting, so its state estimates are not uniformly grounded in observations. California and Virginia require more estimation than Arizona, Texas, and Illinois. Agreement among outputs generated through that framework cannot itself validate the common factors. [E8] The retrieved material does not, however, establish which other articles reuse these EIA inputs or WRI factors. An article-to-article dependency map would exceed the evidence supplied. [E8, E25]

Illustrative comparisons require similar restraint. The close reading of Li et al. (2025) distinguishes measurements of comparator models from direct observations of GPT-3: the comparisons support an argument that a worked inference estimate may be conservative, rather than providing additional GPT-3 measurements. [E29] Alston et al. (2026) explicitly qualify their analysis as illustrative and not an assessment of a specific project. [E28] Folger et al. (2026) use water bottling as a tentative analogy for inventory gaps, not as evidence that the industries have equivalent impacts. [E23] These examples can motivate hypotheses or methods without independently establishing the target quantities.

## What validation and openness establish

Kanbur et al. (2020) provide a particularly useful boundary: agreement between a submodel and prior results, together with earlier cooling validation, does not amount to validation of the complete integrated system, whose absence they acknowledge. [E3] Alissa et al. (2025) report manageable data variability and low overall uncertainty because high-quality use-phase data dominate their assessed impacts; that is their internal data-quality conclusion, not cross-article replication. [E5]

Openness makes independence assessable but does not demonstrate it. Alissa et al. (2025) provide datasets and tools through Zenodo, and Herrera et al. (2025) identify an open data-and-code repository; the retrieved close reading explicitly says the latter repository was not independently checked. [E15, E9] Barnett-Itzhaki (2026), by contrast, declares that no data were used and is described as synthesizing reported figures, so its agreement cannot count as a fresh observational dataset. [E6] Overall, the collection most clearly supports convergent methodological concerns. Independent numerical corroboration, and the extent of shared estimates across publications, remain largely unestablished by these retrieved passages. [E8, E12, E26, E27, E29]
