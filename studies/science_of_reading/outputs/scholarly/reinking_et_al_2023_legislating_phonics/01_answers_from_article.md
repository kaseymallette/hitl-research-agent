# Reinking, Hruby, and Risko (2023): the three questions, from the article

Reinking, D., Hruby, G. G., & Risko, V. J. (2023). Legislating phonics: Settled science or political polemics? *Teachers College Record, 125*(1), 104–131. https://doi.org/10.1177/01614681231155688

Answered from the article alone. This commentary critiques what the authors identify as a phonics-first ideology and its use of research to promote legislation, taking Tennessee’s *Early Reading Training for Teachers* as a representative case. It contrasts that course with a preceding, collaboratively developed Tennessee initiative and argues for phonics within comprehensive, differentiated literacy instruction.

## 1. How is the science of reading defined?

**Short answer.** The authors explicitly reject a single, settled Science of Reading, proposing instead multiple, evolving sciences that inform understanding of reading (p. 122). They distinguish research about reading from claims about how to teach every individual student, and defend scientific inquiry as provisional, questioning, and attentive to context (pp. 122–124). Their target is the settled-science claim they attribute to phonics-first advocates and the Tennessee course, rather than phonics instruction itself (pp. 106–107, 121–124).

- **pp. 106–107.** In the authors’ characterization, a phonics-first ideology treats phonics as both the foremost component of reading and something to master before other aspects receive attention. They list belief in an unassailably supportive settled science among that ideology’s assertions. In their own position, phonics is indispensable, but research supports embedding it in comprehensive instruction, using multiple approaches, and responding to individual needs; certainty about teaching every child is never likely to be warranted.
  *Interpretation:* The disputed definition couples a claim of scientific finality with a particular instructional hierarchy. The authors separate commitment to phonics from commitment to that hierarchy.

- **pp. 117–119; p. 126, notes 10–11.** The authors regard the National Reading Panel (NRP) report as authoritative and as providing solid, though not unequivocal, support for phonics to enhance word identification. They report that its analysis included 38 experimental studies, multiple approaches with none proving superior, moderate overall effects, and numerous qualifications and unresolved questions. They accuse the course of omitting these details while promoting synthetic phonics alone; note 10 lists the several approaches the NRP acknowledged.
  *Interpretation:* Research authority, in the authors’ account, does not establish either a unique method or a final prescription for all learners.

- **p. 122.** The authors identify cognitive, social, and developmental psychology; cognitive neuroscience; cultural anthropology; and psycho- and social linguistics as fields whose researchers study reading. They acknowledge sophisticated methods, replicable findings, and theories, but state: “there is no one uppercase Science of Reading” and “there are multiple lowercase sciences of reading” (p. 122). Continuing research, new tools, and developing theories are their evidence against finality.
  *Interpretation:* Their positive definition is interdisciplinary and ongoing, encompassing social and cultural inquiry as well as cognitive and neural inquiry.

- **p. 122.** The authors ask whether there is a science of teaching reading and call that less clear, and perhaps an inappropriate question. They argue that generalizations about effective practice can break down in particular cases or contexts. Their analogy with physicians emphasizes knowledge of current evidence together with professional judgment, clinical experience, and detailed knowledge of an individual case—even allowing rejection of a generalization in specific circumstances.
  *Interpretation:* Science informs teaching without fully determining the appropriate action in every classroom encounter.

- **pp. 122–123.** The authors characterize the course’s use of brain research as potentially giving its claims scientific respectability that many teachers and reading researchers lack the expertise to assess. They report disagreements among brain researchers and reluctance to apply findings to instruction, and accuse the course of suppressing diverse interpretations. They also challenge the course’s claim that the Institute of Education Science no longer funds research on systematic instruction because the evidence is settled, asking for evidence of such a policy.
  *Interpretation:* Neuroscience is accepted as a contributing field, but its invocation does not itself validate instructional certainty.

- **pp. 123–124.** In the authors’ account, scientists continually test theories, seek anomalies, and investigate how, why, and under what circumstances approaches are appropriate. They reject absolute classifications of instruction as working or not working in every case. They characterize the course’s use of science as a rhetorical demand for uncritical deference that silences debate, and insist that teaching and learning occur within complex social systems involving cultural norms, values, equity, and justice.
  *Interpretation:* The authors define scientific practice partly by its openness to revision and contextual explanation, contrasting it with the political use of scientific authority they attribute to the course.

## 2. How is teachers’ knowledge positioned?

**Short answer.** The authors position teachers’ broad knowledge of reading, knowledge of individual students, instructional repertoires, and firsthand professional experience as essential resources for effective instruction (p. 116). They argue that restrictive legislation and the Tennessee course diminish professional judgment and flexibility, while endorsing collaborative professional learning and research-informed discretion (pp. 105–109, 112, 122). They also recognize limits to teachers’ expertise, especially in evaluating brain research (pp. 122–123).

- **pp. 105–107.** The authors oppose legislative arbitration of professional disagreements and argue that mandates sanction prescribed practice while implicitly sanctioning deviations, even where alternatives have research support. They say this usurps the flexibility and judgment required to meet diverse needs. Writing as teacher educators, they describe teachers’ professionalism as implicitly questioned and diminished by political decisions grounded in polemical arguments.
  *Interpretation:* Teachers are positioned as professionals entitled to exercise informed judgment, whose authority is being displaced by political prescription.

- **pp. 108–109, 111.** The authors say restrictive legislation mutes dissent from qualified professionals informed by research and extensive experience. They characterize the Tennessee course as aimed more at converting teachers to a narrow ideological view than at supplying constructive support for integrating phonics into a multidimensional curriculum and accommodating individual differences. They describe the course as less a resource than a polemic.
  *Interpretation:* This is the authors’ characterization of the course: teachers are treated as recipients of an orthodoxy rather than as professionals supported in making instructional decisions.

- **p. 112.** In their favorable account of the preceding Read to Be Ready initiative, the authors describe state staff meeting regularly with school literacy coaches to design a framework and professional development materials jointly. Staff also consulted literacy professors and teacher-education researchers, including one of the authors, to develop tools for identifying students’ needs and differentiating instruction. The authors contrast this collaboration with legislative mandates and describe teachers and students as vulnerable to shifts in political leadership.
  *Interpretation:* Useful professional knowledge is presented as something developed through collaboration among practitioners, state staff, and researchers.

- **p. 116.** The authors argue that the most important instructional factors are arguably teachers who know all aspects of reading development, know their students’ strengths and needs, have support to respond, possess a large repertoire of approaches, and supplement this knowledge with lessons from firsthand experience. They also cite research suggesting that experience, dedication, materials, leadership, context, and factors outside school may matter more than instructional approach alone.
  *Interpretation:* Professional knowledge combines research, experience, and knowledge of particular learners; phonics knowledge is necessary within a broader repertoire.

- **p. 119.** The authors reproduce NRP cautions about scripted programs that largely eliminate teacher judgment, the need to understand teachers’ roles and encourage their unique contributions, the uneven suitability of a program across students, and the need for guidance on flexible grouping and pacing. They endorse these cautions as qualifications the course fails to address.
  *Interpretation:* Even the authority invoked by the course is presented as recognizing unresolved questions about teacher judgment and the need for instructional adaptation.

- **pp. 120–122.** The authors report that a survey found more experienced teachers more likely to favor balanced instruction, and cite research on exemplary teachers as evidence that successful practitioners use a balanced approach. Their physician analogy then argues for professional judgment capable of modifying general research-based recommendations for a particular case.
  *Interpretation:* Experienced practice is treated as relevant evidence and as a source of judgment in applying research, rather than merely a barrier to adopting it.

- **pp. 122–123.** The authors state that most teachers—and most researchers addressing reading instruction—are not qualified to evaluate brain-research findings, theories, and interpretations. They warn that such research can therefore supply a veneer of scientific respectability to instructional claims.
  *Interpretation:* Their defense of professional expertise is bounded: they do not attribute expertise in every contributing science to classroom teachers.

## 3. How are students positioned?

**Short answer.** The authors position students as individuals with differing strengths, needs, circumstances, and literacy development, requiring comprehensive instruction and flexible professional responses (pp. 107, 116, 120–122). They oppose the uniform learner implied by the phonics-first ideology as they characterize it, and place achievement within social, linguistic, economic, and institutional contexts (pp. 106, 115–116, 124). Students’ interests and motivation matter, but the commentary largely speaks about students through adults and research rather than presenting students’ own accounts (pp. 107, 118–121, 124–125).

- **pp. 106–107.** In the authors’ characterization of phonics-first ideology, all children move through the same sequence with little need for differentiation, nearly all become successful readers after mastering phonics, and virtually all reading difficulties are attributed to and remedied by phonics. The authors dispute this and describe lockstep commercial programs as numbing and often frustrating, drawing here on their own experiences as parents and grandparents.
  *Interpretation:* They contest a uniform model of learners and attend to children’s experience of prescribed instruction. The emotional effects reported here are the authors’ observations, not student testimony.

- **pp. 107, 111–112, 116.** The authors argue for fine-tuning instruction to individual needs and for teachers knowing students’ strengths as well as difficulties. They warn that elevating phonics can subordinate oral language, vocabulary, fluency, and motivation. Their favorable account of Read to Be Ready includes meaningful texts, background knowledge, comprehension strategies, and tools for identifying individual needs.
  *Interpretation:* Students are positioned as developing readers with multiple capacities and needs, whose instruction should respond to more than decoding performance.

- **pp. 113–116; p. 126, note 9.** The authors dispute the course’s national reading-crisis account and its attribution of that crisis to inadequate phonics. They question NAEP proficiency categories and argue that counting only students at or above proficient can misleadingly suggest that two thirds barely read. They nevertheless call the below-basic third a concern because disadvantaged students are disproportionately represented. Note 9 qualifies their description of score trends as flat by acknowledging dependence on graph scale and the possibility of multiple interpretations.
  *Interpretation:* Students’ assessment classifications are treated as politically consequential representations that require interpretation, rather than transparent descriptions of ability.

- **pp. 113–115, Figures 1–3.** My reading of the printed images: Figure 1 compares fourth-grade average scores across racial and ethnic groups and displays persistent group differences; Figure 2 places Tennessee’s at-or-above-proficient percentages near the national percentages; Figure 3 shows 66% at or above basic in 2019, compared with 62% in 1992. The authors use these figures to challenge the crisis claim while acknowledging disparities in the accompanying discussion (pp. 113–116).
  *Interpretation:* In this part of the argument, students also appear as aggregate populations whose statistical representation becomes evidence in a policy dispute.

- **pp. 116, 124.** The authors emphasize increasing cultural and linguistic diversity, more students whose first language is not English, and economic disparities as influences on achievement. They reject the likelihood that one instructional variable explains virtually all variation. Their conclusion explicitly rejects final instructional answers for every child, particularly children living in poverty and experiencing institutional inequity and injustice.
  *Interpretation:* Reading difficulties are situated within students’ social circumstances, challenging a solely instructional or phonics-based explanation.

- **pp. 118–119.** The authors report that about two thirds of the NRP comparisons involved students experiencing reading difficulties and question generalization to higher-achieving students. They also report differing findings by grade and achievement, raise questions about isolated-word assessments versus meaningful text, and endorse NRP cautions that a program suits some students better than others.
  *Interpretation:* Students’ differences limit universal prescriptions and require attention to which learners, outcomes, and circumstances research actually addresses.

- **pp. 120–121.** The authors describe balanced instruction as including comprehension, substantial reading practice, writing, background knowledge, and motivation, with flexibility for student needs. They endorse the NRP statement reproduced on p. 121 that calls for attention to children’s interest in books and understanding of information read aloud alongside phonics skills, and they identify reading comprehension as the overarching goal of phonics instruction (p. 120).
  *Interpretation:* Students are positioned as meaning makers and interested participants in literacy, with successful reading extending beyond accurate decoding.

- **pp. 124–125.** The authors argue that ideological legislation has real consequences against the interests of children, their families and communities, and their teachers. Students are the ultimate beneficiaries whose needs should guide decisions; this conclusion offers no direct student accounts or role for students in making those decisions.
  *Interpretation:* Students’ welfare is central to the authors’ argument, while their own voices and decision-making agency receive little explicit treatment.
