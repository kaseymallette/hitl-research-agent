# Reinking et al. (2023): Close Reading answers

Reinking, D., Hruby, G. G., & Risko, V. J. (2023). Legislating phonics: Settled science or political polemics? *Teachers College Record, 125*(1), 104–131. https://doi.org/10.1177/01614681231155688

Answered from the article and its close reading (`03_close_reading.json`). Every quotation gives the article’s printed page and the close reading claim it comes from (**CR 35.2** = step 35, claim 2). This scholarly commentary critiques the politicization of phonics through legislation, focusing on a Tennessee teacher training course and contrasting it with an earlier collaborative literacy initiative.

## 1. How is the science of reading defined, and how is it enacted?

### The authors define reading science as plural and continually open to revision

- **p. 122 · CR 31.4 · authors’ own view.** "Thus, there is no one uppercase Science of Reading. Instead, there are multiple lowercase sciences of reading, each contributing interesting and relevant findings to our understanding."
- **p. 123 · CR 35.2 · authors’ own view.** "They are continually testing the veracity and utility of current theories, findings, and interpretations."

### They characterize phonics-first advocacy as claiming settled scientific authority for phonics priority

- **p. 106 · CR 4.1 · authors’ characterization of the phonics-first position they dispute.** "Such legislation has been promoted by activists who are guided by what might be called a phonics-first ideology, with “first” denoting two related meanings fundamental to the ideology: phonics as being of foremost importance in learning to read, and phonics mastered before other aspects of reading are addressed."
- **p. 106 · CR 4.9 · authors’ characterization of the phonics-first position they dispute.** "A settled science unassailably supports these beliefs, assertions, and assumptions."

### They trace enactment through legislation, teacher training, prescribed methods, and selective research claims

- **p. 111 · CR 12.1 · authors’ account of the Tennessee course.** "The Tennessee DOE developed the Course in response to a provision in the Tennessee Literacy Success Act (2021) promoted by the current governor."
- **p. 117 · CR 21.3 · authors’ characterization of the course they dispute.** "Yet, the Course promotes only one approach to phonics: a “synthetic” approach (teaching individual letters and their sounds that are then blended into words) without acknowledging that other approaches may be equally effective."
- **p. 108 · CR 8.3 · authors’ account of legislative mandates.** "Yet, many practitioners are required to use Orton-Gillingham instruction by legislative mandate, as a result of successful lobbying by the coalition dedicated to a phonics-first ideology."
- **p. 119 · CR 25.8 · authors’ criticism of the course’s use of the National Reading Panel.** "In short, as these points illustrate, the Course cites claims from the NRP report out of context and misrepresents its findings, and qualifications."

### They endorse phonics within comprehensive, differentiated literacy instruction

- **p. 104 · CR 1.2 · authors’ own view.** "However, we argue that phonics instruction is more effective when embedded in a more comprehensive program of literacy instruction that accommodates students’ individual needs and multiple approaches to teaching phonics—a view supported by substantial research."

## 2. How is teachers' knowledge positioned?

### Teachers need broad reading knowledge, knowledge of individual students, experience, and professional judgment

- **p. 116 · CR 19.2 · authors’ own view.** "Arguably, and consistent with that research, the most important factors instructionally are teachers who are well informed, not just about phonics but about all aspects of reading development; who know their students’ individual needs and strengths and are prepared and supported in their response to those differences; who have a large repertoire of instructional options; and who supplement that knowledge with lessons learned from firsthand professional experience (Bondie et al., 2019)."
- **p. 122 · CR 32.5 · authors’ own view of professional practice.** "In practice, there is much room for professional judgment and variation, even to the point of rejecting findings from generalizations in light of the specific circumstances of unique cases."

### The authors oppose political restrictions on teachers’ professionalism and describe collaborative professional support

- **p. 107 · CR 5.2 · authors’ criticism of political restrictions.** "As teacher educators and professionals who work closely with teachers, we are dismayed to see their options to teach phonics limited, their flexibility to differentiate instruction in response to their students’ individual needs undermined, and their professionalism implicitly questioned and diminished by political decisions based on polemical arguments."
- **p. 112 · CR 13.4 · authors’ account of the earlier Read to Be Ready initiative.** "Further, unlike the top-down implementation of the current legislation, the DOE staff hired a literacy coach for each elementary school in the state and met regularly with those coaches."

### Their defense of professional knowledge includes limits on teachers’ expertise in neuroscience

- **p. 122 · CR 33.1 · authors’ own view.** "Most teachers and researchers addressing issues of reading instruction are not qualified to assess the validity of findings, theories, and interpretations of brain research."

## 3. How are students positioned?

### The authors dispute a uniform phonics sequence and an explanation of reading difficulty centered on insufficient phonics

- **p. 106 · CR 4.4 · authors’ characterization of the phonics-first position they dispute.** "All children should move lockstep through a well-defined sequence of phonics skills. There is little, if any, need to differentiate instruction to meet the differing needs of individual students. Once children master the content of phonics, almost all of them will become successful readers."
- **p. 108 · CR 8.4 · authors’ criticism of phonics-focused legislation.** "The greater problem is that limiting such legislation to narrowly conceived beliefs and practices related to phonics excludes other important components of early reading instruction, wrongly suggests that students are failing because they are not taught enough phonics (thus the need for legislation), and implies that all difficulties in learning to read can be attributed to insufficient or inappropriate phonics instruction."

### Children need meaningful uses of reading and writing and assessment beyond phonics skills

- **p. 119 · CR 25.2 · National Reading Panel view quoted and endorsed by the authors.** "In implementing systematic phonics instruction, educators must keep the end in mind and ensure that children understand the purpose of learning letter-sounds and are able to apply their skills in their daily reading and writing activities."
- **p. 121 · CR 29.4 · National Reading Panel view quoted and endorsed by the authors.** "It is important to evaluate children’s reading competence in many ways, not only their phonics skills but also their interest in books and their ability to understand information that is read to them."

### The authors question achievement categories used to classify students and construct a reading crisis

- **p. 115 · CR 17.1 · authors’ account of NAEP proficiency categories, citing Bracey.** "The cut scores used to define these categories were set arbitrarily by a small group of hand-picked, politically appointed experts—reportedly against the recommendations of psychometricians (test experts) who had been contracted to advise the development of this approach but who were overruled when they argued against doing it at all (Bracey, 2008)."
- **p. 116 · CR 18.5 · authors’ own view.** "There is no indisputable operational or broad consensual understanding of what these levels mean beyond the arbitrary cut scores."

### Students’ reading must be considered within social conditions, poverty, and institutional injustice

- **p. 116 · CR 20.1 · authors’ own view.** "Social, cultural, and demographic factors that vary over time also undoubtedly influence education achievement in general, and reading achievement in particular."
- **p. 124 · CR 37.1 · authors’ own view.** "Neither is there justification for suggesting that there is a settled science of reading that provides final answers about how reading should be taught to every child, particularly among those who live in poverty and who experience institutional inequities and injustice."
