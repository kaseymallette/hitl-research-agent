# Reinking, Hruby, and Risko (2023): baseline answers

Reinking, D., Hruby, G. G., & Risko, V. J. (2023). Legislating phonics: Settled science or political polemics? *Teachers College Record, 125*(1), 104–131. https://doi.org/10.1177/01614681231155688

Answered from the article alone. This peer-reviewed commentary critiques a phonics-first ideology and its use of research to promote legislation, using Tennessee’s Early Reading Training for Teachers as a representative case and contrasting it with an earlier collaborative literacy initiative.

## 1. How is the science of reading defined, and how is it enacted?

### The authors describe multiple, continuing sciences of reading, with no settled prescription for teaching

- **p. 122 · Authors’ own view.** "Yes, there are scientists who have expertise in the areas of cognitive, social, or developmental psychology, cognitive neuroscience, cultural anthropology, and psycho- and social linguistics, and who study reading."
- **p. 122 · Authors’ own view.** "Thus, there is no one uppercase Science of Reading. Instead, there are multiple lowercase sciences of reading, each contributing interesting and relevant findings to our understanding."
- **p. 122 · Authors’ own view.** "Even research that provides generalizations about effective practice across many individuals can break down at the level of a particular case or context (Joyce & Cartwright, 2020)."
- **p. 123 · Authors’ own view.** "Scientists aren’t satisfied with determining what works or not. They want to understand how, why, and in what circumstances different approaches may or may not be a good fit."

### The phonics-first position claims scientific certainty for the primacy of phonics, which the authors dispute

- **p. 106 · Authors’ characterization of the phonics-first ideology they dispute.** "Such legislation has been promoted by activists who are guided by what might be called a phonics-first ideology, with “first” denoting two related meanings fundamental to the ideology: phonics as being of foremost importance in learning to read, and phonics mastered before other aspects of reading are addressed."
- **p. 110 · Authors’ account of advocates’ position, which they dispute.** "In recent years, those promoting a phonics-first ideology have taken the stance that their position is based on a settled science of reading, often drawing on esoteric neurological and psychological studies."
- **p. 117 · Authors’ critique of the Course’s use of the National Reading Panel report.** "For example, the report’s findings were derived collectively from studies that used several distinctly different approaches to teaching phonics, none of which proved superior to the others.¹⁰"
- **p. 123 · Authors’ critique of the Course’s use of brain research.** "To favor its narrow assertions consistent with a phonics-first ideology, the Course truncates the diversity of interpretations among brain researchers, suggesting that behaviors, cognitive processes, and neural activity are isomorphic."

### Claims of settled science are enacted through legislation, restricted methods, and incentivized teacher training

- **p. 111 · Authors’ account of the Course’s legislative origin.** "The Tennessee DOE developed the Course in response to a provision in the Tennessee Literacy Success Act (2021) promoted by the current governor."
- **p. 111 · Authors’ account of the Course’s presentation and incentives.** "Similarly, the Course’s three modules are all entitled “Sounds First, a Research-Based Approach to Foundational Literacy.”"
- **p. 111 · Authors’ account of the Course’s presentation and incentives.** "The Course is offered free online⁷ and was incentivized in the summer of 2021 with a $1,000 stipend for teachers who completed it (“TDOE Announces Stipends,” 2021)."
- **p. 117 · Authors’ critique of the Course’s instructional restriction.** "Yet, the Course promotes only one approach to phonics: a “synthetic” approach (teaching individual letters and their sounds that are then blended into words) without acknowledging that other approaches may be equally effective."
- **p. 124 · Authors’ own view.** "In short, the Course, similar to legislatively inspired efforts in other states, uses science as a rhetorical tool to promote a preferred orthodoxy or, as some have argued more forcefully, as a bludgeon to silence discussion or debate (Ayres, 2006)."

### The authors endorse research-informed phonics within comprehensive, flexible literacy instruction

- **p. 107 · Authors’ own view.** "We endorse, and have consistently endorsed, teaching phonics as an important aspect of learning to read, and we acknowledge research that offers support for doing so."
- **p. 107 · Authors’ own view.** "However, we are also convinced by research suggesting that phonics is more effective when embedded in a more comprehensive and balanced program of reading instruction; that multiple approaches to teaching phonics can be effective; and that there is a need to fine-tune all reading instruction to meet individual students’ needs (e.g., see Snow et al., 1998; Wixson et al., 2019)."
- **p. 121 · National Reading Panel’s view, quoted and endorsed by the authors.** "It is important to emphasize that systematic phonics instruction should be integrated with other reading instruction to create a balanced reading program. Phonics instruction is never a total reading program."

## 2. How is teachers’ knowledge positioned?

### The authors oppose policies that blame educators, constrain their judgment, and demand adherence

- **p. 106 · Phonics-first position, reported by the authors and disputed.** "There is a crisis of reading achievement in the United States. It is explained by a failure of mainstream educators to fully invest in the primacy of phonics."
- **p. 107 · Authors’ own view.** "As teacher educators and professionals who work closely with teachers, we are dismayed to see their options to teach phonics limited, their flexibility to differentiate instruction in response to their students’ individual needs undermined, and their professionalism implicitly questioned and diminished by political decisions based on polemical arguments."
- **p. 109 · Authors’ critique of legislatively inspired initiatives.** "Further, it illustrates how such legislation can inspire initiatives that are aimed more at proselytizing teachers to embrace a narrow, circumscribed, and indeed ideological view of phonics, instead of providing them with the sound, constructive support and resources necessary to help them integrate phonics into a comprehensive, multidimensional curriculum to develop reading ability."
- **p. 120 · The Course’s claim, quoted by the authors and disputed.** "Whole language and balanced literacy are two instructional approaches that remain popular, despite the fact that there is strong evidence they do NOT work"

### Teachers need broad research knowledge, knowledge of individual students, experience, and professional discretion

- **p. 116 · Authors’ own view, drawing on research they endorse.** "Arguably, and consistent with that research, the most important factors instructionally are teachers who are well informed, not just about phonics but about all aspects of reading development; who know their students’ individual needs and strengths and are prepared and supported in their response to those differences; who have a large repertoire of instructional options; and who supplement that knowledge with lessons learned from firsthand professional experience (Bondie et al., 2019)."
- **p. 122 · Authors’ own view.** "In practice, there is much room for professional judgment and variation, even to the point of rejecting findings from generalizations in light of the specific circumstances of unique cases."
- **p. 121 · Authors’ account of research they endorse.** "There is also evidence that the most experienced and successful teachers of reading use a balanced approach (see Pressley et al., 2002, who interviewed and followed teachers nominated as exemplary by their peers and supervisors)."

### Professional support can be developed collaboratively with educators and researchers

- **p. 112 · Authors’ account of the earlier Read to Be Ready initiative.** "Further, unlike the top-down implementation of the current legislation, the DOE staff hired a literacy coach for each elementary school in the state and met regularly with those coaches."
- **p. 112 · Authors’ account of the earlier Read to Be Ready initiative.** "The result was a jointly designed framework and professional development materials for early literacy instruction that accommodated multiple approaches to teaching phonics (e.g., supplementing decoding with making sense of meaningful texts) within a more comprehensive curriculum of foundational skills in the areas of language, vocabulary, background knowledge, and comprehension strategies."
- **p. 112 · Authors’ account of the earlier Read to Be Ready initiative.** "DOE staff also consulted with literacy professors and researchers in teacher education programs, including one of the authors of this commentary, to develop tools to identify individual students’ needs and differentiate instruction accordingly."

## 3. How are students positioned?

### The disputed phonics-first ideology treats children as progressing through the same sequence toward a single remedy

- **p. 106 · Phonics-first position, reported by the authors and disputed.** "All children should move lockstep through a well-defined sequence of phonics skills. There is little, if any, need to differentiate instruction to meet the differing needs of individual students. Once children master the content of phonics, almost all of them will become successful readers."
- **p. 106 · Phonics-first position, reported by the authors and disputed.** "Virtually all difficulties in learning to read are attributed to and remedied by phonics."
- **p. 108 · Authors’ own critique.** "The greater problem is that limiting such legislation to narrowly conceived beliefs and practices related to phonics excludes other important components of early reading instruction, wrongly suggests that students are failing because they are not taught enough phonics (thus the need for legislation), and implies that all difficulties in learning to read can be attributed to insufficient or inappropriate phonics instruction."

### The authors position children as different learners whose reading involves meaning, motivation, and more than decoding

- **p. 105 · Authors’ own view.** "Third, it usurps the professional flexibility and judgment that are necessary to meet the diverse needs of individual students; instead, one approach may be prescribed for all."
- **p. 111 · Authors’ own critique.** "As such, it risks not just overselling phonics, but also neglecting or subordinating equally important aspects of learning to read, including oral language development, vocabulary, fluency, and motivation, among others (e.g., see Almasi & Hart, 2019; Duke & Cartwright, 2021), therefore undermining optimally effective instruction that is more flexibly differentiated to meet individual students’ needs."
- **p. 121 · National Reading Panel’s view, quoted and endorsed by the authors.** "It is important to evaluate children’s reading competence in many ways, not only their phonics skills but also their interest in books and their ability to understand information that is read to them."

### The authors dispute using achievement categories to cast students as evidence of a phonics crisis

- **p. 113 · Authors’ account of the Course’s claim, which they dispute.** "The Course takes that tack, arguing that there is a national crisis in reading and then promoting phonics as the cause (there is not enough of it) and the solution (more of it is needed)."
- **p. 116 · Authors’ own view.** "The one third below basic is still a concern, because it includes a disproportionate number of disadvantaged students, although that discrepancy has been slowly narrowing."
- **p. 116 · Authors’ own view.** "There is no indisputable operational or broad consensual understanding of what these levels mean beyond the arbitrary cut scores."

### Students’ learning is shaped by social circumstances, inequality, and changing policies

- **p. 116 · Authors’ own view.** "Social, cultural, and demographic factors that vary over time also undoubtedly influence education achievement in general, and reading achievement in particular."
- **p. 124 · Authors’ own view.** "Neither is there justification for suggesting that there is a settled science of reading that provides final answers about how reading should be taught to every child, particularly among those who live in poverty and who experience institutional inequities and injustice."
- **p. 112 · Authors’ own view.** "Teachers and students, and the entire hierarchy of education leadership in a state, can be buffeted between two very different views of what constitutes foundational skills and, as in Tennessee, move from a program developed collaboratively to one that is legislatively mandated."
