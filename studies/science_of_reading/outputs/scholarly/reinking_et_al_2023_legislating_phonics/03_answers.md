# Reinking, Hruby, and Risko (2023): the three questions

Reinking, D., Hruby, G. G., & Risko, V. J. (2023). Legislating phonics: Settled science or political polemics? *Teachers College Record, 125*(1), 104–131. https://doi.org/10.1177/01614681231155688

Answered from the article and its close reading (`02_close_reading.json`). Every point gives the article's printed page and the close reading claim it comes from (**CR 35.2** = step 35, claim 2). References also identify relevant material that the close reading leaves out or records outside its claims. This is a commentary combining historical discussion, research synthesis, and a critique of Tennessee's legislatively prompted *Early Reading Training for Teachers*. It argues against a phonics-first ideology and restrictive instructional mandates while affirming phonics within comprehensive, differentiated reading instruction.

## 1. How is the science of reading defined?

**Short answer.** The authors reject a single, settled Science of Reading that determines how every child must be taught. They affirm multiple sciences studying reading, with sophisticated methods, replicable findings, and theories, and describe science as continuing, skeptical inquiry. They distinguish this research from decisions about teaching particular students: findings inform practice but do not eliminate professional judgment. The settled, predominantly phonics account is the position they attribute to advocates and the Tennessee course, and dispute.

- **p. 106 · CR 4.1–4.4.** In the authors' characterization, phonics-first means both foremost in importance and mastered before other aspects of reading. They attribute to its advocates a preference for synthetic phonics, a common sequence for all children, a phonics explanation of virtually all reading difficulties, and the assertion that settled science unassailably supports these positions.
  *Interpretation:* This describes the ideological position under criticism, rather than the authors' own definition of reading science.

- **pp. 107, 117 · CR 5.3–5.4, 21.1–21.2.** The authors endorse phonics as indispensable to learning to read. They say the National Reading Panel (NRP) supplies solid, if not unequivocal, support for phonics to improve word identification; its pooled findings encompass several phonics approaches, none superior to the others. They argue from research for phonics embedded in comprehensive, balanced instruction and adjusted to individual needs.
  *Interpretation:* Their objection to scientific settlement does not withdraw their endorsement of phonics or empirical research.

- **pp. 117–119, 126 · CR 21.3, 22.1–22.3, 23.2, 24.4, 38.2.** The authors describe the NRP's phonics analysis as 38 experimental studies producing 66 comparisons, with overall moderate effects, strongest in kindergarten and first grade. Approximately two thirds of comparisons involved students experiencing reading difficulties; 76% assessed regularly spelled words or pseudowords in isolation. They question generalization to all learners and to reading connected, meaningful texts, and charge the course with misrepresenting the report's findings and qualifications. Note 10 names synthetic, analytic, embedded, analogy, onset-rime, and phonics-through-spelling approaches.
  *Interpretation:* The scope of the samples, comparisons, outcomes, and approaches limits what the authors think this evidence warrants prescribing.

- **p. 119 · CR 24.1 (24.1 verbatim), 24.2.** The article endorsingly reproduces the NRP's warning: “Programs that focus too much on the teaching of letter-sounds relations and not enough on putting them to use are unlikely to be very effective.” It also quotes the panel's requirement that children understand phonics' purpose and apply it in daily reading and writing.

- **pp. 119–120 · CR 25.1–25.4.** The authors know of no comprehensive, authoritative analysis definitively resolving many NRP questions. They report Bowers's synthesis as finding an emphasis on phonics not clearly justified, while explicitly rejecting the inference that this supports whole language. They also report no statistically significant comprehension improvement in the heavily funded Reading First studies despite extensive teacher training and substantial phonics instruction.
  *Interpretation:* These are findings as reported and deployed by this commentary, not independent checks of the cited studies. The article treats them as mitigating claims of conclusive support for phonics dominance.

- **pp. 120–121 · CR 27.2–27.4, 28.1 (28.1 verbatim), 28.2.** The authors distinguish balanced literacy from whole language by, among other things, its explicit systematic phonics instruction. They say balance is not always precisely defined or formulaic but has research-grounded components, including language, vocabulary, comprehension, text reading, background knowledge, writing, motivation, and instructional flexibility. They endorse the NRP statement, quoted on p. 121: “Phonics instruction is never a total reading program.” The quoted report says phonics should dominate neither instructional time nor significance.

- **pp. 121–122 · CR 30.1–30.3.** The authors regard claims of settled science as polemical and political when used to restrict instruction essentially to phonics, especially one approach. Drawing on Grant, they define scientific thinking through continual rethinking and criticize the course's certainty for lacking skepticism, equivocation, collegial debate, and deeper exploration. They report that many scientists and philosophers would regard settled science as antithetical to authentic science and research-informed practice.

- **p. 122 · CR 31.1–31.2.** The authors affirm contributions from cognitive, social, and developmental psychology, cognitive neuroscience, cultural anthropology, and psycho- and social linguistics. These fields have sophisticated ways to collect and analyze data, replicable findings, and theories. They distinguish multiple lowercase sciences of reading from one uppercase Science of Reading, and point to continued method development, theory testing, and journal publication as incompatible with final settlement.

- **p. 122 · CR 31.3–31.4.** Whether there is a science of teaching reading is, for the authors, less clear and perhaps an inappropriate question. Research generalizations can break down in particular cases or contexts. Their physician analogy combines current scientific knowledge with clinical experience, professional judgment, and detailed understanding of the individual, allowing even rejection of a generalization when a unique case warrants it.
  *Interpretation:* Research informs an exercise of judgment; it cannot supply a universally sufficient instructional rule.

- **pp. 122–123 · CR 32.1–32.4.** The authors criticize neuroscience's use as borrowed authority: most teachers and reading-instruction researchers cannot assess its findings, theories, and interpretations, and brain researchers themselves lack unanimity and often hesitate to infer instructional applications. They say the course truncates this interpretive diversity. Their counterexample is Ellis and Solms's account of emotional and motivational brain functions as consistent with whole language's underlying rationale.
  *Interpretation:* This counterexample challenges a unique route from neuroscience to phonics-first teaching; it does not establish whole language as the authors' preferred approach.

- **p. 123 · CR 33.1–33.3.** The article reports the course's assertion that evidence for systematic phonics is so conclusive that IES no longer funds research on its effectiveness, then challenges the course's authors to produce evidence of such a policy. It argues that neither the NRP nor subsequent research establishes an indisputable scientific/nonscientific instructional boundary for every context and student. Scientists continually test explanations, seek anomalies, and reduce ignorance rather than attain final certainty.
  *Interpretation:* The article challenges the funding-policy assertion; it does not document an alternative IES policy.

- **pp. 123–124 · CR 34.1–34.5; CR 34.2 (34.2 verbatim).** The authors reject absolute works/does-not-work categories and instead ask how, why, and under what circumstances approaches fit. They write: “Multidimensional continua, not binaries, are the stuff of science, especially when science is applied to real-world decisions in the realm of instructional practice” (p. 123). They characterize the course's use of science as demanding uncritical deference and promoting orthodoxy that silences debate. They insist that teaching and learning occur within complex social systems involving cultural norms, values, beliefs, equity, and justice.

## 2. How is teachers' knowledge positioned?

**Short answer.** The authors position teachers' broad knowledge of reading, knowledge of individual students, instructional repertoire, and firsthand professional experience as arguably the most important instructional factors. Teachers need preparation, support, and discretion to apply research to particular cases. The authors characterize mandates and the Tennessee course as diminishing that professionalism and demanding acceptance of a narrow view. They also recognize limits to teachers' expertise, particularly in evaluating neuroscience.

- **pp. 105, 107 · CR 2.3, 3.2, 5.2.** The authors compare reading instruction with medicine and law, where certified professionals and professional standards govern practice. They argue that narrowly ideological legislation makes political power arbitrate professional differences, sanctions required practice and departures from it, and usurps the judgment and flexibility needed for individual students. They explicitly say teachers' professionalism is implicitly questioned and diminished.
  *Interpretation:* Teachers' knowledge carries legitimate authority that the authors believe legislation improperly displaces.

- **pp. 108–109 · CR 8.3, 9.3.** The authors say restrictive phonics mandates mute qualified professionals' dissent informed by research and extensive experience. They characterize Tennessee's course as proselytizing teachers into a narrow view rather than supplying sound support and resources for integrating phonics into comprehensive instruction or managing individual differences.
  *Interpretation:* In their account, the course treats teachers as recipients of a prescribed position rather than practitioners supported in informed judgment.

- **p. 110 · CR 11.1; specific proposals not recorded in the close reading.** Reporting Paterson's historical analysis, the authors describe proposed Tennessee bills that would negate teaching certificates issued by institutions lacking a full phonics course, and a proposed Washington bill permitting parents to bring legal proceedings against districts that did not teach phonics. These are historical proposals, not enactments established by this passage.
  *Interpretation:* The examples show how political requirements can seek to define legitimate teacher preparation and practice through sanctions. The close reading records punitive proposals generally but leaves out these particular mechanisms.

- **pp. 111–112 · CR 12.1–12.3, 13.1–13.4, 14.1–14.2.** The authors contrast the legislatively prompted, stipend-incentivized course with Read to Be Ready, developed without legislation under a commissioner who had been a teacher and education dean. They report collaboration with elementary-school literacy coaches to design a framework and professional development materials, and consultation with literacy professors and researchers, including one author, on identifying individual needs and differentiating instruction. The predecessor's report diagnosed substantial word-recognition teaching but insufficient connection to actual reading.
  *Interpretation:* The preferred alternative combines professional learning, practical diagnosis, and collaborative development. This passage supplies no comparative outcome evaluation of the two initiatives.

- **p. 112 · CR 14.4.** The authors state that political leadership changes can buffet teachers, students, and educational leadership between competing definitions of foundational skills and replace collaboratively developed programs with legislatively mandated ones.
  *Interpretation:* Teachers' working knowledge and practice are vulnerable to political reversals.

- **p. 116 · CR 19.2–19.3.** The authors argue that instructional approach may be less influential than teachers' experience and dedication, materials, leadership, context, and out-of-school factors. Their explicitly qualified claim is that arguably the most important instructional factors are teachers informed about all reading development, knowledgeable about students' individual strengths and needs, prepared and supported to respond, equipped with many instructional options, and drawing on firsthand professional experience.
  *Interpretation:* Expertise is both research informed and experiential, and requires institutional support to be usable.

- **p. 119 · CR 24.2; the specific tension concerning scripts is not recorded.** The article endorsingly quotes the NRP's concern that some programs with large effect sizes are scripted so that teacher judgment is largely eliminated. The quoted report asks how instructional consistency can coexist with teachers' unique contributions. CR 24.2 records only the broader point that the teacher's role needs better understanding.
  *Interpretation:* The issue is not simply whether teachers implement a program consistently, but whether its design permits their professional contribution.

- **pp. 120–121 · CR 26.2–26.4, 29.2.** The article disputes the course's claim that whole language and balanced literacy remain popular despite strong evidence they do not work. It reports survey evidence that more experienced teachers more strongly favored balance, while only small minorities endorsed whole language, and cites research following teachers nominated as exemplary as evidence that experienced, successful teachers use balanced instruction.
  *Interpretation:* The authors mobilize experienced practitioners' choices and documented practice against the course's categorical dismissal; popularity alone is not presented here as an experiment establishing effectiveness.

- **p. 122 · CR 31.3–31.4, 32.1.** The authors defend case-specific professional judgment through their medical analogy while stating that most teachers and reading-instruction researchers lack the qualifications to assess neuroscience's validity and interpretation. Brain research can consequently lend a veneer of scientific respectability to instructional assertions.
  *Interpretation:* Defending teachers' judgment does not make their expertise unlimited. The authors locate instructional expertise in applying evidence to learners and contexts, while warning about claims outside practitioners' disciplinary competence.

## 3. How are students positioned?

**Short answer.** The authors position students as individually different learners whose needs, strengths, motivations, and social circumstances require comprehensive, flexible instruction. They reject treating all children as following one phonics sequence or explaining all reading difficulty through deficient phonics. Students are also the people whose interests policy should serve and whose learning is affected by political changes and institutional inequities. The commentary does not directly gather students' accounts; its claims about them come through the authors' argument, reported research, policy discussion, and assessment data.

- **pp. 105–107 · CR 3.2, 4.2–4.4, 5.1–5.4.** The authors characterize phonics-first advocates as expecting all children to follow a common sequence with little differentiation and almost universal success after phonics mastery, postponing vocabulary, fluency, comprehension, and motivation. Against this, they argue that reading's complexity and individual differences make certainty about teaching every child never likely to be warranted. They describe personally observing numbing and often frustrating effects of commercial programs designed for mandates, and endorse fine-tuning all reading instruction to individual needs.
  *Interpretation:* The attributed uniform learner contrasts with the authors' differentiated account. Their account of program experience is a statement of personal standpoint, not a reported student-interview study.

- **p. 108 · CR 7.2–7.3, 8.2–8.3.** The authors say phonetic regularity in decodable texts can come at the expense of coherent meaning; they report some evidence that young children better comprehend natural, authentic texts even when less decodable. They also report no definitive evidence for Orton-Gillingham in the meta-analysis they cite concerning children with decoding difficulties. They dispute the implication that students fail because they receive insufficient or inappropriate phonics.
  *Interpretation:* Children are positioned as comprehenders of meaningful text, and reading difficulty does not by itself establish a particular phonics remedy.

- **pp. 111–112, 120–121 · CR 13.1, 14.1–14.2, 27.4.** The authors describe oral language, vocabulary, fluency, and motivation as equally important aspects that narrow phonics advocacy risks neglecting or subordinating. Their account of balanced instruction includes comprehension strategies and monitoring, background knowledge, extensive reading, writing, and motivation, with flexibility to address individual needs. The predecessor Tennessee initiative developed tools to identify those needs.
  *Interpretation:* Learners have several interacting dimensions of development, rather than being defined solely by code mastery.

- **pp. 114–116 · CR 16.2–16.3, 17.1–17.4, 18.1–18.4.** In the authors' prose discussion of NAEP, aggregate trends do not establish an indisputable reading crisis. They criticize treating the 34% at or above proficient as proof that most children cannot read adequately, and argue that including basic places approximately two thirds at or near grade level. They nonetheless express concern about disadvantaged students' disproportionate representation below basic. They present Ravitch's alternative interpretation of proficiency categories but explicitly say it is no more valid than anyone else's and that no indisputable operational or broadly consensual meaning exists beyond the cut scores. They also state that any score distribution has a bottom 30%.
  *Interpretation:* The authors challenge how assessment categories define children as failures while retaining concern about unequal outcomes. These are their interpretations of the categories and trends, reported from the prose rather than independently inferred from the figures.

- **p. 116 · CR 18.2, 20.1–20.3.** The authors identify social, cultural, demographic, and economic influences on achievement, including increasing racial and ethnic diversity, more non-native English speakers, and widening economic disparities. They report the argument that income inequality matters more than in-school factors. They consider one instructional variable unlikely to explain virtually all achievement variation, and insufficient phonics even less likely to explain crime and incarceration, as the course suggests.
  *Interpretation:* Students' learning is situated within changing social conditions. The authors resist reducing both educational difficulty and broader social harms to phonics provision.

- **p. 125, note 6 · CR 37.1.** Qualifying Flesch's comparison of American and European reading difficulties, the authors report that children learning English have been found to take twice as long to learn phonics basics as children reading more regularly spelled European languages. They say Flesch failed to acknowledge English's more irregular letter–sound relations.
  *Interpretation:* The language being learned matters to accounts of children's progress; national differences cannot simply be attributed to whether teachers use phonics.

- **pp. 117–118 · CR 22.1–22.3, 23.2; generalization questions also in CR 22, moves and meaning.** The article reports the strongest NRP phonics effects in kindergarten and first grade, no statistically significant word-identification effects for low-achieving readers in Grades 2–6, and predominantly struggling-reader samples. It asks whether those samples justify claims for all children and whether results on isolated regular words or pseudowords extend to authentic connected text. For the nonsignificant result it entertains several possibilities, including other instruction, more intensive phonics, or too few comparisons.
  *Interpretation:* Learners differ by stage, achievement, and task. The authors' questions leave explanations open; they do not conclude that older struggling readers cannot benefit from phonics.

- **p. 119 · CR 24.2–24.3.** In quotations the authors endorse, the NRP says phonics must help children use skills in daily reading and writing, notes that a program fits some students better than others, and calls for guidance on flexible instructional groups and pacing.
  *Interpretation:* Students need a usable understanding of phonics and instruction responsive to their differing progress.

- **p. 121 · CR 28.2–28.3.** The authors endorse the NRP recommendation that first graders receive both controlled-vocabulary decoding practice and exposure to quality literature, story, vocabulary, and comprehension. The quoted report says competence should be evaluated through interest in books and understanding information read aloud as well as phonics, attending to all processes contributing to reading growth.
  *Interpretation:* The successful learner is assessed more broadly than through decoding performance alone.

- **pp. 122–123 · CR 31.3–31.4, 32.4, 33.2.** The authors argue that a general research finding may not determine the best instruction for a particular student in a particular context. They report Ellis and Solms's interpretation of emotional and motivational brain functions as mediating all learning. They deny that the NRP or subsequent research establishes one indisputable scientific boundary governing instruction for every student.
  *Interpretation:* Individual circumstances and affective dimensions remain relevant even when science informs teaching.

- **p. 124 · CR 34.5; the conclusion's specific emphasis is not recorded in CR 35.** Beyond describing teaching and learning as embedded in systems involving equity and justice, the authors' conclusion singles out children living in poverty and experiencing institutional inequities and injustice when rejecting final answers about teaching every child. The close reading records the general social-system argument but leaves out this specific emphasis in the conclusion.
  *Interpretation:* Their objection to universal prescription has an explicit equity dimension, not solely an individual-difference rationale.

- **pp. 124–125 · CR 35.3.** The authors say ideologically manufactured truth pursued through legislation has real consequences contrary to the interests of children, families, communities, and teachers. They prioritize serving students' needs in their concluding criticism of advocacy.
  *Interpretation:* Students are the central stakeholders on whose behalf the commentary argues. They are represented by the authors rather than directly speaking within this article.

## Where the close reading and the article differ

- **p. 110 · CR 11.1, omission of specific mechanisms.** The close reading records punitive legislative proposals generally but omits the proposed negation of teaching certificates and proposed parental legal proceedings against districts. Those details clarify how professional preparation and authority could be subjected to political enforcement. The article presents them as proposals, not established enactments.

- **p. 119 · CR 24.2, compressed quotation.** The close reading says the teacher's role needs better understanding, but omits the quoted NRP passage's specific concern that scripts largely eliminate teacher judgment and its question about reconciling consistency with teachers' unique contributions. The answer above restores that tension.

- **p. 124 · CR 35, omission.** The conclusion explicitly emphasizes children living in poverty and experiencing institutional inequities and injustice in its rejection of final instructional answers for every child. CR 35 does not record this point. CR 34.5's general account of equity and justice does not preserve the conclusion's particular emphasis.

- **pp. 128–130 · CR 19.2 and 34.2, corrupted bibliographic support.** The support attached to CR 19.2 does not faithfully isolate the article's Stahl and Miller (1989) reference: it begins with authorship and text from another entry and concatenates numerous references. The support for CR 34.2 likewise begins with a different article and runs through many entries before reaching the correct Yaden, Reinking, and Smagorinsky (2021) reference, printed on p. 130. These are faults in the close reading's supporting apparatus, not grounds to change the corresponding body claims. The answers use this article's prose, without consulting the cited works.

No substantive reversal of the relevant body claims was found.
