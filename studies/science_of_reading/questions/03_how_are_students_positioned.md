# How are students positioned?

Asked 2026-11-25. Answered from the six legislation discourse analyses (`outputs/legislation/*/run/discourse_analysis.md`), mainly the subject positioning (SUB), presupposition (PRE), and foregrounding (FOS) sections, not by retrieval. The study has no index yet, and the scholarly and popular-press corpora are still empty, so this answer covers legislation only.

## Short answer

Students are positioned two ways at once: as **beneficiaries** of required instruction and intervention, and as **objects of measurement and placement**, identified by screeners, compared with a grade-level standard, and promoted or retained. The analyses code this as SUB-3 (student as victim / beneficiary), qualified every time. The beneficiary side is clear, but no law casts students as victims of earlier instruction.

What varies is how much a reading score is allowed to define the student. Ohio speaks of a "substantial deficiency in reading," but it also recognizes proficiency in other fields. Minnesota forbids retention "solely due to delays in literacy." California alone treats students' home languages as assets.

## Law by law

| Law | How students are positioned |
| --- | --- |
| Arkansas Act 1063 (2017) | **Nearly absent.** The law regulates teachers and districts. Students appear only through the "Right to Read" title, which names reading as something owed without saying who holds the right, and through parents being notified when a district is put on probation. |
| Arkansas Code § 6-17-429 (2025) | **Beneficiaries under progression rules.** A third grader who hasn't met the reading standard "shall not be promoted," unless exempted for good cause. Required support includes at least 90 minutes a day of science of reading instruction, and exempted students stay in the support group. English learners with less than three years in a program are exempt, and dyslexia gets a named place in the materials rules. |
| California AB 1454 (2025) | **Learners with linguistic assets.** Materials in "pupils' primary languages" should let teachers "build upon pupils' linguistic assets." English learners' language-development needs are a required criterion for materials, and tiered supports are named for students with reading difficulties, English learners, and students with exceptional needs. There is no retention. |
| Georgia HB 1193 (2026) | **Supported learners and objects of placement decisions.** Students at risk get intensive intervention and may be retained after it. A committee decides, and no one stays in first grade more than two years. Screeners identify students "with characteristics of dyslexia" within a broader at-risk group. English learners are named in teacher preparation and placement guidance, but not in the screener requirements. |
| Minnesota Read Act (2026) | **Universally capable, with continuing support and protection from retention.** The law rests on the belief "that every student can learn and thrive." A student below grade level keeps getting intervention "until the student reads at grade level," and "may not be retained solely due to delays in literacy." Students in partner-language programs are screened in that language, and teachers build on "strengths in their native languages." |
| Ohio Revised Code § 3313.6028 et al. (2026) | **Deficient readers, service recipients, and capable in other fields.** Students are identified as having "a substantial deficiency in reading" and receive "intense remediation services until the student is able to read at grade level." Retained students who are proficient in another subject must be taught at their level in it. All students "benefit" from the listed instruction. English learners are recognized through exceptions to retention. |

## Patterns across the six

- **Identified by measurement.** Screening and the grade-level standard decide who is struggling and what happens next, so assessment organizes the response (coded FOS) in Arkansas 2025, Georgia, Minnesota, and Ohio.
- **Retention divides the laws.** Arkansas 2025 and Ohio restrict third-grade promotion, with exceptions. Georgia allows first-grade retention after intervention, with a cap. Minnesota forbids retention on literacy alone, and California says nothing about it.
- **Support is guaranteed, often until grade level.** Arkansas, Ohio, and Minnesota make intervention continue until the student reads at grade level.
- **Dyslexia is named, within a broader group.** Georgia, Arkansas 2025, and Minnesota name dyslexia specifically, but in all three it is one kind of reading risk among others.
- **English learners range from exceptions to assets.** Arkansas, Ohio, and Georgia mainly recognize them through exemptions and placement rules. Minnesota makes the partner language part of screening and instruction, and California treats it as an asset to build on.
- **No victim narrative.** No law says students were harmed by earlier instruction, and none names racial or income groups when positioning students.
- **Students never speak or act.** Parents, teachers, principals, and committees decide; students are acted on.
