# How is teachers' knowledge positioned?

Asked 2026-11-25. Answered from the six legislation discourse analyses (`outputs/legislation/*/run/discourse_analysis.md`), mainly the subject positioning (SUB) and presupposition (PRE) sections, not by retrieval. The study has no index yet, and the scholarly and popular-press corpora are still empty, so this answer covers legislation only.

## Short answer

Across the six laws, teachers' knowledge is something the state must **verify, update, and supervise**, and it is never something teachers are simply trusted to have. Every law except California puts a duty on the teacher, such as a credential, a demonstration, required training, or fidelity checks. But none of them says outright that teachers lack the knowledge. The analyses code the knowledge-gap presupposition (PRE-3) as "reframed" or "qualified" in every case. The gap shows up as a missing credential or a gap in getting research into the classroom, not as a finding that teachers don't know.

Alongside the regulation, most laws also position teachers as **developing professionals** who are owed support: districts must provide training, and coaches are kept out of evaluation. How the two are balanced varies by state.

## Law by law

| Law | How teachers' knowledge is positioned |
| --- | --- |
| Arkansas Act 1063 (2017) | **Credentialed knowledge, in levels.** Teachers must show "proficiency" (K–6 and special education) or "awareness" (all others) in "scientific reading instruction" through a prescribed credential, as a condition of licensure. Districts must provide the professional development, and failing to do so is an accreditation violation. The law says who must know how much far more clearly than what the knowledge contains. |
| Arkansas Code § 6-17-429 (2025) | **Knowledge proven through demonstration and evaluation.** Teacher status alone isn't enough; knowledge counts once it's credentialed. A teacher who hasn't shown proficiency can be placed in "intensive support status" under an evaluator. Coaches "partner with teachers," and teachers decide what additional services a student needs, but coaching feedback can feed into evaluations. |
| California AB 1454 (2025) | **The lightest touch.** No new duty or sanction falls on classroom teachers. The requirement lands on preparation programs, which must prepare administrators and specialists to "support teachers." The presumed need is in the preparation system, not in individual teachers. |
| Georgia HB 1193 (2026) | **Teachers supported, coaches verified.** Educators "shall have access to and be provided with research on the science of reading," so the gap is framed as getting research into practice. Coaching must be "nonevaluative" and "side-by-side," and coaches may not evaluate teachers or join placement decisions. Coaches, by contrast, must periodically prove their own competence and ensure curricula are implemented "with fidelity." |
| Minnesota Read Act (2026) | **Accountable learners, with protections.** Approved training is the route to the literacy goal ("To meet this goal…"). Not finishing it because of "insubordination or willful refusal" leads to discipline, but delay for leave or a learning disability doesn't cost the teacher anything. Teachers trained under the newest preparation standards are exempt. Literacy leads treat educators as "adult learners," and the department must give facilitators ongoing coaching. |
| Ohio Revised Code § 3313.6028 et al. (2026) | **Existing knowledge that needs maintaining.** All teachers, administrators, school psychologists, and speech-language pathologists must complete science of reading training. The course "updates and reinforces" knowledge, which assumes the knowledge already exists. Recent coursework can exempt someone from the introductory course, and the hours count toward license renewal. |

## Patterns across the six

- **Regulated, but not called deficient.** All six analyses find teachers regulated (SUB-1). None finds the law saying teachers are deficient, and each flags that a training mandate could just as well be ordinary professional standard-setting.
- **Knowledge is defined by who must have it, not by what it is.** Arkansas 2017 is the clearest case: it creates credential levels but never states their content.
- **The state certifies knowledge.** The state decides what counts and who has it, through credentials, approved training, and competency assessments.
- **Teacher judgment appears only in small places.** Arkansas 2025 lets teachers decide on additional services, and Minnesota lets a parent and teacher jointly opt an older student out of screening. No law gives teachers a say in setting the standard.
- **Support and surveillance sit side by side.** Georgia separates them most clearly, with nonevaluative coaching for teachers and verification for coaches. Arkansas 2025 connects them, since coaching feedback can enter evaluations.
