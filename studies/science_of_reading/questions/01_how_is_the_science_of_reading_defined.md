# How is the science of reading defined?

Asked 2026-11-25. Answered from the six legislation analyses (`outputs/legislation/*/run/analysis.json` and `discourse_analysis.md`), not by retrieval: the study has no index yet. The scholarly and popular-press corpora are still empty, so this covers legislation only.

## Short answer

Only two of the six laws define the term outright, and they define it differently:

- **Ohio** calls it a body of evidence: an "interdisciplinary body of scientific evidence" that informs how students learn to read and write, explains why some have difficulty, says all students benefit from explicit and systematic instruction in six components, and does not rely on three-cueing.
- **Arkansas (2025)** calls it a field of study: "the study of the relationship between cognitive science and educational outcomes."

The other four laws use the phrase without defining it, or don't use it at all. Every law except Arkansas 2017 gives the term its content by other means: a list of instructional components, a description of how instruction should proceed ("explicit, systematic…"), or a ban on a named method (three-cueing).

## Law by law

| Law | Uses "science of reading"? | How it is defined |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arkansas Act 1063 (2017) | No; "scientific reading instruction" | Not defined. Teachers must know "the best practices of scientific reading instruction," with no components, research source, or criteria. The only anchor is a board-adopted assessment. |
| Arkansas Code § 6-17-429 (2025) | Yes, defined | "The study of the relationship between cognitive science and educational outcomes" (n)(1). Structured literacy is defined separately as teaching "in an explicit, systematic, cumulative, and diagnostic manner" (n)(2). Interventions aligned to it address the five components (phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary, comprehension). K–2 programs may not use three-cueing or visual memory as the primary basis for word recognition. |
| California AB 1454 (2025) | No | Never uses the label. "Evidence-based means of teaching foundational reading skills" is given a list broader than the five pillars: print concepts, phonological awareness, phonics and word recognition, fluency, oral and written language, vocabulary and background knowledge, comprehension, and tiered supports for struggling readers, English learners, and students with exceptional needs. No method is banned. |
| Georgia HB 1193 (2026) | Yes, 18 times, not defined in the bill | Used as the standard everything must align to, "exclusively" in teacher-preparation standards, and "only" aligned materials may be used in K–3. The meaning is deferred to Code § 20-2-153.1, which the bill doesn't reproduce. The closest description is in the coaching endorsement: instruction "founded on the science of how children learn to read" and "explicit, systematic, cumulative, and diagnostic" in phonology, sound-symbol association, syllables, morphology, syntax, and semantics. |
| Minnesota Read Act (2026) | Yes, not defined | Framed as the Act's purpose (instruction "aligned with the science of reading"), but defined only indirectly. Structured literacy is "consistent with the principles identified in the science of reading": systematic, explicit, sequential, diagnostic instruction in phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary and oral language, and comprehension. "Evidence-based" adds spelling and oral language and excludes three-cueing (MSV). Comprehension is "a function of word recognition skills and language comprehension skills" and also "an active process" of constructing meaning, close to the Simple View, though not named. |
| Ohio Revised Code § 3313.6028 et al. (2026) | Yes, defined | An "interdisciplinary body of scientific evidence" that (a) informs how students learn to read and write proficiently, (b) explains why some students have difficulty, (c) indicates all students benefit from explicit and systematic instruction in phonemic awareness, phonics, vocabulary, fluency, comprehension, and writing, and (d) does not rely on meaning, structure and syntax, and visual cues, including three-cueing. Other sections borrow this definition. The PreK–5 three-cueing ban allows waivers and IEP exceptions. |

## Patterns across the six

- **Research, field, or program.** Ohio defines a body of evidence, and Arkansas 2025 defines a field of study. Georgia and Minnesota treat it as a standard that curricula, materials, and training must "align to," without saying what it contains.
- **The five components are the common anchor.** Arkansas 2025, Minnesota, and Ohio list them, and California's list resembles them. Ohio adds writing, Minnesota adds spelling and oral language, and California adds print concepts, background knowledge, and language development. None names the National Reading Panel.
- **Manner of teaching does definitional work.** "Explicit and systematic" appears everywhere it is defined. Arkansas, Georgia, and Minnesota add "cumulative" or "sequential" and "diagnostic," the structured-literacy descriptors. No law attributes them to the International Dyslexia Association.
- **Exclusion defines the boundary.** Arkansas 2025, Minnesota, and Ohio define the term partly by what it is not: three-cueing / MSV. Ohio writes the exclusion into the definition itself. California and Arkansas 2017 ban nothing.
- **Comprehension stays in.** No law ranks phonics above comprehension, and Minnesota defines comprehension as active meaning-making.
- **Scientific authority without a source.** None of the six names a study, report, or organization as the source of its definition.
