## CO. Conclusions

Section-level and closing conclusions the author draws (as opposed to reported evidence).

| ID | Conclusion | Location |
|---|---|---|
| CO1 | AI's three workload characteristics (higher compute/heat; near-100% GPU utilization for weeks or months; concentration at hyperscale sites with 50–100+ kW/rack vs 5–10 kW) "establish that AI infrastructure presents a qualitatively different water challenge from general-purpose cloud computing, warranting dedicated assessment and mitigation strategies." | §1 end, p. 2 |
| CO2 | Although U.S. data center consumption (66 billion L in 2023, 0.066 km³) is a small fraction of national/global withdrawals (~4000 km³ globally; agriculture 72%, industry 15%, municipal 13%), "aggregate comparisons understate the problem" because demand is geographically concentrated, often in water-stressed regions, and growing; data-center water serves digital services "whose societal necessity varies widely"; so local scarcity context "is therefore essential." | §3, p. 3 |
| CO3 | Water footprint of AI data centers is "primarily determined by cooling architecture and local climate"; "similar AI workloads can translate into vastly different local water burdens." | §3.1, p. 3 |
| CO4 | Spatial distribution of AI infrastructure "reveals systematic disregard for water availability constraints." | §3.2 opening, p. 3 |
| CO5 | Corporate aggregates "mask facility-level impacts and hinder site specific accountability." | §3.2, p. 3 |
| CO6 | The Colón case: the mechanism is institutional (concessions by CONAGUA based on availability assessments that may not reflect current conditions; industrial allocations carry legal standing and are rarely reallocated in drought); this rigidity "is not unique to Mexico"; "Such allocation failures raise fundamental environmental justice dimensions." | §3.2, p. 3 |
| CO7 | "Substantial water reductions are technically achievable," but each approach has trade-offs, scalability constraints, or unresolved ecological questions. | §4 intro, p. 3 |
| CO8 | Three interconnected trade-offs constrain water-sustainable AI: (1) financial–environmental (water priced far below scarcity value favours evaporative cooling); (2) energy–water (dry cooling saves water but raises electricity/carbon); (3) regional (sunny regions arid; water-abundant northern regions rely partly on hydropower with reservoir evaporation). "No single siting or technology choice resolves all three tensions simultaneously," so integrated water–energy–carbon assessment is needed. | §4 end, pp. 4–5 |
| CO9 | Dedicated nuclear/SMRs could reduce indirect water from fossil grids but SMRs with conventional cooling would require cooling water, "potentially shifting rather than eliminating water demand"; net implications depend on design/siting and "merit further assessment." | §4 end, p. 5 |
| CO10 | Technical solutions show water-efficient AI is achievable, but "their limited deployment and scalability show that technology alone is insufficient"; water-sustainable AI "requires robust governance frameworks" for accountability, standardized disclosure, and reframed societal expectations. | §5 intro, p. 5 |
| CO11 | Siting concentration "reflects a market failure": siting is driven by electricity cost, latency, tax incentives, land; water priced as a bulk commodity does not register as material financial risk; companies "externalize the true cost of water consumption onto local communities and ecosystems, a pattern that will persist absent regulatory correction." | §5.1, p. 5 |
| CO12 | Digital water sobriety proposes not binary approval/prohibition but "a graduated governance approach linking resource costs to demonstrated societal benefit" (spectrum from medical diagnostics/climate modelling/drug discovery to recreational image generation/marketing/low-stakes queries); mechanisms: scarcity-adjusted pricing, mandatory water impact assessments above computational thresholds (EIA analogy), facility-level water budgets tied to real-time watershed conditions, preferential permitting. | §5.1, p. 5 |
| CO13 | Water governance may have structural advantages over climate/biodiversity governance (local, visible impacts; existing water rights/permitting infrastructure; municipal control), but those precedents are cautionary: effective digital water sobriety "will require sustained political will and enforceable regulatory mechanisms, not merely voluntary corporate commitments." | §5.1, p. 5 |
| CO14 | "Awareness alone is insufficient" (weak correlations between digital-pollution knowledge and willingness to adopt sustainable practices, Barnett-Itzhaki and Tsoury, 2025); "AI's water footprint results from deliberate policy and design choices, not technological necessity." | §5.1 end, p. 5 |
| CO15 | "Corporate responses to water concerns remain inadequate despite growing awareness": "water positive by 2030" pledges rely on replenishment credits that fail to address temporal/spatial mismatch; WUE reporting "remains voluntary and unstandardized," aggregate data obscures facility impacts. | §5.2, p. 5 |
| CO16 | Regulation is beginning (Virginia's proposed disclosure bill; similar initiatives in Europe and western U.S.), "yet transparency remains severely limited"; standardized protocols and mandatory disclosure "are critical priorities." | §5.3, p. 5 |
| CO17 | Five research priorities "remain major bottlenecks": AI-specific water accounting; lifecycle water integration; ecological impact monitoring; district heating techno-economics; standardized disclosure protocols. | §6.1, pp. 5–6 |
| CO18 | Four policy imperatives: mandatory facility-level transparency (on-site, embedded-in-electricity, temporal variation); water-conscious siting regulations (restrict new construction in severely stressed watersheds, cumulative impact assessment in permitting); digital water sobriety frameworks (tiered governance; listed mechanisms); heat recovery integration incentives. | §6.2, p. 6 |
| CO19 | The central question "shifts from marginal efficiency to resource justice: how much 'intelligence' per liter of water is justified"; Meta Luleå (25 million L/yr "suffices for hyperscale operations") and China's subsea installations (zero freshwater) suggest "outcomes reflect policy and siting choices, not technological necessity." | §6.3, p. 6 |
| CO20 | "The Colón case epitomizes governance failures"; consuming public water during drought while agricultural communities face restrictions "represents governance failure, not technical constraint." | §6.3, p. 6 |
| CO21 | Closing: choices on where AI is deployed, how cooled, and whose water it consumes will determine whether it exacerbates water insecurity or catalyzes integrated water–energy–heat innovation; "Water-sustainable AI is achievable, the question is whether the society possesses the political will to implement it before computational expansion irreversibly deepens water stress in already vulnerable regions." | §6.3 end, p. 6 |
| CO22 | **§4.1 Cold-climate siting and free-air cooling.** **Conclusion:** cold-climate siting lets operators "exploit low ambient temperatures for free-air cooling, substantially reducing or eliminating the need for mechanical chillers and evaporative cooling towers"; facilities in Nordic regions (Sweden, Finland, Norway, Iceland) "can achieve exceptional energy and water efficiency with very low WUE." Qualified: "hydropower itself is not water-free" — reservoir evaporation "can result in significant consumptive water losses that vary widely among plants" (Scherer and Pfister, 2016), and "This indirect water cost should be considered in lifecycle assessments of data centers powered by hydroelectricity." **Trade-offs:** "Limited geographic flexibility, potentially increasing electricity transmission distances and associated losses. Seasonal temperature variability requires backup cooling systems for hot days." **Scalability:** Nordic regions suitable for year-round free-air cooling "are geographically limited and face competing demands for renewable energy and infrastructure capacity." **Example:** Meta's data center in Luleå, Sweden ("located south of the Arctic Circle"), which "withdraws only 6.7 million gallons (25.4 million liters) of water annually" (Zhang, 2024), "achieving minimal on-site water consumption through predominantly air-cooled operation"; powered by hydropower from the nearby Lule River (~13.6 million MWh/yr). | §4.1, p. 3 |
| CO23 | **§4.2 Natural water bodies as thermal sinks.** **Conclusion:** installations coupled to lakes, rivers, or seawater "via closed-loop heat exchangers, eliminating consumptive withdrawals while leveraging substantial thermal mass"; "These systems may have low consumptive water use and favourable WUE, but they raise ecological questions about local warming plumes, effects on stratification, and cumulative impacts if widely scaled." **Trade-offs and uncertainties:** "These systems present unresolved ecological concerns." "Discharge temperatures elevated by 5–8 °C can disrupt thermal stratification in lakes and affect dissolved oxygen levels, with potential cascading effects on aquatic ecosystems." "Neither facility has published independent long-term ecological monitoring data despite multi-year operation." "In many jurisdictions, particularly the European Union, strict regulations limit the permissible temperature increase of returned cooling water"; by analogy with thermoelectric plants forced to curtail in heat waves (projected 6–19% capacity reductions in Europe by mid-century; van Vliet et al., 2012), data centers "employing once-through or open-loop cooling from natural water bodies would face analogous regulatory constraints, creating an operational and financial risk: facilities may face forced curtailment precisely during extreme heat events when cooling demand peaks." (The curtailment risk is stated for once-through or open-loop systems.) **Scalability:** "geographically constrained to sites with suitable water bodies, adequate depth, and acceptable flow rates"; environmental impact assessment requirements, thermal discharge regulations, and public opposition "may further limit deployment, particularly in jurisdictions with stringent water quality standards." **Examples:** Google's Hamina facility, Finland, drawing seawater from the Bay of Finland through heat exchange modules, "ranking as the company's most energy-efficient data center" (Google, 2025); Alibaba's Qiandao Lake facility, China, which "pipes 17 °C water from 35 m depth, achieving WUE below 0.2 and enabling free cooling over 90% of operational time" (Mah, 2015). | §4.2, p. 4 |
| CO24 | **§4.3 Subsea data centers.** **Conclusion:** subsea facilities "place sealed server capsules directly on the seabed, using surrounding seawater as a passive heat sink"; the Hainan installation achieves "WUE of zero by eliminating freshwater consumption entirely." **Trade-offs:** "Microsoft discontinued its Project Natick subsea demonstration (2018–2020) in 2024 despite technical success, suggesting economic or operational constraints" (the constraints are the author's inference: "suggesting"). "Potential impacts include thermal pollution affecting local water temperatures and marine organism behavior, acoustic disturbances from cooling systems, electromagnetic emissions, chemical leaching from construction materials, and benthic habitat disruption during installation and maintenance." "Initial assessments report minimal thermal effects (<1 °C temperature rise), but comprehensive ecological monitoring data remain unpublished." **Scalability:** "High capital costs, complex maintenance requirements, and limited suitable sites constrain widespread deployment. Regulatory frameworks for subsea industrial installations are underdeveloped." **Examples:** China's Hainan Underwater Intelligent Computing Center, "the first commercial-scale installation with capacity for 400–800 AI servers" (CGTN, 2025; Xiaoying, 2025); Microsoft's Project Natick as the discontinued counter-example (no location given). | §4.3, p. 4 |
| CO25 | **§4.4 "Waterless" systems and non-potable water use** (the heading puts "Waterless" in quotation marks). **Conclusion:** modular data centers "combining immersion cooling or direct-to-chip liquid cooling with dry heat rejection can achieve near-zero on-site water consumption"; other facilities "reduce pressure on potable supplies by using treated wastewater or reclaimed water." **Trade-offs:** "Waterless cooling typically increases electricity consumption for dry heat rejection by approximately 10–25% compared to evaporative alternatives, particularly in hot climates, and can shift a portion of cooling-related water use from the data center to water-cooled power plants, depending on the local grid mix." Because electricity is "the dominant operational cost for data centers (typically 40–60% of total operating expenses), while water is priced far below its scarcity value in most jurisdictions, operators face strong economic incentives to favour evaporative cooling over dry alternatives" — in contrast with thermal power plants in arid regions, "where regulatory mandates have driven dry cooling adoption despite similar energy penalties" (Shehabi et al., 2024). "Non-potable water use reduces potable water pressure but requires dual infrastructure systems and treatment facilities, increasing costs." **Scalability:** "Dry cooling performance degrades in hot/dry climates where water-scarcity is acute, limiting geographic applicability. Reclaimed water availability varies by region and may face competing demands from agriculture." **Examples:** waterless — ZutaCore's HyperCool, "completely waterless two-phase cooling capable of handling hundreds of megawatts" (ZutaCore, 2025), and Deep Green's immersion-cooled units "capturing 96% of waste heat for reuse" (Deep Green, n.d.), neither with a location; non-potable — AWS reclaimed wastewater systems "at 20 facilities, including 16 in Virginia" (Swinhoe, 2023); Google non-potable water "at over 25% of campuses" (Hölzle, 2022); Los Alamos National Laboratory's facility in New Mexico, treated non-potable water (Waldvogel et al., 2024). | §4.4, p. 4 |
| CO26 | **§4.5 Waste-heat recovery.** **Conclusion:** integrating data centers into district heating repositions "waste heat as a resource rather than a disposal problem" (Wahlroos et al., 2018). **System-level benefits:** "Waste heat recovery enhances overall energy efficiency by enabling dual services: computing and thermal energy, from single electrical input, potentially reducing demand for separate heat generation"; when substituting for fossil-fuel-based district heating, "this may yield indirect water savings by displacing thermal power generation" (Yuan et al., 2023). **Trade-offs and constraints:** "Waste heat recovery requires proximity to heat demand, compatible temperature profiles, and seasonal alignments with data center operation. It may not reduce on-site water consumption, instead, it can shift water demand within the energy system so lifecycle analysis is needed to quantify net benefits." **Scalability:** "Currently most examples are pilot-scale"; systematic integration "requires coordinated planning, capital investment in heat distribution networks, and policy frameworks incentivizing heat recovery over simpler cooling approaches." **Examples:** Deep Green's UK swimming pool installations "offset 62% of conventional natural gas heating requirements" (Judge, 2023); for the Paris 2024 Olympics, Equinix "exported server heat to the Olympic Aquatics Centre via district heating network" (Kerner and Walker, 2024); Stockholm's Open District Heating scheme, which "partners with approximately 20 heat suppliers including multiple data centers, recovering heat sufficient for roughly 30,000 apartments"; Microsoft's planned data center region in Finland (Espoo), "expected to supply around 40% of the district heating demand for approximately 250,000 customers" (Espoo, 2022) — a planned facility, stated as "expected". | §4.5, p. 4 |
