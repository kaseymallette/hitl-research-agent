## B1. Premise acceptability — 2

The evaluation weighs the evidence behind the central conclusions rather than accepting the model outputs as observations of operating facilities. It distinguishes the accounting identity in Eq. (4), p. 7, from the empirical adequacy of SPECpower observations, fitted server-performance surfaces, cooling simulations, and U.S. electricity factors. Its discussion of the benchmark denominator is supported by §2.2, pp. 7–8, SI S2, and the explicit representativeness and omitted-load limitations on p. 27. Its treatment of annual representative-city weather is supported by SI S3, especially Table S3.2 (PDF p. 43). It also recognizes positive evidence: joint PUE/WUE modeling captures the cooling tradeoff in §3.3 and Fig. 4, and Fig. 3 supplies a sufficient counterexample to comparisons based on WUE alone. These are substantive judgments about method, provenance, and applicability, with identifiable passages.

## B2. Relevance of critique — 1

Most criticisms attach closely to actual claims, preserving important qualifiers. The evaluation acknowledges the paper's stated omissions, recognizes that low-WCF power remains favorable despite the numerical inconsistency, and treats cooling benefits as conditional. Its concern about fair comparisons has a real target: §4 calls workload-level accounting the ideal comparison basis, and §5, p. 26, claims that the analysis enables impartial comparisons.

There is nevertheless some enlargement of the target in the sensitivity discussion and final verdict. A modeled importance ranking is stated in the abstract and §3.7, but the paper does not propose a universal order of interventions or identify the cheapest intervention. Section 5, p. 26, explicitly calls for case-by-case strategies and specifies circumstances under which server efficiency is the most effective lever. The evaluation's warning that sensitivity should not be read as a universal order is useful, but its conclusion that such an order is not earned can read as a criticism of a stronger prescription than the authors make. Likewise, availability is not an unstated constraint: §3.5, p. 19, expressly names the availability of grid options. These are limited scope problems, not a straw man underlying the evaluation's main argument.

## B3. Sufficiency — 2

The evaluation consistently distinguishes what follows from what is merely possible. It explains why one modeled counterexample suffices to defeat the inference from low WUE to low water use per workload, while a modeled range does not establish prevalence or typical savings. It distinguishes benchmark comparability from equivalence of production services; sensitivity contributions from feasible or cost-effective interventions; and operational intensity savings from reductions in national totals. It identifies what additional support would be needed: equivalent service definitions and boundaries, site-specific electricity substitution and operating conditions, local and seasonal consequences, and assumptions about demand and implementation. These address the main measurement and conservation conclusions, not just peripheral results.

## B4. Unstated warrants and missing qualifiers — 2

The evaluation identifies consequential inferential bridges and explains their importance. The move from benchmark operations to impartial comparisons requires equivalence of delivered services, beyond merely standardizing the benchmark. The move from fewer liters per workload to reduced national consumption requires workload growth not to offset intensity savings. The move from an unweighted sum of onsite and offsite consumption to relief of local water stress requires judgments about where and when consumption occurs. The move from variance importance to intervention priority requires feasible changes and implementation evidence. The first three are well grounded in the tension between Eqs. (1)–(4), the broad comparison claims on pp. 24–26, the local motivation on p. 2, and the national conservation/co-benefit claims on pp. 26–27. Some other constraints it discusses, notably benchmark representativeness, omitted loads, U.S.-average WCFs, and electricity availability, are already stated by the authors; the evaluation generally acknowledges this instead of presenting all of them as discoveries of unstated assumptions.

## B5. Self-attribution — 2

The evaluative conclusions are plainly the evaluator's. The closing paragraph explicitly identifies its preference for local-consequence and implementation evidence and concedes that this standard exceeds what a determinant study alone must deliver. Its account of disciplinary framing is presented as interpretation, with the affiliations on manuscript p. 1 as its basis. It distinguishes that interpretation from a claim of commercial motive and accurately notes the public and philanthropic support acknowledged on p. 28. Author claims, evaluator inferences, and alternative policy possibilities remain distinguishable.

## Assessment for the researcher

The evaluation offers a substantially reliable, independent assessment of the paper's measurement logic and the limits of its numerical and policy conclusions. It gives credit to the accounting argument, checks the preferred technologies, identifies consequential unstated warrants, acknowledges its own policy-oriented standard, and catches a genuine figure/table inconsistency. Its main weakness is occasional drift from the authors' conditional determinant analysis to stronger possible readings, especially a universal intervention order. Preserve its distinctions between demonstrated accounting results, modeled possibilities, and untested implementation outcomes; qualify those broader targets before relying on them as defects in what the authors actually claim.
