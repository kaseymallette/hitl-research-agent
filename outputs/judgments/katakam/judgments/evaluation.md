## B1. Premise acceptability — 2

The evaluation weighs the evidence supporting both central contributions: the desalination benchmark and SWUI's environmental interpretation. It explains why optimization over RO pressure/recovery and MVC exchanger-area/compressor-work tradeoffs supports the modeled engineering results (§§2.1–2.2, pp. 5–7), and checks the seawater hybrid's cost and energy decomposition against §3.2 (pp. 11–12). It limits that confidence to the modeled designs, scrutinizing NaCl feed chemistry, achievable recovery, and generic concentrate-handling charges (§2.4 and Table 1, pp. 8–9). For SWUI, it examines the provenance and relevance of the regional stress weighting and pooled WUE projections, rather than accepting the index as an observed environmental outcome (§§2.3–2.4, pp. 7–10). This is substantive assessment of the evidence behind the central conclusions, with identifiable passages.

## B2. Relevance of critique — 2

The criticisms address claims the article actually makes, with its qualifications preserved. In particular, the evaluation explicitly acknowledges that the authors do not interpret SWUI as saline-source depletion and instead invoke competing uses of desalinated freshwater (§2.3, p. 7; §3.3, p. 16). Its counterfactual objection therefore addresses that opportunity-cost rationale, not an invented claim that seawater extraction directly depletes freshwater. The stronger environmental language it challenges is present: §3.3 describes reduced water-stress impact, environmentally benign brine management, negligible additional energy/carbon burdens, and alleviated hydrological impacts (pp. 14–16). The chemistry criticism also acknowledges the authors' own future-work qualification (§2.4, p. 8). Its recommendations for comparing alternative supplies and cooling options are plainly its own proposed next step, not attributed requirements that the article claimed to have fulfilled.

## B3. Sufficiency — 2

The evaluation consistently distinguishes what the calculations establish from what they only permit. It accepts the conditional mass balance and modeled cost decomposition, but explains why these do not establish site-specific feasibility, a working residuals outlet, or environmental benefits. Its strongest example holds site and freshwater demand constant: Figure 7b's 5.0-to-2.9 SWUI change establishes reduced modeled saline intake, without itself establishing additional freshwater for other users. It also distinguishes robustness under alternative stress weightings from validation of impact categories, and a projected WUE input from evidence about a particular facility's future cooling system. It specifies the missing evidence: realistic feed chemistry, residual-stream destinations, competing-use counterfactuals, local hydrology, electricity supply, and comparisons at equal cooling service. These are developed for the central engineering and siting conclusions, not merely listed as general limitations.

## B4. Unstated warrants and missing qualifiers — 2

The evaluation identifies consequential inferential bridges: that desalinated product allocated to a data center would otherwise be available to other users; that lower stress-weighted intake establishes reduced regional water-stress impact; that smaller intake/brine volumes establish environmental improvement; and that a burden small relative to computing demand is negligible for choosing between treatments. It explains why each matters. The opportunity-cost rationale itself is stated in the article; what remains undefended is the existence of the alternative allocation in the cases analyzed. Likewise, the evaluation does not present the NaCl simplification as a hidden assumption: it treats it as an acknowledged limitation requiring conditional interpretation. Its proposed qualifications—modeled designs, conditional scenarios, and a screening index rather than demonstrated local improvement—follow from the evidence it discusses.

## B5. Self-attribution — 2

The evaluation clearly distinguishes its judgments from the authors' positions. Its final paragraph explicitly states its preference for counterfactual comparisons and recognizes that this standard could be too demanding for a first-pass equipment screen. It locates the authors' framing in the engineering design problem and treats the declared interests and university funding accurately (p. 18), without inventing a financial motive. Conditional examples about municipal buyers and freshwater wells are visibly the evaluator's reasoning, not reported facts about the three sites.

## Assessment for the researcher

This evaluation provides a well-grounded distinction between conditional engineering benchmarks and stronger environmental interpretations. Its main strengths are faithful treatment of the authors' qualifications, a concrete controlled comparison using Figure 7b, and explicit reflection on its own counterfactual standard. Its principal limitation is evidential reach: it establishes why site-specific benefits remain unproven, rather than independently validating equipment performance or determining actual outcomes at the three sites. That limit is acknowledged and appropriate to the supplied article; no factual correction was identified.
