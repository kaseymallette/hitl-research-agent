## B1. Premise acceptability — **1**

The run tests whether several pieces of evidence can bear the weight placed on them, and it names the passages:

- **The 25,500 m³ figure (§3, p. 3).** It redoes the arithmetic: 8.76 million kWh × 2 L/kWh = 17,520 m³, so the stated figure implies about 2.9 L/kWh. It judges that this "weakens the precision of a key scaling illustration".
- **The Luleå total (§6.3; Table 1 footnote a).** "An absolute facility total without a workload denominator cannot establish comparative efficiency".
- **The location evidence (§3.2).** Location in a stressed region "does not by itself establish" disregard without data on consumption, water sources and permits.
- **The Colón case.** It "does not quantify the data center's contribution".
- **The headline projection.** The 4.2–6.6 billion m³ figure is given without its assumptions or a scope that can be compared with the operational figures.
- **Withdrawal and consumption.** It notes the §2 caveat that most figures are withdrawals while the review's stated focus is consumption.

That is real weighing of the evidence behind conclusions (a) and (b). It falls short of 2 because it hardly looks at **provenance or method**, which is what B1 names first:

- It never says that much of the load-bearing evidence comes from journalism, a preprint, blogs or vendor sites, against §2's statement that peer-reviewed sources were prioritized (P23).
- It never questions the unspecified "targeted review" method.
- It doesn't notice that cited sources fail to match their claims (P24).
- It doesn't notice that the Colón case rests on one piece of investigative journalism.
- It describes the §3 comparison of U.S. consumption with global withdrawals as an honest concession ("This is not an evasion"). It doesn't see that the comparison sets a consumption figure against a withdrawal figure and a national figure against a global one (P15).
- The one point on provenance is "self-cited awareness studies" (§5.1).

Most of its critique is about the inference drawn from the evidence rather than whether the evidence deserves belief. On inference it is strong; on acceptability it is partial.

## B2. Relevance of critique — **2**

Each main criticism is aimed at the claim as the article states it:

- **"Systematic disregard."** Quoted from §3.2 and criticized as an inference from location alone.
- **"Not technological necessity."** Quoted from §6.3 and set against the constraints in §4. The run's "not universal substitutability" fits, because the article's claim carries no qualifier.
- **The Luleå claim.** "Suffices for hyperscale operations" is criticized for what it actually asserts.
- **Digital water sobriety.** The run does not attack a ban. It says outright that the "graduated approach is more plausible than binary prohibition", which matches §5.1's "does not propose binary approval or prohibition". It then criticizes the application ranking for having no decision procedure. That is fair: §5.1 links costs to "demonstrated societal benefit", which needs a procedure the article doesn't give. This is the reading the reference list's scope note for P31 asks for.
- **The water-bottle illustration.** The run says only that it "can imply" a stable per-interaction footprint. It doesn't say the article claims one.
- **The urgency claim.** The run criticizes the reason the article gives, that 73% of UK respondents were unfamiliar with "digital pollution", which is the reason actually stated.

I found no criticism aimed at a broader or weaker claim than the one made.

## B3. Sufficiency — **2**

This is the run's strongest criterion. For each central conclusion it says whether the evidence requires or only permits it, and what more would be needed:

- **Technical feasibility to "governance failure" (§6.3).** "Demonstrating freshwater avoidance at selected installations establishes possibility, not universal substitutability." Limited deployment doesn't show "how much additional deployment regulation could reasonably achieve. That would require comparative cost and implementation evidence."
- **Location to disregard (§3.2).** It needs "actual consumption, water sources, seasonal operations, and permit conditions".
- **Colón to attribution.** The case "provides a serious reason for investigation, but not a complete attribution of harm". It would also need "the distributional outcome under an alternative allocation".
- **The application ranking.** It "would need marginal benefits, available substitutes, beneficiaries, and attributable water costs". The run notes that §6.1 concedes the accounting doesn't exist.
- **Overall.** The technical review "supports several possible policies. It does not uniquely establish the author's full framework." "The evidence earns water-aware governance more clearly than it earns 'digital water sobriety' in its full normative and institutional form."

One soft spot: the run calls the disclosure recommendations "well supported" without applying the same test to them. In particular, it doesn't notice that the §5.3 sentence on what disclosure requires is cited to an unrelated source (P24). That doesn't lower the score, which concerns the central conclusions.

## B4. Unstated warrants and missing qualifiers — **2**

**Warrants it finds, and why they matter:**
- **Data-centre water use stands in for AI water use (P21).** "Evidence about data centers generally cannot isolate AI's incremental burden when the paper itself identifies AI-specific attribution as an unresolved research problem."
- **Location shows intent or disregard (P6).** The run gives a counterexample from the article's own technology discussion: a dry-cooled facility in a stressed region.
- **Possibility at selected sites shows that constraints are absent (P1).**
- **Political will is the decisive remaining obstacle (P28).**
- **Application labels track societal benefit (P27, P31).** Its counterexamples show why the warrant fails: medical systems that are "ineffective or commercially exclusionary", and conversational systems that support accessibility.

**Missing qualifiers it finds:**
- "Not technological necessity" is stated without the qualifier "at selected sites".
- "Suffices for hyperscale" is stated without a capacity denominator.
- "Systematic" is drawn from a pattern of locations alone.

**What it misses:** It doesn't track the hedges the article drops as claims are repeated:
- "suggests … built or planned" hardens into "located" (P18);
- §4.5's "may yield indirect water savings" becomes §6.2's "delivering co-benefits" (P5, caught only in substance);
- the abstract says waste heat "can reduce on-site demand", but §4.5 says it "may not" (P4).

On the normative side, it grants the framework's ethical premise and then attacks the decision procedure. It never names the descriptive-to-normative gap (P31 problem 1) as an unstated warrant. It does reach the conclusion that the ranking is "a proposal worth debating, not a conclusion established by the review". Even with these gaps, the run finds the warrants the central conclusions depend on and says why each matters, so the score is 2.

## B5. Self-attribution — **2**

**Its own judgments are marked as its own.** The run writes in its own voice throughout: "My judgment is…", "I agree with the framework's underlying ethical premise…", "I do not think that question should be excluded".

**It discusses bias on both sides:**
- **The author's.** "The author's sustainability perspective makes community vulnerability and externalized costs highly visible; it also encourages interpreting infrastructure expansion primarily as an allocation failure". It also notes that the awareness studies are self-cited.
- **Its own.** "My own preference for measurable impacts and administrable rules could underweight the legitimate democratic question of what scarce resources should support." It then says how that preference bears on its conclusion.

**One small attribution issue:** It gives the framework's "underlying ethical premise" as "ability to pay does not settle entitlement to a scarce common resource". The article's stated premise is that freshwater is "a finite common resource requiring equitable allocation" (§5.1). "Ability to pay" is the run's gloss. It also sits awkwardly with the article's own proposal of scarcity-adjusted pricing. This is a paraphrase of the author's premise, not a conclusion of the run's presented as the author's, so it doesn't lower the score.

## Strengths and weaknesses

Run B is a disciplined, well-aimed evaluation.

**Strengths:**
- **It covers the article's central structure.** It touches all nine Major problems. It gets the article's three biggest inferential leaps right: data-centre evidence stands in for AI; possibility at selected sites becomes a claim of "not technological necessity"; descriptive water evidence becomes an application-worth ranking.
- **Its sufficiency reasoning (B3) is excellent.** For each conclusion it says what evidence would be needed.
- **It catches the article's one checkable arithmetic error.**
- **It aims at the claims as made.** It doesn't straw-man the "digital water sobriety" framework into a ban.
- **Its bias reflection is genuine and specific.**
- **It applies the prompt's demand for symmetrical scrutiny to the author's preferred instruments.** Scarcity pricing could let wealthy firms outbid poorer users. WUE-based permitting could shift water burden to electricity. Thresholds could be gamed.

**Weaknesses:**
- **It works almost entirely at the level of inference.** It rarely asks whether the evidence itself is credible. It doesn't notice the gap between the stated peer-review priority and the journalistic and vendor sources the argument actually rests on, or the citations that don't fit their claims.
- **It misses several plain internal contradictions:**
  - the abstract says waste heat recovery reduces on-site demand, but §4.5 says it may not;
  - the "structural advantages" of municipal control conflict with the Mexico case;
  - "frameworks shown" in §6 refers to frameworks that were only proposed;
  - the hedge on the two-thirds statistic is lost as it is repeated.
- **It credits the disclosure recommendations as "well supported" more readily than its own standard would justify.**

Its selectivity is deliberate and mostly well chosen, but what it leaves out falls mainly on the article's evidence base and internal consistency.
