Kanbur, Wu, and Duan make a credible case for investigating their proposed system, but their claim of economic feasibility outruns the evidence they present. Published in Wiley’s *International Journal of Energy Research* in 2020, the paper addresses engineers familiar with heat transfer, energy-system modeling, and thermoeconomic optimization. Its purpose is both exploratory and persuasive: it proposes a new combination of immersion cooling and membrane distillation, then argues that the combination performs well enough to justify practical development. My judgment divides at that transition. The physical proposal is plausible, and the component modeling offers a useful starting point. The reported payback and optimal design, however, depend on calculations and boundaries that require substantial clarification.

The argument moves through a recognizable engineering sequence. Data centers generate heat that must be removed. Two-phase immersion cooling can collect that heat in a liquid stream. Membrane distillation can use relatively low-temperature heat to produce fresh water. Combining them might therefore turn a cooling obligation into a useful second product. Conservation equations translate this proposal into predicted performance; economic assumptions translate performance into costs; optimization selects a preferred configuration. This sequence is coherent. Its weakest premise is the assumption that successfully connecting the processes will produce a worthwhile overall system. The introduction establishes the importance of cooling and surveys neighboring heat-recovery applications, but those facts cannot establish the attractiveness of this particular integration. The paper nevertheless announces practical economic feasibility in the summary, before the reader encounters the assumptions needed to assess it.

The strongest evidence supports the thermal connection. The authors explain how seawater passing through the immersion tank’s condenser pipes becomes the heated feed for desalination. Their temperature constraint also respects the cooling mechanism: the seawater must remain below the dielectric liquid’s boiling temperature, with an additional engineering margin. Table 1 reports agreement within approximately 1.5% between their membrane-module calculations and a prior study under matching conditions. Earlier work supports the immersion-cooling component. These are meaningful reasons to regard the proposal as technically investigable. The authors also acknowledge that the integrated system has not been validated experimentally (pp. 10459–10460). That acknowledgment deserves weight. Agreement for separate components supports proceeding to an integrated experiment; it leaves open whether the coupled apparatus will maintain the predicted temperatures, flows, and performance during operation.

The system boundary makes that distinction consequential. The model supplies the membrane’s permeate side with water at 293.15 K, or 20°C, while seawater enters at approximately 29.6°C (p. 10455). The economic model identifies pump electricity, but it does not establish how the colder stream is continuously supplied or restored. A suitable external source might make that condition practical. If cooling equipment is required, its energy and cost would belong in the assessment. The issue is therefore an unresolved site requirement, rather than proof that the design cannot work. Similarly, neglecting fouling, corrosion, salt deposition, and heat loss is understandable for an initial calculation. Those exclusions become harder to carry into a commercial conclusion, particularly when seawater passes through cooling equipment. The paper develops the idealized transport calculations at length while postponing several conditions that could determine actual service performance.

The economic conclusion has a more immediate problem: its scale is difficult to reconcile with the paper’s own inputs. The maximum predicted production is 6.13 kg of water per day, approximately 0.00613 m³. The paper prints the water-price parameter as 2.74 m³/h, which is dimensionally unsuitable for a price (p. 10460). If the intended value is SGD2.74/m³, maximum production would yield only about SGD6.13 annually, even allowing 365 days of operation. Table 2 lists a pump price of SGD950. If that applies to the additional permeate pump, the stated capital multiplier of 6.32 would make its estimated capital contribution approximately SGD6,004. Pump maintenance at the stated 1.092% rate would itself exceed the maximum annual water value, before electricity and membrane costs. This is my arithmetic check, conditional on the intended tariff unit. It does not identify the precise implementation error, but it makes the reported 3.72-year payback untenable without a correction or an unreported source of benefit.

The nearly constant payback across the operating range does little to resolve that discrepancy. The authors tentatively attribute its stability to the small loads and membrane areas studied (p. 10465). Yet weak variation within a model cannot establish robustness to uncertain assumptions. Water valuation, additional pumping requirements, maintenance, and capital estimation are precisely the assumptions that need examination. The comparison with stand-alone immersion cooling is a sensible way to isolate the incremental desalination investment. It also narrows what can be concluded: even a sound favorable result would establish attractiveness against that comparator under specified conditions. It would leave comparative claims about other heat-recovery or water-supply options unresolved.

The exergy analysis introduces a second problem that reaches directly into the optimization. Equation (24), defining exergy efficiency, repeats the expression given for the gain-output ratio in Equation (22): latent heat associated with water production divided by data-center heat input (p. 10459). That expression compares energy quantities. It does not specify the useful-exergy output and exergy input expected of a second-law efficiency. Moreover, the results describe different membrane-area behavior for these supposedly identical expressions. A printing error is possible. The published account nevertheless leaves the reader unable to determine which formulation produced the reported exergy efficiencies. Because exergy efficiency is a central optimization objective, this ambiguity affects the selected design as well as the terminology.

The exclusion of chemical exergy also needs a stronger justification. The authors explain that the chemical components of the streams do not change. Desalination, however, separates constituents and changes their concentrations without requiring a chemical reaction. The absence of new chemical species does not establish that compositional exergy is irrelevant. An analysis restricted to thermal and mechanical effects could still answer a useful question, provided that restriction governed its interpretation. Here, the paper uses the results to discuss overall efficiency and a sustainability index. That index measures a particular relationship between exergy supply and destruction. Its agreement with the efficiency trend supplies no independent evidence about broader sustainability outcomes such as material demands, concentrate management, or lifetime performance. The Sustainable Development Goals framing establishes relevance to energy and water; it cannot establish net benefit.

The preferred operating point is consequently much more conditional than its numerical precision suggests. Equal weighting of normalized cost and efficiency objectives is an explicit decision rule, but the paper offers little reason why it represents an operator’s priorities. The choice near 5 cm² of membrane area also favors a configuration producing much less water than the 75 cm² configuration highlighted for maximum output. That could be reasonable, but it raises the question of what service the system is meant to deliver. A minimum water requirement could change the preferred area. A fixed computing workload could change the optimization more fundamentally: reducing the cooling load may mean serving less computation, rather than improving the same service. The reported 1.1 kW operating point therefore requires an account of which demands are adjustable and which must be met.

The authors’ framing makes sense within their research setting. Their earlier immersion-cooling experiments, existing equipment, and experience with exergy-cost methods give them both expertise and a reason to pursue this particular combination. The repeated emphasis on novelty and a unique cost matrix reflects an academic audience that rewards new configurations and analytical methods. The acknowledged institutional funding provides context, but no basis for alleging that it dictated the findings. My own assessment gives more weight to reproducible accounting and clearly specified service requirements than to configuration novelty. That preference could undervalue an early conceptual study if applied as a demand for deployment readiness. Here, however, the paper itself claims economic feasibility. I accept its recommendation to conduct a controlled integrated experiment. I do not accept the reported payback or optimal design as established. Its strongest contribution is a testable engineering proposal; the practical endorsement remains to be earned.
