## A. Executive Summary / abstract-like claims

All A items occur on PDF page 2, printed page 2, under Executive Summary.

- **A01.** Accelerating U.S. AI data center development raises the stakes for water, energy, and land. Community pushback highlights the need for clean power, greater water efficiency, and fair, affordable development that protects communities and natural resources.
- **A02.** Although companies typically focus on water used for cooling inside data centers, water required to generate their electricity has an even greater impact; energy production is the largest and least visible share of overall data center water use.
- **A03.** The report examines electricity-related water requirements in Virginia, Texas, California, Illinois, Georgia, Ohio, and Arizona, which account for about 50% of U.S. data centers.
- **A04.** Much of these states' electricity comes from water-intensive generation, and many plants operate in regions exposed to water stress or drought.
- **A05.** This dependence creates risks for companies, investors, and communities: water shortages, drought restrictions, and competing demands can affect generation, raise costs, complicate infrastructure development, and strain supplies for residents, farmers, and businesses.
- **A06.** Companies can assess these risks more fully and work with producers, policymakers, and other stakeholders to mitigate them; electricity-generation water use should be included in data center policy discussions.
- **A07.** Addressing these risks and other buildout impacts offers data center companies and power producers a major opportunity to advance responsible U.S. AI development. Sustainable solutions and policies can support financial returns, business continuity, and national competitiveness.
- **A08.** The analysis uses the most recent publicly available 2024 EIA plant-level data, supplements missing reported water data with modeled estimates, and estimates withdrawals rather than consumption, including hydropower.

## S. Section and subsection conclusions, supporting claims, and scope conditions

### Background — PDF pages 5–6, printed pages 4–5

#### Rising Electricity Demand from Data Centers — PDF page 5

- **S01.** AI expansion drives rapid data center growth and electricity demand. U.S. data center capacity is expected to nearly double; electricity use is projected to reach 9%–17% of national demand by 2030, compared with 4%–5% today.
- **S02.** Growth increases both direct cooling-water needs and indirect water needs at power plants. The report calls indirect water the largest and least visible share of overall data center water use.
- **S03.** U.S. data centers used roughly 176 TWh of electricity in 2023, carrying an estimated 211 billion gallons of indirect water dependency based on regional grid mixes.
- **S04.** By 2030, electricity generation is projected to account for 72% of total U.S. data center **water consumption**, even as facilities improve cooling efficiency and use reclaimed water internally. This is a background projection, not the report's 2024 seven-state withdrawal estimate.

#### Increasing Data Center Water Risk — PDF page 5

- **S05.** Ceres' 2025 *Drained by Data* analysis projects a 400% increase in annual water use associated with data center electricity consumption around Phoenix as planned facilities come online.
- **S06.** Data centers can face compounded risk when both their own locations and the regions supplying their water-intensive electricity are already water stressed.
- **S07.** Roughly two-thirds of new or planned U.S. data centers in 2022–2025 were in water-stressed areas of states including California, Arizona, Texas, Illinois, and Virginia. Nine in ten data centers have another within five miles, concentrating pressure on shared resources and scrutiny from communities, regulators, and utilities.
- **S08.** Water-related power disruptions and community concerns can constrain operations. Some coal and nuclear plants have already curtailed generation or shut down because of water strain, while drought has reduced hydropower output.
- **S09.** Local opposition over water and grid capacity disrupted $130 billion in data center projects in Q1 2026; concern about local effects has led to state growth moratoriums and is shaping energy/water policy, permitting, resource-use requirements, and best practices.

#### Evaluating Water Risk from Power Generation — PDF pages 5–6

- **S10.** Assessing indirect water risk requires examining connected power plants' generation technologies, cooling systems, fuels, water sources, and seasonal demand, alongside local basin stress and drought.
- **S11.** Thermal electricity generation uses significant water, especially for cooling, and cooling technology strongly determines water use. Once-through cooling withdraws large volumes and returns most at higher temperatures; recirculating towers/ponds withdraw less but lose more to evaporation; dry cooling uses air to minimize water use.
- **S12.** Wind and solar require little or no operational water; hydropower depends on reservoir water and can involve significant evaporative losses.
- **S13.** National data center electricity supplies remain heavily dependent on water-using generation: natural gas 40%, renewables 24%, nuclear 20%, coal 15%. Although 65% of U.S. renewable PPAs signed since 2021 have been dedicated to data centers, renewable options are often constrained by timing and grid transmission/distribution issues. These constraints qualify the availability of lower-water alternatives.

### Study Area: U.S. Data Center Hotspots — PDF page 7, printed page 6

- **S14.** Purchased electricity can be a main driver of water dependency and impacts. The report therefore examines embedded water and production-region water stress in the seven named states, described here as hosting approximately 50% of U.S. data centers. The states span different market-growth projections, generation sources, water-stress levels, and drought conditions. The Appendix describes the corresponding share as about 50% of data center **capacity**; retain the report's location-specific wording.

### Data Analysis — PDF page 7, printed page 6

- **S15.** Five steps underpin the analysis: identify active plants of at least 1 MW (3,907) and the freshwater-dependent subset (1,434), excluding saltwater/brackish generation from water-use analysis; aggregate reported water data and WRI-based estimates; overlay plant locations with WRI Aqueduct and U.S. Drought Monitor data, assuming local withdrawals; attribute state withdrawals using EPRI's 2024 state data center electricity shares, assuming in-state electricity sourcing; and review public data center and producer disclosures about indirect dependency and growth-related water risk.
- **S16.** Withdrawals, rather than consumption, capture the full water volume on which an asset depends and are a strong indicator of risk exposure. Findings are directional estimates of scale and direction, not exact accounting. Water-source details and seasonal electricity-demand variability, though important for full risk assessment, are excluded, as are behind-the-meter generation (which may use water) and water use elsewhere in the value chain. Appendix details further qualify the findings (M01–M11).

### Key Findings — PDF pages 8–16, printed pages 7–15

#### Finding 1: Power generation makes up the bulk of data center water use — PDF pages 8–9

- **S17.** Applying EPRI state electricity-use shares, 2024 power-generation withdrawals attributable to study-area data centers total an estimated 3.4 trillion gallons, about 12 times combined annual water use in Los Angeles, Phoenix, and Washington, D.C. State estimates range from 25 billion gallons in Ohio to 1.4 trillion in California.
- **S18.** California's attributed water use is about ten times Los Angeles' annual use; Virginia's is roughly 21 times Washington, D.C.'s. The accompanying comparison also shows Arizona at five times Phoenix (F02).
- **S19.** Embedded withdrawals **could** rise to 4.1–7.6 trillion gallons annually by 2030 under EPRI's growth scenarios, which reflect projected electricity demand, industry growth, AI adoption, and data center efficiency. These are scenario estimates applied to this analysis's power-plant withdrawals, not a single certain forecast (F03).

#### Finding 2: The amount of water needed to produce electricity varies widely by state, depending on generation type — PDF pages 10–11

- **S20.** Greater grid reliance on thermal generation or hydropower means greater freshwater requirements and indirect data center water dependency. Water-using plants produce 78% of study-area electricity; Ohio, Georgia, and Virginia each derive over 90% from water-intensive thermal plants.
- **S21.** In 2024, 1,434 of 3,907 active plants (37%) depended on freshwater for generation and produced 1,142 TWh of the study area's 1,467 TWh (almost 78%). The remaining 63% of plants—solar, wind, battery storage, and thermal plants using saltwater—generated approximately 325 TWh (22%). These percentages refer to different denominators: plants versus electricity output.
- **S22.** Thermal generation, including gas, coal, nuclear, and some geothermal, biomass, and petroleum, dominates; the text gives a 57% share for California and 95% for Ohio. Figure 3 distinguishes freshwater/reclaimed thermal from saltwater/brackish thermal (F04).
- **S23.** Generation mix drives state withdrawal differences: approximately 1.6 trillion gallons in Ohio, largely associated with coal and hydroelectric fleets, versus 37.9 trillion in California, associated with its large hydroelectric fleet. Illinois relies primarily on nuclear energy (63%). Gas and coal account for roughly 90% of Texas' withdrawals; adding substantial low-water renewable generation has not eliminated its fossil-generation water dependence.
- **S24.** Hydropower accounts for approximately 78% of study-area withdrawals, especially in California, Arizona, and Georgia. It passes large volumes through turbines and returns them to the source; the measured dependency is therefore not equivalent to consumption.
- **S25.** Freshwater-using power plants across the seven states withdraw roughly 65 trillion gallons in total, approximately 227 times the three comparison cities' combined 2024 water use. This is total power-fleet withdrawal, not the 3.4 trillion gallons attributed to data centers (F05).

#### Finding 3: Most electricity is generated by plants in areas facing water stress or drought (or both) — PDF pages 12–13

- **S26.** Local context is critical. Ceres' earlier Phoenix analysis indicates data center growth could raise annual water stress by as much as 32% in basins hosting plants serving Phoenix-area data centers. Plant-location overlays reveal potentially compounded exposure where facilities operate or obtain power.
- **S27.** Across the seven states, 66% of electricity from water-using plants is generated in regions with medium-high to extremely high baseline annual water stress. At least 44% is generated in locations exposed to moderate drought (D1) for three months or more during the year. The report says approximately 32% of total seven-state generation comes from plants exposed to both stress and prolonged moderate drought. Preserve the text's stated denominators rather than substituting percentages of plants (F06).
- **S28.** Exposure differs substantially by state. California has 66% of generation in water-stressed basins but little drought exposure; Ohio has substantial drought exposure but relatively little baseline stress. Arizona has compounded exposure for 75% of generation. The finding highlights Arizona and Illinois as having the greatest exposure to stress and drought.
- **S29.** Drought and water stress vary seasonally and from year to year. California's 2021 drought and low reservoirs reduced statewide hydropower output; the 2024 exposure snapshot does not imply fixed future conditions.

#### California: Hydropower reliability risk — PDF page 14, left-hand case study

- **S30.** California is a leading existing/proposed data center market and major hydropower producer. Although renewable and relatively low in water consumption, hydropower can expose dependent data centers to water and electricity reliability risks. About 39% of California's water used for hydropower in 2024 occurs in medium-high to extremely high water-stress basins. Reservoir evaporation, drought, and storage/river-flow requirements can limit generation. PG&E's 2022 Integrated Resource Plan projects 15%–21% lower near-term hydro generation because of climate effects (warmer temperatures, lower snowpack, extreme droughts) and anticipated permitting changes reducing hydropower water allocations.

#### Illinois: Concentrated water risks related to nuclear energy — PDF page 14, right-hand case study

- **S30a.** Illinois is the most nuclear-dependent study state, with nuclear providing 63% of 2024 generation. Much capacity is concentrated in the northwest near water-stressed portions of the Mississippi–Missouri basin; 75% of Illinois nuclear generation comes from plants in high-water-stress areas.
- **S30b.** Support for new nuclear capacity is increasing despite existing exposure: Illinois lifted its large-reactor moratorium in January 2026; the White House seeks 400 GW nationally over 25 years, including 5 GW of new capacity and higher existing-facility generation by 2030. Some advanced reactors reduce or avoid water-intensive cooling, but most existing and planned nuclear facilities still depend on water. Future nuclear development should therefore account for water risk.

#### Finding 4: Most power producers identify data centers as a primary demand driver, but few consider the associated water risks — PDF page 15

- **S31.** Some producers already have water-management practices that could provide a foundation for addressing data center demand. Vistra discloses assets in high/extremely high water stress; SRP targets a 30% reduction in generation-related water-use intensity by 2035; Dominion met a 50% withdrawal-reduction target in 2023 and prioritizes replacing coal/oil with solar/wind.
- **S32.** Technology and generation changes also reduce withdrawals: PG&E uses dry cooling at some gas plants; Southern California Edison uses less water-intensive simple-cycle turbines; NRG reduced generation-related withdrawals by 77% through cooling towers and a coal-to-gas transition.
- **S33.** Most large study-area producers assessed via sustainability reports, integrated resource plans, and CDP disclosures recognize data centers as major load-growth drivers, but few evaluate the linked water risks or develop management strategies specifically for the added demand.
- **S34.** AEP anticipates approximately 12 GW of new contracted load in 2026–2030, driven by data centers, and is investing $5.2 billion in transmission/distribution while exploring fuel cells; its disclosures do not address increased water reliance. Dominion reports 81 new Northern Virginia data center projects in 2020–2024 and $65 billion of gas/solar/wind investment through 2030, but readily available disclosures do not explain effects of continued data center growth on water use.
- **S35.** Broader planning **may** capture some data center-related water demand: SRP includes water alongside reliability, affordability, and emissions in energy scenarios and plans for drought with partners. APS uses long-term supply planning, retirement of water-intensive units, reclaimed water, and regional engagement. The report allows for these practices rather than claiming all producers ignore water.

#### Finding 5: Most data center companies do not account for purchased-electricity water risks — PDF page 16

- **S36.** Operator strategies primarily focus on internal cooling and equipment; Amazon, Microsoft, and Apple increasingly set direct-water targets. Purchased-electricity water disclosures are much more limited and most efforts do not clearly connect to risk mitigation, although a few companies have begun addressing embedded water.
- **S37.** Meta discloses total embedded water **consumption** in purchased electricity, including contracted renewable energy, and avoided consumption associated with procurement decisions.
- **S38.** CyrusOne considers on-site water consumption (WUE Site) and electricity-production water (WUE Source), estimated with WRI guidance. Its WUE Source has fallen 67% since 2018, primarily from renewable procurement, while it pursues an SBTi-validated 2030 climate-neutral target.
- **S39.** Oracle announced replacing planned gas turbines/grid dependence at Project Jupiter in New Mexico with Bloom Energy fuel cells. The technology requires neither a steam cycle nor cooling towers and is **expected** to minimize water use compared with the originally planned gas turbines; this is an announced change and expected benefit.

### Recommended Actions — PDF pages 17–18, printed pages 16–17

- **R00 — Section conclusion/premise:** Concrete actions by data center buyers, power producers, policymakers, and investors can help mitigate electricity-embedded water risks. The following are the report's prescriptions, not findings that all actors already implement them.

#### Data center companies — PDF page 17 (six bullets)

- **R01.** Integrate direct and purchased-electricity water into risk assessments under current/future climate, demand-growth, and energy-mix scenarios; include site water sources, seasonal supply/demand, and infrastructure needs. Comprehensive assessment supports evaluation of dependencies, impacts, and risks and more effective mitigation.
- **R02.** Disclose direct and electricity-embedded water and associated risks for each existing/proposed site, or at city/county/basin level if data limitations require. Explain mitigation and how risk affects siting, cooling, and procurement. Transparency helps establish trust with shareholders, communities, and regulators.
- **R03.** Evaluate PPAs, renewable purchases, and behind-the-meter investments for their water-risk effects, including water availability and electricity reliability, to understand renewable procurement's water benefits and inform decisions.
- **R04.** Work with producers to understand stress/drought in electricity-supplying regions and support existing efforts to address vulnerabilities.
- **R05.** Expand precompetitive peer collaboration on sector-wide risk mitigation, best practices, shared learning, industry data, barriers to action, and standards.
- **R06.** Work across peers, producers, researchers, and other sectors on a standardized methodology and database for embedded-water disclosure and assessment, including operational risks and local impacts, to provide a scientific, data-driven approach accessible to all stakeholders.

#### Power producers — PDF pages 17–18 (three bullets)

- **R07.** Disclose plant-level withdrawals, consumption, water sources, drought vulnerability, basin risk, generation-related water targets, and mitigation measures, to clarify vulnerabilities and support customers concerned about value-chain risk.
- **R08.** Integrate water risks associated with data center growth into resource planning and infrastructure decisions, including more efficient cooling technology, to strengthen water resilience and long-term business continuity.
- **R09.** Collaborate more with customers, water managers, and community stakeholders to adapt to changing water supply and growing power demand.

#### Policymakers — PDF page 18 (five bullets)

- **R10.** Require standardized direct and electricity-embedded water reporting for consistent, accessible information on risks and impacts.
- **R11.** Incentivize lower-water technologies and conservation to scale savings and sustain resilient supplies for all users.
- **R12.** Incentivize deployment of low-water-intensity renewable energy for more sustainable, resilient data centers and other customers.
- **R13.** Account for basin stress, seasonal supply/demand, and community impacts in siting, permitting, infrastructure, and resource planning so community and other water needs are met now and in the future.
- **R14.** Ensure behind-the-meter generation minimizes local freshwater impacts, particularly in stressed regions, so the trend does not create unintended water consequences.

#### Investors — PDF page 18 (three bullets)

- **R15.** Encourage comprehensive producer and operator disclosure of value-chain water risks to inform investment decisions.
- **R16.** Encourage operators to demonstrate how supplier-region water stress/drought affects procurement and siting, allowing assessment of whether vulnerabilities are addressed and risks mitigated.
- **R17.** Engage operators and producers about adequate accounting and mitigation to support long-term value creation. The Ceres Valuing Water Finance Initiative offers a platform and tools for this engagement.

### Appendix: Data Sources and Methodology — PDF pages 19–20, printed pages 18–19

These claims and limitations qualify all numerical findings; they are included in the inventory's endpoint.

#### Scope and power plant selection — PDF page 19

- **M01.** The seven states account for roughly 50% of U.S. data center capacity. EIA 860/923 plant- and generator-level 2024 filings identify 5,230 plants with at least 1 MW capacity, of which 3,907 actively generated electricity. Water-use analysis retains 1,434 after excluding retired, zero/negative-net-generation, saltwater/brackish-cooled, and minimal/no-water plants such as wind/solar.
- **M02.** A plant with multiple generators or fuels is treated as one facility by unique EIA plant ID and classified by the technology with the largest 2024 generation share.

#### Water use data — PDF page 19

- **M03.** Reported EIA cooling-water values are used where available. Only 194 of 1,434 plants report withdrawals or consumption under EIA-923 Schedule 8D; the remaining 1,240—including smaller thermal plants, hydroelectric facilities, and plants outside reporting requirements—are estimated by applying WRI technology-specific water-intensity factors to 2024 net generation.
- **M04.** The WRI method estimates withdrawals across technologies and treats hydropower as a withdrawal rather than instream use. This convention is integral to the large hydroelectric withdrawal totals.

#### Attributing water use to data centers — PDF page 19

- **M05.** EPRI's 2024 state data center electricity-use shares are applied proportionally to each state's total reported-plus-estimated power-plant withdrawals to attribute water to in-state data centers. EPRI 2030 low, medium, high, and higher growth scenarios are used for projections. This is proportional state attribution, not tracing a facility's actual power purchases.

#### Water risk characterization — PDF page 19

- **M06.** Plant coordinates are mapped to WRI Aqueduct 4.0 baseline annual watershed stress and to U.S. Drought Monitor 2024 weekly drought categories. All 53 collection periods are combined in a multiband raster to identify sites with at least three months of moderate drought (D1). Locations with both medium-high through extremely high water stress and moderate-or-greater drought in 2024 are identified as having compounded exposure; the body/figure specifies the three-month drought threshold.

#### Limitations — PDF pages 19–20

- **M07.** Estimates are directional, not precise measurements. Water data are self-reported to EIA and not independently verified. National-average WRI factors do not reflect an individual plant's age, cooling design, climate, or efficiency.
- **M08.** A significant portion of the analysis is modeled because many small plants and hydroelectric facilities need not report water. Arizona, Texas, and Illinois, with larger thermal fleets, rely more on reported points; California and Virginia, with more dams and small plants, rely more on estimated values.
- **M09.** Water sources are assumed colocated with plants. Although withdrawals, stress, and drought change across seasons/years, the study uses only 2024 to illustrate indirect-water scale, direction, and potential risk.
- **M10.** Actual delivered electricity reflects plant dispatch and balancing-authority boundaries, which cross state lines or cover only part of a state: PJM covers 13 states including Virginia's data center corridor; ERCOT covers most but not all Texas. State-level results approximate exposure across a state's fleet and are not precise delivered mixes or water quantities for particular facilities or utility territories.
- **M11.** Attribution follows electricity generation location rather than ultimate consumption. Water use may be overstated in net-exporting states and understated in importing states such as Virginia and California that rely partly on out-of-state generation.
