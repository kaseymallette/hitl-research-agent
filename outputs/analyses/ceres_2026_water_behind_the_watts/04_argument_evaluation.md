Source title: Water Behind the Watts: The Hidden Risk of Powering Data Centers
Source type: other
Publisher: Ceres
DOI: null

Ceres’s August 2026 report makes a persuasive case that data center water assessments should extend upstream to electricity generation. It makes a less secure case about the magnitude of the resulting community impacts and the superiority of particular remedies. Its strongest contribution is to identify a consequential dependency that facility-level accounting can miss. Its central weakness is that withdrawal volume, geographical exposure, operational vulnerability, and damage to other water users sometimes carry one another’s argumentative weight, although they require different evidence.

The publication setting helps explain this pattern. This is an advocacy organization’s research report directed toward companies, investors, and policymakers. Ceres explicitly describes its mission on the final page as promoting a cleaner, more just, resilient economy through corporate action and policy. The executive summary already establishes clean energy, efficiency, fairness, and resource protection as standards for development; the concluding recommendations assign responsibilities to actors able to advance them. The report therefore seeks to persuade readers to change decisions, as well as inform them. Its investor vocabulary connects environmental protection to business continuity and returns, making sustainability intelligible within the audience’s existing priorities. That alignment is a proposed outcome, however, rather than something its analysis establishes for every intervention.

The argument proceeds through four premises: data center electricity demand is growing; much existing generation depends on water; substantial generation occurs in stressed or drought-affected places; and corporate attention inadequately captures that dependence. Together these support extending assessment, disclosure, and planning beyond data center premises. The report develops the middle premises most fully through plant inventories, generation mixes, withdrawal estimates, and spatial overlays. Demand growth comes largely from external projections, while institutional preparedness receives a shorter review of public disclosures. The recommendations are announced before this evidential work begins. That structure suits an action-oriented report, but makes it especially important to distinguish findings that substantiate the opening position from findings that merely fit it.

The physical dependency argument is convincing. The discussion of cooling systems on printed page 5 explains why electricity production can expose a facility to water conditions elsewhere. Once-through systems need large flows, recirculating systems withdraw less but evaporate more, and dry cooling changes the dependence. These mechanisms make the concern more than an inference from geographic coincidence. The California case study supplies an especially useful link between mechanism and consequence: PG&E reduced its near-term hydropower forecast by 15–21% in response to climatic and anticipated permitting constraints. This does not quantify data center outages, but establishes that water conditions can change suppliers’ planning even when generation consumes relatively little water. The conclusion that indirect dependence deserves assessment is earned here.

The headline withdrawal figures require greater restraint. The report attributes approximately 3.4 trillion gallons annually to data center electricity in seven states and compares the volume with municipal water use. Yet approximately 78% of withdrawals across the studied power fleet are attributed to hydropower, whose turbine flows return to the source (printed page 10). The appendix explicitly treats hydropower as withdrawal rather than instream use. Counting that water is defensible for describing dependence: generation cannot occur without sufficient flow. It does not make each gallon equivalent to municipal demand in its consequences for competing users. Water can support generation and remain available downstream. Conversely, consumption and warmed return flows create effects not captured by gross volume alone.

Ceres acknowledges these distinctions, so the problem is less concealment than emphasis. The city comparisons make an abstract total vivid while inviting readers to interpret it as water taken away from communities. The executive summary’s assertion that electricity-related water use has a greater impact than facility cooling goes beyond what the report’s withdrawal accounting alone establishes. Its background cites external consumption estimates, but its own seven-state analysis does not provide a comparable direct-use inventory demonstrating relative harm. The comparison would be stronger if withdrawals, consumptive losses, and reliability dependence were presented separately. The present total establishes scale under an accounting convention, not the scale of depletion or social damage.

Attribution introduces a second limit. The study applies data centers’ state-level electricity-use shares to withdrawals by power plants within each state. This is a transparent screening approximation, but it does not trace electricity actually delivered to facilities. The appendix recognizes the problem: PJM crosses thirteen states, and importing states such as Virginia and California may have understated attributed water use. More fundamentally, allocating a share of existing withdrawals is different from estimating the additional withdrawals caused by another data center. That requires knowing which plants respond to new demand, when they operate, and what new capacity or procurement changes follow. The 2030 scenarios show what the adopted framework yields under alternative demand-growth assumptions; they do not independently establish the future dispatch and technology changes that determine water outcomes. This distinction matters because the report’s policy concern is largely the consequences of expansion.

The spatial analysis likewise establishes a reason to investigate, rather than a measured probability of failure. Finding that 66% of water-using generation occurs in medium-high to extremely high stress areas and 44% in locations with prolonged drought is significant. But plant coordinates are assumed to represent water-source locations, and annual exposure does not establish whether scarcity coincided with peak production or exceeded a plant’s operating tolerance. Contracts, reclaimed supplies, cooling designs, storage, and alternative generation can change vulnerability. Only 194 of the 1,434 plants reported withdrawal or consumption data; the rest use technology-based estimates. That plant-count ratio does not reveal the proportion of volume directly reported, but it does show why apparent precision needs caution. The candid limitations strengthen the report’s credibility while narrowing what its percentages can establish.

The organizational argument is weaker still where it moves from disclosure to internal attention. AEP and Dominion publicly discuss major data center load growth without corresponding discussion of water implications. This demonstrates an accountability gap. It does not establish that they have failed to consider those implications internally. Indeed, the report concedes that broader planning at SRP and APS may capture data center growth without a dedicated strategy. Similarly, limited operator disclosures cannot by themselves establish the headline claim that most operators do not account for electricity-related water risks. A defined company sample and explicit assessment criteria would make the prevalence claims more persuasive. As presented, the evidence strongly supports requests for clearer disclosure and only tentatively supports a diagnosis of managerial neglect.

The preferred remedies deserve the same differentiation. Better source-specific assessment, disclosure of both withdrawals and consumption, and coordination with water managers follow closely from the identified blind spots. Incentives for low-water renewables are plausible but need an additional comparison of alternatives. The report acknowledges transmission and timing constraints, yet does not evaluate complete supply arrangements providing comparable reliability. Nor does a reduction in withdrawal volume necessarily establish lower basin depletion: its own cooling discussion shows why evaporation matters. Dry cooling, reclaimed water, different generation portfolios, and demand management could reasonably emerge as preferred responses in different settings. Ceres’s evidence supports making water a decision criterion; it does not establish a universal technology ranking or the optimal incentive design.

My assessment favors distinctions between exposure, causation, and realized harm. That preference could underweight preventive action where waiting for precise damages would be costly. Ceres reasonably treats plausible vulnerability as grounds to investigate before expansion locks in infrastructure. I accept that judgment because the mechanisms are credible, the California example supplies practical corroboration, and information gathering does not require proof of imminent failure. I am less persuaded when precaution becomes an implicit warrant for particular investments. Ceres’s sustainability mission helps explain that emphasis without invalidating its evidence. The report succeeds as an agenda for broader water-risk assessment. Its most defensible conclusion is that electricity-related water dependence must inform decisions; determining which projects harm communities, and which remedies best reduce that harm, requires the more specific analysis it ultimately recommends.
