## Summary claims (the report's abstract equivalent)

Each entry is located in the Summary, PDF p. 2; main-text counterparts are indicated.

- **A1. Growth and concern:** Expanding AI use and other computing trends have increased data-center development; increased facility numbers and size have raised concerns about operational resources, including water use, consumption, and discharge, and have drawn congressional attention. Members and constituents may ask about impacts on local water quantity and quality. See S1.
- **A2. Function:** A data center is a physical facility housing and running large computer systems; its equipment processes, stores, manages, and disseminates data to support IT services. See S2.
- **A3. Heat and regulation:** Computing and networking devices generate heat; thermal regulation removes excess heat and maintains function through airflow, cooling, and other air-conditioning management. See S3.
- **A4. Cooling choices:** Cooling generally uses air or liquid; liquid-cooled systems generally use water to transfer heat. Facilities may use several technologies depending on size, computing load, location, climate, equipment needs, and water availability/access. See S3–S4.
- **A5. Local effects:** Effects of withdrawals and consumption depend on local/regional water conditions and supply options and other water demands. Consumption means withdrawn water no longer available for reuse. See S4, S9.
- **A6. Sources:** Most operators currently acquire water from municipal/public water systems; other potential sources are treated wastewater/reclaimed water and direct surface-water or groundwater withdrawals. See S5, S9.
- **A7. Allocation authority:** Congress generally defers to states' primacy over intrastate allocation, and state laws/regulations largely govern freshwater access within a state. See S8.
- **A8. Federal infrastructure:** Despite that deference, federal investment through USACE and Reclamation has developed water resources. Their facilities store water for various purposes potentially including data centers; some data centers may indirectly use such water through public systems or communities with federal agreements. See S10.
- **A9. Discharge types:** Data centers may generate wastewater and stormwater; development may also discharge dredged/fill material into wetlands or streams. See S17–S20.
- **A10. Federal requirements:** Developers and facility owners/operators may need federal approvals or Clean Water Act permits intended to prevent or minimize water-quality effects. See S22–S28.
- **A11. Pollutant variability:** Wastewater pollutants depend on source-water quality, cooling technology, site operations, and whether discharge comes from normal operation or construction/commissioning. See S21.
- **A12. Wastewater destination:** Data centers currently most commonly send wastewater to publicly owned treatment works. See S26.
- **A13. Legislation:** Some Members introduced 119th-Congress bills, identified in the report's table, to improve water-use information, incentivize practices such as reuse, or limit/facilitate construction. See S29 and T1.

### S1. Introduction (pp. 1–2)

- Internet growth and computationally intensive activity, including AI and cryptocurrency, have increased data-center scale and incidence. Water concerns include both local supply constraints and effects of wastewater on treatment and receiving ecosystems.
- Direct facility water use excludes off-site electricity generation and upstream manufacture/building-material water use. The report cites an estimate that direct use/consumption accounts for approximately 2% of total U.S. water consumption; a relatively small national share does not resolve community concerns.
- An IEA estimate equates a 100-MW U.S. data center's direct daily consumption to roughly 2,600 households, averaged across cooling strategies. Footnote 4 explains the derivation: 40% direct and 60% indirect of a total equivalent to 6,500 households; the report's direct-use comparison does not include the latter.
- Municipal/public systems are estimated to supply 97% of water provided to U.S. data centers, with a few facilities self-sourcing surface water or groundwater.
- Comprehensive quantification is difficult and understanding of local effects is evolving. Bills seek better information, incentives, or support/limits on development. The report concerns **direct** water use; indirect use is beyond scope.

### S2. What Are Data Centers? (p. 2)

- Data centers support varied IT services through equipment for processing, storage, management, and dissemination of data. They range from server closets to entire buildings, generally need dedicated uninterruptible power and cooling, and larger stand-alone facilities may have backup generation.
- Services include traditional and on-demand enterprise IT, high-performance computing, internet, and hosting. Processor choices vary with tasks: CPUs for general workloads, GPUs primarily for AI training, and ASICs for particular applications, frequently cryptocurrency and increasingly AI. Facilities include thermal-regulation systems as well as server racks.

### Data Center Water Use (parent section, pp. 2–4)

No separate concluding prose; its answers are S3–S4.

### S3. How Do Data Centers Use Water? (pp. 2–3)

- Some thermal-regulation systems use water to remove computing/networking heat; on-site generation, where present, may also need water for thermal regulation.
- Air cooling transfers heat using chilled air, liquid cooling using liquids; hybrid systems combine technologies, and two-phase systems use liquid/vapor changes. Small/medium or co-located facilities typically use direct-expansion computer room air conditioners; larger facilities may use other systems.
- AI training and inference increase cooling needs. Some firms plan liquid-cooling retrofits; these can be more efficient than air cooling but may not be cost-effective for smaller facilities. Immersion and two-phase cooling are also being explored.
- Water requirements vary with technology and facility size, load, climate, and water availability. Overall water use is increasing: one study estimates U.S. data centers directly used approximately **17 billion gallons in 2023**, versus **5.6 billion gallons in 2014**.

### S4. What Are Some Potential Options to Reduce Water Used by Data Centers? (pp. 3–4)

- Water use effectiveness (WUE) is typically facility water consumption divided by IT-equipment electricity demand. Consumption permanently removes withdrawn water from its original source through evaporation or other irreversible processes, such as deep-well wastewater injection. Ordinary WUE measures direct facility use, excluding off-site electricity-generation water.
- A combined direct/indirect WUE may be more comprehensive and some consider it more appropriate, but different electricity sources complicate comparisons across cooling systems.
- Technologies use different amounts of water for equal cooling. Evaporation effectively dissipates IT heat; water-cooled chillers generally have the highest WUE (least water-efficient) largely because of cooling towers.
- Waterside economizers may reduce mechanical chiller/compressor use and WUE; airside economizers may allow chilled-water shutdown in suitable weather. Operational changes such as higher temperature setpoints may further reduce water use.
- Water/energy trade-offs remain: evaporative systems generally use energy more efficiently than air-cooled or other waterless systems; air-cooled chillers use no water but comparatively more energy.

### Water Sources and Authorities (parent section, pp. 4–18)

No separate concluding prose; its answers are S5–S16.

### S5. What Are the Potential Water Sources Available for Data Centers? (pp. 4–5)

- Sources include municipal drinking-water systems, treated wastewater/reuse, and direct surface-water or groundwater withdrawals. One analysis estimates public systems supplied **97% of on-site needs as of 2025**.
- Cooling requires appropriate water quality. Regulated public-system water generally suffices; SDWA disinfection/corrosivity controls may make it less damaging to cooling equipment than water causing corrosion, mineral scaling, or microbial growth. Self-sourced or reclaimed water may require pretreatment.
- Proximity matters. Access to treatment infrastructure and nonpotable “purple pipe” can enable reclaimed-water use, but competing demands may limit supply.
- Time to obtain permits and install self-supply infrastructure may influence source selection. Connecting to an existing public system may require fewer steps and less time.

### S6. What Drives a Data Center's Water Demand? (pp. 5–6)

- Facility size, cooling technology, and ambient temperature shape demand. Open-loop water cooling requires more water than closed-loop water cooling; evaporative demand generally rises during hotter hours and seasons.
- Incoming water quality affects how often water can cycle through cooling. Lower dissolved-solids concentrations may permit multiple cycles; evaporation concentrates solids until a threshold requires discharge.

### S7. Do Data Center Operators Pay to Use the Water from Public Water Systems? What Determines the Price They Pay? (pp. 6–7)

- Systems generally charge customers, including data centers, to cover service costs; rate and quantity generally determine payment. Municipal service agreements may specify volume, rates, and other terms and may restrict disclosure. Rates generally are not publicly available, though footnote 25 notes partial voluntary/state datasets.
- Rate structures vary: uniform per-unit rates, flat fees, increasing blocks encouraging conservation, and decreasing blocks potentially encouraging industries by discounting high-volume use. Commercial and residential rates may differ. These arrangements have implications for communities adding large users.
- PUC/PSC authority varies by state. Most states regulate certain private water systems; exceptions named are Georgia, Michigan, Minnesota, North Dakota, and South Dakota. Some regulate public systems as well; Wisconsin regulates all systems' rates, and nine other listed states have varying public-system jurisdiction.
- Regulators may apply full cost recovery, generally with investor returns where relevant. Recovery of operation, maintenance, and capital costs is intended to sustain service.

### S8. Why Are Local Water Authorities and the States the Main Entities Determining If Water Is Available for Data Centers? (pp. 7–9)

- Congress generally defers to state primacy in intrastate allocation; the Water Supply Act of 1958 recognizes state/local primary responsibilities. This is a policy of deference, not a denial of potential constitutional federal powers (footnote 34 also identifies federal public-domain and reserved rights).
- State water-access regimes differ among states and between surface water and groundwater. Directly self-sourcing data centers address these requirements themselves; otherwise their water provider does so. Footnote 36 describes riparian, prior-appropriation, and hybrid regimes and differing permit/reporting requirements.
- Municipalities/providers largely develop and distribute supplies; federal agencies offer authorized financial/technical assistance and protect drinking water and fish/wildlife resources.
- State law often governs utility authority and duties. Utilities may have a duty to serve if capacity, rights, and other conditions are met; some may set terms or decline service for certain uses/reasons. Terms may have to be applied fairly to similarly situated customers.
- Federal deference coexists with federal investment: Reclamation/USACE infrastructure stores water for irrigation, municipal/industrial uses, and other purposes.

### S9. Are There U.S. Data Centers That Directly Source Their Water? (pp. 9–10)

- Most use municipal suppliers, but some reportedly use or propose groundwater as primary or backup supply. A proposed Richland Parish, Louisiana, facility plans Mississippi River Alluvial Aquifer cooling water. Footnote 41 distinguishes its registered ceiling (over 23 million gallons/day, 8.4 billion/year) from the company's projected actual use (500–600 million/year, about 1.5 million/day, after opening in 2028).
- Groundwater management is primarily state-controlled, with limited federal exceptions, chiefly drinking-water protection, and some federal support for nonfederal management/recharge.
- Comprehensive public source information is unavailable. CRS had not found sources identifying **currently operating U.S. commercial data centers self-sourcing surface water**; that does not establish their absence. Interest in nonmunicipal supply may grow with siting outside service areas and community responses to impacts; irrigation districts are one possible route.
- Footnote 44 illustrates possibilities and constraints: Marana prohibited municipal potable cooling water and a proposed campus expected irrigation-district nonpotable supply; an Imperial County developer's proposed agricultural-water conversion was denied by the irrigation district and followed by litigation.
- Local effects depend on quantity, quality, seasonal/weather availability, alternative supplies, competing household/industrial/agricultural/environmental demands, and coincidence of peak demand with other users or constrained supply. Direct withdrawals remain subject to state water law.

### S10. Are Federal Reservoirs and Infrastructure Providing Water to Data Centers? (pp. 10–11)

- Reclamation operates in 17 western states, commonly acquires state water rights, and contracts with irrigation districts/municipalities for project water. USACE generally treats water-supply storage as secondary to multipurpose dams and generally lacks project water rights for nonconsumptive storage; a requesting water-supply entity secures state rights.
- CRS research in early July 2026 identified **no direct data-center water-supply agreements** with Reclamation or USACE, but existing/planned centers may receive federal-project water indirectly through utilities/communities.
- Examples: paused Project Turbo near Gainesville planned city water primarily from USACE Lake Lanier; Amazon's Caddo/Bossier project planned Blanchard Utilities water from Caddo Lake's USACE-operated dam. Footnote 51 qualifies the latter: USACE operates the dam but lacks reservoir/real-estate authority, so Blanchard withdrawals need no USACE supply agreement/easement.
- Some Arizona facilities appear to receive Reclamation-project water indirectly. A 2026 study counted 66 completed/under-construction Central Arizona data centers, including Tucson 9, Phoenix 25, Scottsdale 5, and Mesa 8; all four cities receive significant Colorado River/Central Arizona Project supplies, and the latter three also Salt River Project supplies. Some developers may seek irrigation-to-municipal/industrial conversions.
- H.R. 9497, ordered reported in July 2026, would require an Army report within one year of enactment on new commercial/industrial users' impacts on USACE projects authorized for water supply/conservation; this could provide relevant information.

### S11. How Does and Can the Federal Government Support State and Local Water Supply Planning, Including for Data Centers? (p. 12)

- Public systems usually supply data centers from surface water or groundwater under state access rules; interstate compacts and river-basin bodies can also govern shared resources and interstate effects.
- Congress authorizes selected federal technical/planning support, including USACE water-resource/watershed planning and Reclamation drought planning/resilience in the 17 western states. Reclamation assistance has been limited to state/local governments and entities with water/power delivery authority; S. 3737 would broaden eligibility to include data centers.
- Some states undertake their own planning, but incorporation of recent data-center demands may be unclear. Broader federal planning existed before the mid-1980s.
- Congress established the Interagency Committee on Water Management and Infrastructure in 2022 to improve coordination and identify cross-agency research/coordination needs. Groups have advocated implementation as directed.

### S12. Box: Federal Water Planning Since 1965 (p. 13)

- Interest in national supply planning coexists with state concern over federal control. Congress has enacted no overarching water-policy legislation since the 1965 Water Resources Planning Act, which created the Water Resources Council (WRC).
- Federal/state/basin planning after 1965 drew both support and criticism over cost/usefulness; declining state-planning funds and early-1980s disbandment of WRC and most basin commissions shifted planning from coordinated watersheds toward individual projects, with exceptions and limited WRC re-formation in the mid-2010s.
- The last national water-resource conditions assessment was in 1975; the latest systematic comprehensive review of nationwide federal water policy was the National Water Commission's 1973 *Water Policies for the Future*. The commission existed from 1968 to 1973.
- Federal coordination has largely been ad hoc since 1983. The 2022 statutory interagency committee followed two informal years and is intended to coordinate overlapping federal responsibilities including storage, supply, infrastructure, and forecasting.

### S13. What Are the Available Data on Data Center Water Use? (pp. 13–14)

- Facility/sector usage data are limited. Utilities may collect/report data without making it public, and public-supply data typically aggregate all users, making individual effects hard to quantify. Self-suppliers often face fewer reporting requirements, depending on state/local law.
- States/localities may seek more data. Texas directed its PUC to survey data centers and virtual-currency mining facilities and report to the governor and Legislative Budget Board by the end of 2026.

### S14. What Is the Current Federal Role in Assessing Data Center Water Use and Its Effect on Water Resources? (pp. 14–16)

- The federal government has not conducted a systematic data-center water-use assessment. USGS has compiled national/state/county withdrawal estimates for about 70 years; data centers appear historically classified as commercial through 1995, after which separate commercial estimates ceased.
- Under Secure Water Act mandates, USGS developed AI/machine-learning models heavily dependent on state/local information and federal datasets. Its 2025 integrated assessment estimated 2010–2020 public-supply, irrigation, and thermoelectric-cooling surface/groundwater withdrawals monthly at subwatershed scale. Public-supply models separate domestic deliveries from combined commercial/industrial/institutional deliveries.
- A 2026 USGS dataset estimates 2020 self-supply industrial use at county/subwatershed scales, but its facilities are categorized as manufacturing, not an inventory of data centers.
- Federally supported Virginia Tech researchers' 2025 United States Water Withdrawals Database standardizes reported withdrawals/estimates across 42 states, including industrial/commercial/public use, but does not supply further specific facility information. USGS assessments supplement state reports with modeled gap-filling and exclude self-supply commercial use.
- USGS can partner on sector/regional impacts using cooperative matching funds. Congress funded an assessment of water bottling and resource effects ($2.75 million across the cited FY2021/2022 directives). Like data centers, bottling lacks a comprehensive inventory linked to volumes/sources; its study may be an analogue because a data-center study would likely face similar comprehensive-data challenges.

### S15. What Is Water Reuse? (p. 16)

- Reuse/recycling/reclamation captures wastewater, stormwater, saltwater, or graywater and treats it as needed for a beneficial purpose. Purposes include industrial/agricultural/landscape uses, replenishment, and drinking water; required treatment depends on use and health/environmental risk.
- Some data centers recycle on site or obtain nearby treatment-plant reclaimed water in response to freshwater concerns. EPA reports many operators want recycled cooling water but face an unclear permitting pathway for that use.

### S16. Does the Federal Government Have a Role in Regulating Water Reuse? (pp. 16–18)

- No federal laws/regulations govern water reuse as such; states primarily regulate resource allocation/development, through reuse-specific or existing frameworks. Requirements vary by recycled-water source/end use. As of February 2024, EPA data indicated 20 states had industrial-reuse guidance/regulations.
- EPA supports adoption and addresses technical/institutional/financial barriers with partners. The 2020 National Water Reuse Action Plan (WRAP) included 37 actions and over 200 milestones; REUSExplorer summarizes over 185 state regulations to clarify standards and help states develop guidance.
- IIJA Section 50218 directed EPA to establish an interagency working group coordinating reuse activities/resources through a national action plan.
- April 2026 WRAP 2.0 expands the initiative, emphasizing industry, technology, and energy. EPA expressly describes it as collaborative, **not a federal regulatory mandate**, recognizing state/local knowledge.
- Data-center actions include compiling industrial-cooling permitting best practices with states, addressing recycled-water regulatory hurdles, hosting a utility forum, establishing an AI recycled-water Center of Excellence, and supporting decentralized on-site reuse through BILD.

### S17. What Types of Water-Related Discharges Do Data Centers Generate? (pp. 18–19)

Data centers may generate wastewater and stormwater, and construction may entail dredged/fill discharges into wetlands or streams. The three nested answers are S18–S20.

### S18. Wastewater (pp. 18–19)

- Main streams are cooling-system effluent (predominant) and domestic wastewater (generally limited employee-facility flows). Cooling streams include noncontact cooling water, blowdown, construction/commissioning/maintenance discharges; source-water or wastewater treatment can add streams.
- Frequency and volume depend on cooling type, size/design, operation, location, and seasonal temperature/humidity, and differ between construction/commissioning and operation.
- Open-loop/evaporative systems typically produce more operational effluent; closed-loop systems may produce less during normal operation. Both produce maintenance/construction wastewater. Air systems may produce little/none; immersion/hybrid systems may reduce needs and effluent.
- Cleaning, degreasing, passivation, and flushing during commissioning may generate short-lived, high-volume flows with higher pollutant concentrations than routine operation. Multiphase development can repeat commissioning rather than generate only one initial event.

### S19. Stormwater (p. 19)

Runoff arises during construction and from operational industrial activities. Phased development may repeat construction runoff periods. Operational sources include generators, fuel/chemical tanks, outdoor equipment storage, and material loading areas.

### S20. Discharges of Dredged or Fill Material for Development (p. 19)

Some developments on parcels containing wetlands or surface waters may discharge dredged/fill material into those waters; this is conditional on the site and development activities.

### S21. What Types of Pollutants Do Data Centers Discharge in Their Wastewater? (p. 20)

- Pollutants depend on source water, cooling technology, site operations, and operational versus construction/commissioning stage.
- Evaporation concentrates source pollutants, commonly dissolved solids and potentially chlorine in chlorinated supplies. More cycles affect concentration; adding freshwater may dilute water to permit further cycles.
- Treatment chemicals vary and sometimes are proprietary: acids, disinfectants, dispersants, corrosion inhibitors, and biocides may appear in effluent, especially during maintenance or startup. They can enable additional cycles or control corrosion/scaling/biological growth.
- Pipe corrosion, startup/passivation/cleaning, and leaching during stagnation can introduce metals such as copper, nickel, and chromium, including without intentional chemical additions. Effluent pH and elevated temperature can also count as CWA pollutants.

### S22. How Are Water-Related Discharges from Data Centers Regulated at the Federal Level? (pp. 21–26)

Developers/operators may need federal CWA permits/approvals to prevent/minimize water-quality effects. Requirements depend on site, facility and operation, discharge destination, and varying state/local requirements. Nested regulatory answers are S23–S28.

### S23. Regulation of Discharges of Dredged or Fill Material (pp. 21–22)

- Placement of dredged/fill material in waters of the United States during development requires a Section 404 permit, generally administered by USACE (Michigan and New Jersey administer programs with retained USACE jurisdiction in certain waters).
- Individual permits provide site-specific review, typically for more-than-minimal effects; general permits cover similar categories with only minimal individual/cumulative adverse effects. USACE reissues nationwide permits every five years.
- In 2026, USACE clarified NWP 39 covers data centers. It covers qualifying commercial/institutional foundations, pads, and necessary attendant features in nontidal jurisdictional waters; discharge must not cause loss of more than **half an acre**. Footnote 123 distinguishes the rule preamble from the 2025 executive order directing review: the preamble does not reference that order.
- Public USACE data showed 21 individual data-center permits issued in 2009–2026 as of July 2026, with more pending; there are no public-facing counts of general-permit-authorized data centers.
- A Section 404 permit also often triggers Section 401 state/authorized-tribal water-quality certification. Section 401 applies to federal permits/licenses for activities that may discharge to waters of the United States; EPA certifies in specified federal/Indian jurisdictions where appropriate.

### S24. Regulation of Stormwater (pp. 22–23)

- NPDES permits regulate point-source pollutant discharges to waters of the United States and may be individual or general. Forty-seven states and the Virgin Islands administer authorized programs; EPA administers the remaining listed jurisdictions and Indian lands (no tribe has assumed a program).
- Construction disturbing at least **one acre** and discharging stormwater to jurisdictional waters requires NPDES coverage, often under a general construction permit with pollution prevention and sediment/erosion controls.
- Operational industrial stormwater may also need permits. Data centers' inclusion in listed industrial sectors is unclear, but a permitting authority can designate a facility anyway. At least one state proposed a data-center general permit covering runoff from generators, tanks, outdoor storage, loading/unloading, and related activities.

### S25. Regulation of Wastewater (pp. 23–26)

Needed permits depend on discharge contents and destination: municipal sewers, surface water, or alternative disposal. The three nested answers follow.

### S26. Municipal Sewer Systems (pp. 23–25)

- Most commonly, centers send wastewater to POTWs, which treat before surface discharge. If the POTW discharges to jurisdictional waters it has an NPDES permit; the center is an indirect discharger subject to the National Pretreatment Program.
- Pretreatment aims to prevent pollutants interfering with POTWs or passing through untreated. Local municipalities mostly implement/enforce it. Approved POTWs act as control authorities; otherwise the authorized state or EPA does so.
- General/specific prohibitions apply to all industrial users: no interference/pass-through, plus eight prohibited categories. Industry-specific categorical standards arise from EPA Effluent Limitation Guidelines and Standards (ELGs). Local numeric/narrative limits and best practices protect infrastructure, receiving waters, workers, and sludge management.
- EPA has **no data-center ELGs**, so categorical pretreatment standards do not apply. Public information indicates data centers are not typically significant industrial users because flow thresholds exclude cooling blowdown, although those flows may affect treatment. Centers still must follow general/specific prohibitions and any local limits applied at the connection.
- Some authorities report limited effluent information, intermittent high-volume construction/maintenance slug loads, relatively low-strength routine flows, and insufficient POTW capacity where data centers cluster.
- Slugs may cause interference/pass-through or temporary permit noncompliance; startup/maintenance metals may affect sludge limits, and chemicals may corrode infrastructure.
- Some authorities say clearer quantity/quality information permits compliance measures: adjust treatment, require data-center pretreatment or storage, or require alternative disposal such as hauling to an appropriate facility.

### S27. Surface Waters (pp. 25–26)

- Direct discharges to waters of the United States need NPDES wastewater permits. EPA says this is less common for data centers than other industries but may occur without municipal wastewater access.
- Permits can set site/source-dependent limits and monitoring for flow, temperature, pH, nutrients, chlorine, additives, and metals. Without data-center ELGs, writers typically use best professional judgment for discharge limits.
- An NPDES-authorized data center designed to withdraw at least **2 million gallons/day** from jurisdictional waters for cooling must meet Section 316(b) intake-structure performance requirements. These seek best available technology to minimize environmental harm, especially injury/death of aquatic life from impingement/entrainment.

### S28. Alternative Disposal Methods (p. 26)

Alternative disposal may invoke other federal/state/local frameworks. Subsurface injection of wastewater or residuals requires SDWA Underground Injection Control authorization. On-site storage/treatment such as lagoons/impoundments may face federal or state design, construction, operation, and permit standards protecting ground/surface waters.

### S29. Congressional Action / Which Bills Have Been Introduced in the 119th Congress That Address Data Centers and Water? (pp. 26–27)

- Members introduced bills/amendments on availability, use, discharge, and other data-center topics. As of July 30, 2026, **at least one relevant provision was enacted**: FY2026 NDAA Section 1531 expands DOD's High Performance Computing Roadmap to estimate extra water/electricity and community effects of military-installation data-center construction/expansion, **to the extent estimates do not delay triennial updates**.
- Measures were referred to multiple committees and most remained at introduction. Water provisions generally sit within broader data-center or energy bills; approaches include information gathering/publication, reuse incentives, and facilitating/limiting construction.
- Table A-1 is a **selection**, not a claim to exhaust all potentially applicable legislation. Bills using “commercial” or “industrial” without naming data centers may also apply depending on wording/interpretation; H.R. 9497's proposed USACE impact report is an example.
