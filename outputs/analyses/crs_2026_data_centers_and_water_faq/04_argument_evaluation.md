Source title: Data Centers and Water: Frequently Asked Questions
Source type: government_report
Publisher: Congressional Research Service
DOI: null

The Congressional Research Service’s July 31, 2026 report is most convincing as an explanation of why data-center water policy requires several different questions, and less conclusive about how those questions should be answered. Its congressional setting matters. Written by specialists in energy, environmental policy, and natural resources, it gives legislators and staff a technical and jurisdictional reference. The FAQ format accommodates readers arriving with particular constituent concerns. Its purpose is principally to inform, while making potential congressional actions intelligible. It neither advocates unrestricted development nor argues for a moratorium. Evaluating it as though it had promised either would miss its actual achievement and its more consequential limitations.

Its underlying argument is that growing computing activity creates water demands whose effects depend on technology, place, timing, and institutional arrangements; existing information cannot adequately isolate those effects; and several levels of government already possess relevant powers. These premises support a qualified conclusion: national averages and the label “data center” cannot decide whether a particular development is acceptable. This conclusion appears in the summary before its supporting explanations. It is subsequently earned through accounts of cooling, water sources, seasonal demand, wastewater, and fragmented reporting. The long legislative appendix supplies possible responses, but the existence of a bill is evidence of political attention, not evidence that its mechanism will work.

The strongest reasoning connects physical mechanisms to local conditions. Evaporative cooling needs more water in hotter periods (pp. 5–6), while the significance of additional demand depends partly on whether it coincides with other users’ peaks or constrained supplies (p. 10). This establishes why an annual national percentage cannot settle a community’s capacity question. It does not establish that every hot-weather facility causes shortages: that would require local supply, demand, and operating data. The report usually preserves this distinction. Similarly, its explanation that repeated cooling cycles concentrate dissolved solids connects conservation at the intake to the character of eventual discharges. Quantity and quality are related problems, rather than interchangeable measures of harm.

The opening numbers are less thoroughly developed than these mechanisms. The introduction offers an approximately 2% national consumption share, a 100 MW facility’s household equivalent, and an estimate that municipal systems provide 97% of data-center water (p. 1). These come from different sources, including a utility association and a commercial research firm’s press release. Their presentation provides little opportunity to assess coverage or uncertainty. The household comparison is unusually transparent about deriving direct consumption from a broader estimate, but averaging cooling strategies makes it an illustration, not a prediction for any proposed facility. The later estimate of growth from 5.6 billion gallons in 2014 to 17 billion in 2023 supports concern about expansion, without showing where that growth exceeded available capacity. The narrative is strongest when these quantities establish scale and weakest if readers treat them as a unified risk assessment.

The exclusion of indirect water use is defensible for a report centered on local supply arrangements, but it limits the conclusions available about technological improvement. Pages 3–4 acknowledge that air-cooled systems generally require more electricity and that facility-level water-use effectiveness omits water consumed in electricity generation. That qualification prevents a straightforward inference from lower on-site consumption to lower overall water demand. It also means the report cannot rank cooling systems comprehensively within its chosen boundary. An energy-intensive system might ease pressure on a particular local supply while shifting water demand elsewhere. Whether that is preferable depends on the locations and conditions of both supplies. The report identifies the tradeoff but does not resolve it.

Its case for an information problem is more substantial than a general appeal for further research. Pages 13–15 explain how public-supply totals combine customers, self-suppliers may face fewer reporting requirements, and federal statistical categories fail to isolate data centers. Better spatial resolution does not repair a missing sectoral category. This is a convincing account of why available measurements may not answer policymakers’ questions. It supports collecting information that connects facilities, sources, timing, and local conditions. It does not uniquely justify a particular federal disclosure regime: targeted watershed studies, state reporting, or utility requirements could address different parts of the gap. Nor does uncertainty determine whether approvals should continue or pause while information improves. That decision requires a judgment about the costs of delay and the consequences of discovering harm after construction.

The wastewater discussion shows both the report’s practical value and its evidentiary limits. Short, concentrated commissioning or maintenance discharges can challenge treatment plants even when ordinary operations produce weaker wastewater (pp. 19, 24–25). Cooling blowdown can also be excluded from flow-based classification thresholds while remaining operationally consequential. Together these points undermine any assumption that a regulatory category reliably measures physical risk. Yet the evidence of treatment problems relies substantially on professional guidance, a webcast, and reports from some control authorities. These sources can establish credible mechanisms and experienced difficulties; they do not supply a representative incidence rate. The report warrants attention to intermittent loads, but neither a finding of nationwide regulatory failure nor reassurance that existing controls are sufficient follows.

This distinction matters because the account spends far more attention identifying legal tools than evaluating their performance. Its explanation of state primacy carefully separates Congress’s historical deference from an inherent absence of federal power (p. 7, footnote 34). Its pretreatment discussion also distinguishes the absence of data-center-specific standards from the absence of regulation. Both are valuable corrections. But the availability of local limits, professional judgment, storage requirements, or on-site treatment does not establish that authorities have adequate staff, information, or bargaining power to use them effectively. Conversely, identifying implementation difficulties does not prove uniform national standards would outperform local tailoring. The institutional map supplies a starting point for that comparison, not its result.

Reuse receives sustained, constructive attention without an explicit CRS endorsement. The report describes treatment needs, competing demands for reclaimed water, and uncertainty over permitting before detailing federal coordination initiatives. Those qualifications deserve weight. Still, accounts of plans, partnerships, and proposed tax credits demonstrate administrative activity rather than additional water savings. Evaluating reuse would require asking what would otherwise happen to the reclaimed water, what treatment and infrastructure cost, and whether assistance changes investment decisions. A reuse project could relieve potable demand yet divert water from another beneficial use. This possibility follows from the report’s own recognition of competing demand (p. 5). It deserves the same scrutiny applied to conventional supply options.

The treatment of pricing has a related boundary. Pages 6–7 explain different tariffs and full cost recovery, but recovering a utility’s costs does not by itself establish that existing residents bear none of the expansion costs or environmental consequences. Confidential service terms make those questions harder to answer. The report identifies this obstacle without resolving it. Its institutional perspective makes utility operations, permits, and congressional proposals especially visible; affected residents’ experiences and distributional outcomes receive less independent development. The appendix’s proposed study of low-income communities and communities of color identifies an unanswered question, rather than supplying the missing evidence.

I am inclined to favor disclosure and planning because they appear to improve decisions without deciding every siting dispute in advance. That preference could make the report’s administrative framing feel more adequate than it is. Information has costs, arrives unevenly, and cannot itself choose whose water needs receive priority. Equally, my concern about concentrated local harm cannot convert a plausible mechanism into evidence that a particular project is unacceptable. The report’s caution is useful precisely because it constrains both reactions.

My judgment is that the report succeeds as a disciplined explanation of mechanisms, uncertainty, and divided authority. Its strongest conclusion is that meaningful assessment must connect a facility’s operations to the receiving water and infrastructure systems. It leaves the comparative effectiveness of policy responses largely unproved. That is consistent with its informational purpose, but readers should not mistake the detail of its regulatory inventory for evidence of protection, or the prominence of reuse and reporting initiatives for proof of their sufficiency. The report equips Congress to ask better questions; it does not yet establish which answers should govern development.
