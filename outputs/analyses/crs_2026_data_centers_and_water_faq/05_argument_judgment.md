## B1. Premise acceptability — 2

The evaluation weighs the provenance and limitations of evidence behind the report’s central accounts of water demand, information gaps, and wastewater impacts. Its paragraph beginning “The opening numbers” distinguishes the utility-association estimate, the commercial research press release, and the IEA household comparison. Page 1, notes 3–5, supports those distinctions; note 4 supplies the direct/indirect allocation and cooling-strategy averaging that the evaluation credits. It correctly treats these estimates as illustrations of scale rather than a methodologically unified assessment of local risk.

Its wastewater paragraph also examines evidentiary quality rather than merely repeating conclusions. Pages 19–20 and 24–25 cite Water Environment Federation guidance and a webcast, alongside InsideEPA reporting about control authorities. The evaluation reasonably distinguishes credible mechanisms and reported difficulties from a representative incidence estimate. It also explains why the aggregation and classification practices on pp. 13–15 substantiate the information problem. These are passage-based judgments about what the evidence can establish, without pretending to have independently validated the underlying sources.

## B2. Relevance of critique — 2

The criticisms remain attached to the report’s stated scope and qualifications. The evaluation expressly recognizes that CRS informs Congress, does not endorse unrestricted development or a moratorium, and does not explicitly endorse reuse. It credits the direct-water-use boundary, the discussion of energy tradeoffs, and the distinction between missing sector-specific standards and missing regulation. These readings are supported by p. 2, pp. 3–4, pp. 16–18, and pp. 23–25.

Several criticisms concern conclusions the report cannot establish—comparative policy effectiveness, equitable cost allocation, or net savings from reuse—rather than conclusions it actually asserts. That limits their force as objections, but the evaluation generally identifies them as boundaries or possible reader overinterpretations. It does not accuse CRS of claiming nationwide regulatory failure, proving existing controls sufficient, or demonstrating that reuse incentives work. Accordingly, these passages do not amount to a straw man.

## B3. Sufficiency — 2

The evaluation consistently distinguishes evidence that permits a conclusion from evidence that establishes it. For the central local-impact question, it explains that cooling mechanisms and national totals do not establish a shortage at a particular facility; local supply, demand, and operating information would be needed. Pages 5–6 and 10 substantiate that distinction. For wastewater, it separates possible interference and reported implementation difficulties from a national failure rate (pp. 24–25).

It also specifies what is missing from stronger policy conclusions: implementation capacity and comparative performance for regulatory alternatives; counterfactual uses, treatment and infrastructure costs, and investment additionality for reuse; and information about expansion-cost allocation for pricing. Its conclusion that the report explains mechanisms and authority more securely than it establishes which policy should govern development is supported by the descriptive treatment of existing authorities and the legislative inventory on pp. 26–37. These are concrete sufficiency judgments, even where the report never undertook the stronger policy comparison.

## B4. Unstated warrants and missing qualifiers — 1

The evaluation identifies consequential inferential bridges: lower on-site water consumption would need an additional premise to imply lower overall water demand; legal tools would need effective implementation to imply protection; reuse initiatives would need a counterfactual to imply additional savings; and utility-wide cost recovery would need an allocation argument to imply that residents bear no expansion costs. It explains why these bridges matter.

However, it rarely demonstrates that the report’s own conclusions depend on an unstated warrant. CRS explicitly discusses the indirect-water boundary and electricity tradeoff (pp. 2–4), competing uses for reclaimed water (p. 5), and implementation and information problems (pp. 24–25). It does not conclude that full cost recovery protects every resident or that reuse initiatives prove savings. Much of the evaluation therefore identifies unwarranted extensions a reader might make, rather than assumptions secretly carrying the author’s argument. Its strongest missing-qualification observation is the limited methodological and uncertainty information accompanying the opening estimates (p. 1, notes 3–5). This merits partial credit, rather than full credit for locating unstated dependencies in the report’s conclusions.

## B5. Self-attribution — 2

Judgments are clearly the evaluator’s. Phrases such as “My judgment” and its explicit acknowledgment of a preference for disclosure and planning distinguish its interpretation from CRS’s position. The self-reflection is substantive: it identifies how that preference could make the administrative framing seem sufficient, while its concern about local harm could encourage overreading plausible mechanisms as grounds to reject a project. It also recognizes that information cannot determine whose water needs should take priority. Nothing in these passages presents the evaluator’s policy preferences as the authors’ recommendations.

## Researcher-facing assessment

The evaluation is strongest on evidence provenance, the distinction between plausible mechanisms and demonstrated impacts, and the limits of moving from an institutional inventory to a policy recommendation. Its factual account is supported by the report, and it clearly owns its policy framing. Its principal weakness is that several supposed inferential gaps are limits CRS already acknowledges or stronger claims CRS never attempts. It is useful as an assessment of what the report equips readers to conclude, with less success at identifying unstated premises on which the report’s actual conclusions depend.
