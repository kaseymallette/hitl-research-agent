Source title: System-scale design and analysis of desalination systems for meeting water needs of data centers  
Source type: peer_reviewed_paper  
Publisher: Elsevier  
DOI: 10.1016/j.watres.2026.126748

Katakam and colleagues make a convincing case for treating saline water as a supply option that data center planners should price and test. They make a weaker case that their preferred ways of treating it relieve regional water stress. The distinction matters because the paper announces both contributions in its abstract: a benchmark for desalination and an index that purports to quantify the local stress implications of using it. In Elsevier’s *Water Research*, the paper speaks most directly to water treatment researchers and engineers, while addressing planners through three Texas siting scenarios. It is a design study with a practical aim: show how large computing facilities might secure cooling water without drawing as much on existing freshwater supplies. The engineering analysis earns a place for desalination in that discussion. The siting analysis does not yet tell a planner when to choose it.

The best-supported inference starts with the salt balance (§2). For a given feed and a fixed quantity of freshwater product, recovering more product from each unit of feed means less intake and a smaller volume of more concentrated residual brine. The question is what it takes to get there. The authors devote most of their methods and results to answering it. They let the two reverse osmosis stages vary their pressure and recovery and let the mechanical vapor compression system trade exchanger area against compressor work. They minimize the cost of producing a cubic metre of usable water, including allowances for feed pretreatment, product polishing and concentrate handling. That objective exposes tradeoffs that a search for minimum energy alone would miss. In the worked seawater hybrid that reaches 130,000 ppm concentrate (§3.2, Fig. 5), vapor compression accounts for 7.8 of the 9.4 kWh and $2.60 of the $3.20 per cubic metre of combined product, even though reverse osmosis produces most of the water. This breakdown is more useful than the headline recovery gain: it tells a designer where the incremental burden lies and why electricity price and thermal equipment costs matter.

That finding is credible within the modeled designs. It does not establish the same cost or even the same feasible recovery at a specific site. The authors criticize earlier studies for simplifying equipment behavior or leaving out costs. Their own benchmark deserves the same scrutiny where it approaches saturation. Modeling pretreated feed as sodium chloride is a reasonable way to isolate the main process tradeoffs, but minor constituents can govern scale formation and constrain achievable recovery. The paper acknowledges that additional treatment may be needed (§2.4). Likewise, Table 1’s concentrate handling charges let the authors compare scenarios on a common basis; they cannot establish that a given site has an affordable outlet for its concentrate. A modeled brine concentration near saturation does not establish that a facility can operate without liquid discharge or sell the resulting solids. The engineering results justify investigating those options, particularly where disposal is already difficult. The environmental and mineral recovery benefits need evidence about what happens to the residual stream.

The central problem with Saline Water Utilization Intensity (SWUI) is that its physical quantity and its proposed interpretation come apart. Equation (12) first estimates litres of saline feed withdrawn per unit of IT energy, then multiplies by one plus the regional Aqueduct stress score. The first calculation answers a useful question that ordinary facility water usage effectiveness leaves out. The second expresses a preference to give the same intake more weight in a stressed region. The authors explicitly say the weighting does not describe depletion of the saline source (§2.3 and p. 16). They instead argue that the desalinated freshwater might have gone to other users in that region. That is a plausible concern where the project competes for an existing supply or where there is a credible alternative buyer for new production. The paper does not establish that alternative. If an intake and treatment plant would only be built to serve the data center, the existence of municipal demand does not mean the facility displaced a municipal supply. Conversely, if brackish pumping affects nearby freshwater wells, the effect depends on local hydrology, which a regional score cannot supply.

The coastal example isolates the distinction. In Fig. 7b, the middle cooling-demand projection under business-as-usual regional stress gives conventional seawater treatment a score of 5.0 and minimum liquid discharge a score of 2.9. At the same site, with the same freshwater demand per unit of IT energy, the roughly 40% decline is a decline in modeled seawater intake per unit of IT energy: the stress multiplier has not changed. The facility also uses the same amount of freshwater product in both cases, so the example does not demonstrate any change in the amount available to other users. Lower intake could still matter for marine impacts, and a smaller brine stream could ease some disposal problems. Neither benefit can be inferred from volume alone, and the added electricity has consequences that depend on how it is supplied. Comparing it with the large computing load, as the authors do in §3.3, does not establish that the extra power or emissions are negligible for the choice between treatments. The relevant comparison is what the extra treatment costs and what it avoids.

The other site comparisons are useful demonstrations of how the index works. They show how more recovery can offset a higher regional stress score, and the reported test of alternative weightings shows that the ranking of the main cases survives those particular changes. That is a check on the arithmetic ranking, not a validation of the impact categories borrowed from a freshwater index. Nor does the upward trend to 2030 establish that the three facilities will become more water intensive. It largely follows from feeding in a rising water usage effectiveness trajectory pooled across cooling approaches. The close fit to the underlying projections through 2028 says little about which cooling system an individual facility will use. Figure 7 is most informative when read as a set of conditional comparisons: given a cooling demand, a feed and a recovery, here is the implied intake, and here is how a chosen stress weight changes the ranking.

The authors’ engineering perspective helps explain both the care in the treatment models and the decision to take computing demand largely as given. They declare no known competing interests and acknowledge a university Energy Institute grant (p. 18); the framing is better explained by the stated design problem than by a presumed financial stake. My own preference is to judge siting claims against a specific alternative: what water would otherwise be used, who would have access to it, and where the residuals and power demand would go. That preference would be too strict for a first-pass equipment screen, where transparent common assumptions are an advantage. It is appropriate when a screen is used to claim local environmental improvement. I would carry forward the paper’s estimates of recovery, intake, energy and cost as separate decision inputs, then compare them at equal cooling service with realistic freshwater, reuse and lower-water cooling options. The paper shows why recovery belongs in that comparison. It leaves open how much recovery is worth buying at any of the three sites.
