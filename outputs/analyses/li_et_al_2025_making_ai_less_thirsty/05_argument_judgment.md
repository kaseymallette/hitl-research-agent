## B1. Premise acceptability — 2

The evaluation weighs the inputs behind the central estimates rather than accepting their provenance or precision at face value. Its discussion of training identifies the published 1,287 MWh estimate, annualized Microsoft WUE/PUE, regional EWIF inputs, undisclosed training location, and projected facilities (p. 59, “Training” and Table 1). Its strongest evidence appraisal concerns inference: the article moves from approximately 0.004 kWh per generated page, with unspecified measurement boundaries, to an assumed 0.004 kWh per conversational request including prompt processing and non-GPU energy (pp. 59–60, “Inference”). The evaluation correctly distinguishes that assumption from direct measurement and evaluates the limits of the Llama-3/Falcon comparisons offered in its defense. It also checks the provenance and limited reach of Figure 2's five-day Virginia example (p. 60). These are substantive, passage-grounded judgments about the evidence behind the main conclusions.

## B2. Relevance of critique — 1

Most criticisms address the article's actual claims and preserve its qualifications. The evaluation explicitly acknowledges that dry cooling is said only to *potentially* exacerbate stress (pp. 57–58), that informed users only *may* shift inference (p. 61), and that the carbon/water argument needs examples of divergence rather than a universal correlation result (pp. 60–61). It appropriately challenges the stronger claims that judicious scheduling “can significantly cut” the footprint (pp. 56, 61).

There is a limited overreach in the criticism that the paper supplies no comparison establishing its preferred tools as “sufficient or preferable across settings.” The recommendations advocate disclosure, scheduling, research, and reconciling carbon/water conflicts; they do not assert that these measures alone suffice or dominate restrictions on demand or siting. Similarly, treating accommodation of AI growth as the paper's assumption is a plausible interpretation of its emphasis, not an explicit commitment established by the text. These peripheral criticisms reach beyond the claims as stated. They do not make the main critique a straw man.

## B3. Sufficiency — 2

The evaluation consistently separates what the evidence establishes from what it merely permits. It accepts operational accounting and separate carbon/water reporting while treating GPT-3 numbers as scenarios rather than a reconstruction of a measured training event. It explains why cross-model energy comparisons make higher consumption plausible without proving a GPT-3 lower bound. It distinguishes variation in attributed water intensity from demonstrated causal savings through scheduling, and liters consumed from local environmental damage.

It also specifies what would close the important gaps: watershed-specific availability and sources, incremental electricity supply, intervention-specific scheduling evidence, and constraints or behavioral participation relevant to realizing savings. Its final distinction between evidence sufficient for an agenda-setting reporting recommendation and evidence sufficient for directing workloads is particularly well matched to the article's purpose.

## B4. Unstated warrants and missing qualifiers — 2

The evaluation identifies several consequential inferential bridges. The consumption-to-stress argument needs information about where and when consumption competes with scarce water; pp. 56–58 recognize scarcity and watersheds, but Table 1 does not operationalize those conditions. The scheduling recommendation needs a bridge from average fuel-mix attribution (p. 58; Figure 2, p. 60) to the electricity generation and water use changed by shifting demand (p. 61). The claim that the inference estimate is conservative needs transferability from different models and serving configurations (p. 60).

These are explained as reasons the conclusions require additional qualification, rather than merely named assumptions. Some qualifications are already present—the authors expressly disclose an assumed request-energy input, projected sites, and the unknown training location. The evaluation generally recognizes those disclosures and questions what follows from them; it does not need to treat every limitation as concealed to earn this score.

## B5. Self-attribution — 2

The evaluation plainly owns its judgments through statements such as “I find,” “My judgment,” and “I would accept.” Its final paragraph identifies its preference for separating attribution from causal intervention and volume from local harm, and acknowledges that this standard could undervalue an agenda-setting feature. It also identifies the authors' computing/optimization perspective, relates this to their earlier cited work, and correctly avoids inferring a hidden commercial motive from NSF support (p. 61, affiliations, acknowledgments, and references 1 and 10). The interpretation of growth accommodation is too categorical for the textual evidence, but remains recognizable as the evaluator's reading rather than a quotation or reported author claim.

## Assessment for the researcher

This is a substantively grounded evaluation whose main strength is distinguishing conditional accounting estimates, causal interventions, and environmental consequences. Its strongest criticisms can be traced directly to the article's methods, table, and recommendations; it also gives a defensible positive assessment of separate water reporting. Its main weakness is occasional expansion from the article's limited agenda to unproved assumptions about accommodating growth or preferring its tools over a broader policy menu. The numerical and textual checks found no definite factual errors. The researcher can use the central methodological critique while treating those broader framing judgments as contestable interpretations.
