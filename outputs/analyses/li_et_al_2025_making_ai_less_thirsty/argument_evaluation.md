Source title: Making AI Less 'Thirsty': Uncovering and addressing the secret water footprint of AI models
Source type: other
Publisher: Communications of the ACM (ACM)
DOI: 10.1145/3724499

Li and colleagues make a convincing case that water belongs in AI's environmental accounting, but a less complete case for how that accounting should guide decisions. Published in the July 2025 Communications of the ACM sustainability section, the article addresses a broad computing audience through accessible cooling explanations, memorable comparisons, and a compact mathematical framework. Its purpose is openly persuasive: it seeks to make water visible to a community accustomed to carbon reporting and to propose technical and reporting responses. I find its disclosure recommendation well supported. Its headline consumption estimates deserve treatment as conditional scenarios, while its claims about reducing water stress require more than the volume accounting supplies.

The argument proceeds from growing datacenter demand and freshwater scarcity to an overlooked connection between them. Cooling and electricity generation consume water; carbon metrics do not reliably capture that consumption; therefore AI assessment needs separate water metrics. The authors then estimate GPT-3's operational consumption and recommend disclosure, scheduling, and joint carbon-water optimization. This sequence rests on a defensible value judgment: computing should account for its demands on shared resources beyond its immediate premises. However, the transition from physical consumption to environmental priority also assumes that the quantities presented reveal consequential competition for water. That assumption receives less development than the cooling mechanisms and accounting equations.

The strongest part is the explanation of system boundaries. The distinction between water circulating through server cooling and water evaporated during facility heat rejection prevents a misleading equation of liquid cooling with consumption (p. 57). Likewise, distinguishing withdrawal from consumption prevents returned water from being counted as though it had all disappeared from the immediate environment. Including electricity-related water avoids crediting a facility for eliminating onsite evaporation while ignoring increased power demand elsewhere. The operational equation respects these boundaries: server energy multiplies onsite water intensity, while power usage effectiveness applies to the electricity-related term (pp. 58-59). This is a coherent attribution method. It does not depend on accepting the article's largest projections.

Table 1 makes this contribution concrete. In the U.S.-average scenario, about 4.731 million of the 5.439 million liters attributed to training are offsite. The projected India facility has zero onsite consumption but substantial electricity-related consumption. These results establish that onsite efficiency alone can miss most of the estimated footprint. They do not establish that dry cooling is environmentally inferior. The article appropriately says additional electricity could worsen overall stress, but deciding whether it does would require comparing the affected watersheds and the incremental electricity supply. Moving consumption away from a severely constrained local source might be worthwhile even if total attributed liters increased. The authors' broader boundary is necessary for that comparison, but cannot decide it by itself.

The GPT-3 estimates are transparent enough to inspect, although their presentation risks giving calculated precision the authority of measurement. Training uses a published energy estimate, recent annualized facility parameters, and regional electricity factors; the actual training location is undisclosed (p. 59). Some facility parameters are projections for sites under construction. The U.S.-average result therefore describes what training would consume under specified inputs, rather than reconstructing the actual event. Holding training energy fixed across locations makes the comparison interpretable, but it is not evidence that all locations could deliver the same training run with identical server energy. Reporting three decimal places in the table adds arithmetic detail without establishing comparable empirical certainty.

The inference estimate has a weaker foundation. The authors start with an estimate of 0.004 kWh per page generated, infer that it likely covers only token-generation GPU energy, and assume the same 0.004 kWh for a shorter conversational response including prompt processing and other server energy (pp. 59-60). This may be a reasonable exploratory input, but the conversion is not independently demonstrated. Evidence that other models consume 0.010 or 0.016 kWh per request makes higher consumption plausible; differences in models and serving arrangements prevent those comparisons from proving a lower bound for GPT-3. Similarly, selecting an electricity factor below a newer U.S. estimate makes that component conservative relative to that comparator, not every component conservative under every deployment. The bottle comparison communicates scale effectively, but its approximately 10-50 responses should remain attached to these assumptions. The table itself extends to roughly 66 and 70 responses in Texas and Ireland.

The article earns a warning about potentially substantial demand more securely than it establishes the resulting environmental damage. Its opening corporate totals concern datacenters generally, with AI identified as a growth driver rather than isolated through a workload-level decomposition (p. 54). The global projections depend on electricity forecasts and assumed water intensities. Comparing withdrawal with several Denmarks makes magnitude legible, but says little about which communities face scarcity or when. The paper recognizes this problem conceptually: it stresses uneven freshwater distribution, watershed availability, and peak cooling needs on hot days. Yet the case study sums liters without weighting scarcity or tracing offsite consumption to particular water sources. A lower-liter location is consequently not demonstrated to be the better location for water stewardship. This is a limitation of the inference from the metric, not a reason to discard the metric.

The evidence that carbon and water need separate attention is stronger. Figure 2 combines regional dispersion with a five-day Virginia example whose reported correlation is 0.06 (p. 60). One short series cannot characterize every grid or season, but the authors do not need that universal claim to defeat the assumption that carbon efficiency reliably stands in for water efficiency. Examples of divergence, supported by different physical mechanisms, are enough. Their recommendation to consider both measures follows. How to trade them off remains unresolved, however: a joint objective requires judgments about local scarcity, emissions, service requirements, and whose interests count. Calling the approach holistic names this requirement without supplying a decision rule.

Scheduling receives lighter scrutiny than the alternatives it is meant to improve. Spatial and temporal variation creates an opportunity, but Figure 2 uses average fuel-mix accounting. Moving a workload changes demand at the margin; the generators responding need not resemble the average mix. Nor does annualized Table 1 quantify the savings from a particular scheduling intervention. Capacity, latency, long training durations, and additional infrastructure could constrain the opportunity. These considerations do not refute scheduling. They mean the paper establishes a research direction more firmly than a demonstrated reduction in total consumption. The conditional suggestion that informed users might shift inference is appropriately modest, but no behavioral evidence establishes participation or savings. Disclosure would need consistent boundaries and uncertainty information to support useful choices.

The authors' framing helps explain both the achievement and the omissions. Computing researchers, including authors of earlier water-aware scheduling work cited here, naturally see an overlooked impact as something to measure and optimize. Their NSF support is disclosed; it does not establish a hidden commercial motive. More consequential is the paper's assumption that AI growth should be accommodated through improved efficiency and stewardship. It briefly recognizes that aggregate demand may rise despite per-request gains, yet gives little attention to reducing low-value demand, restricting siting, or public allocation of scarce water. Those alternatives could reasonably follow from the same scarcity premise. The article supplies no comparison establishing that its preferred tools are sufficient or preferable across settings.

My judgment also reflects a preference for separating attribution from causal intervention and resource volume from local harm. That standard could undervalue an agenda-setting feature whose immediate purpose is to correct neglect. The authors need less evidence to justify measurement and research than to justify a deployment choice. On that distinction, the article succeeds: it establishes that water cannot safely be inferred from carbon or onsite cooling alone, and its data gaps strengthen the case for disclosure. It does not establish a universal water cost per response, a ranking of environmental damage by location, or the scale of achievable scheduling savings. I would accept its reporting agenda and conditional warning while requiring watershed-specific and intervention-specific evidence before using its estimates to direct workloads or claim sustainability improvements.
