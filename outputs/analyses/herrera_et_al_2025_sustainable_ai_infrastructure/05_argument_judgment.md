B1 — Premise acceptability: **2/2**.

The evaluation examines the evidence behind the central numerical conclusions rather than accepting the forecasts at face value. It checks the provenance and AI-specific scope of the electricity baseline (§5.1, p. 7, Eq. 5), the conversion of compute growth directly into energy growth (Algorithm 1, p. 8), the limited stochastic inputs (§5.1), and the sensitivity results against the additive accounting equations (§2.2, p. 3; Appendix, p. 10). It also distinguishes qualitative cooling comparisons from measured whole-facility performance (§3.3–3.4, p. 5) and national facility counts from watershed-level evidence (§4, pp. 5–6). These are concrete assessments of method, provenance, and evidential scope, with traceable passages.

B2 — Relevance of critique: **1/2**.

The main criticisms address claims the authors actually make. In particular, the conclusion explicitly attributes up to 95% lower 2050 consumption to WUE, EWIF, and hardware lifecycle improvements (p. 10), while the scenarios also change compute growth (§5.1). The evaluation correctly acknowledges the exploratory purpose, the explicit absence of full Bayesian inference, the regionalisation concession, and the costs and compatibility limits of liquid cooling.

One criticism reaches beyond the stated claim: its closing discussion of alternative water sources invokes an assumption that substituting non-potable water eliminates the relevant burden. Section 6.3 recommends non-potable sources to address municipal water stress; it does not assert elimination of the total water burden. The demand for return-flow accounting is reasonable as a proposed extension, but that stronger assumption is not established as the authors’ position. The evaluation’s opening claim about insufficient headline qualification also needs care: the abstract says “Results suggest,” “without mitigation,” and “could,” and §7 explicitly conditions growth on unchecked trends. The defensible criticism concerns unsupported numerical interpretation, rather than an absence of conditional language.

B3 — Sufficiency: **2/2**.

The evaluation consistently separates a useful accounting framework from established magnitudes and intervention effects. It explains that direct and electricity-related water consumption justify integrated accounting without validating the 2050 totals. It identifies why a comparison that changes both demand and efficiency cannot isolate technological mitigation, and proposes holding demand growth constant. It also explains what further evidence is needed for AI-specific baselines, adoption–consumption decoupling, whole-system cooling comparisons, and local siting decisions. The calculation behind the central scenario criticism checks out: 1 − 1.38/28.11 = 95.09%, while 1.10^25 = 10.83. Those calculations expose a real identification problem, without implying that all scenario planning requires causal identification.

B4 — Unstated warrants and missing qualifiers: **2/2**.

The evaluation identifies consequential inferential bridges: treating compute growth as proportional electricity growth without separately modelling compute efficiency; treating a multi-assumption scenario difference as a technological mitigation effect; treating stable compute as evidence that adoption can expand while water consumption falls; and moving from efficient chip-level heat removal to low total water consumption. It explains why each matters. It also identifies the unstated destination-of-discharge assumption behind counting blowdown as consumptive blue water (§3.2 versus §2.2). Zero compute growth itself is expressly stated in §5.1; the evaluation correctly treats its implementation and consequences, rather than its existence, as insufficiently examined. Its discussion of the sustainability label makes clear that this is an interpretation of the paper’s evaluative framing, not an omitted mathematical variable that automatically invalidates the model.

B5 — Self-attribution: **2/2**.

Judgments are recognisably the evaluator’s own: the opening separates the accounting contribution from forecast credibility, and the final paragraph explicitly states its preference for distinguishing exploratory calculations from demonstrated intervention effects. It acknowledges that this preference might undervalue precautionary scenario planning. It identifies engineering and sustainability framing as possible influences without alleging a financial conflict, and correctly reports the declaration of no known competing interests (p. 10). Its alternative of selective expansion is presented as another reasonable inference, not as the authors’ recommendation. The unsupported implication about non-potable substitution is accounted for under B2 and B6; it does not generally obscure whose judgment is being expressed.
