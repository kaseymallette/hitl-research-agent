Source title: Sustainable AI infrastructure: A scenario-based forecast of water footprint under uncertainty
Source type: peer_reviewed_paper
Publisher: Elsevier
DOI: 10.1016/j.jclepro.2025.146528

Herrera and colleagues make a convincing case that AI infrastructure needs water accounting beyond its electricity and carbon metrics. They make a less convincing case that their particular forecasts establish the scale of future demand or the effectiveness of their preferred interventions. Published in Elsevier’s Journal of Cleaner Production in 2025, the article addresses sustainability researchers, engineers, infrastructure planners, and policymakers. It combines technical explanation, a forecasting proposal, and advocacy for water-conscious development. Its strongest contribution is an accounting perspective. Its numerical claims require substantially more qualification than the abstract and conclusion give them.

The argument proceeds from rising AI workloads to increased heat and electricity demand, then to direct cooling consumption, power-generation consumption, and water embodied in hardware. Because these burdens are geographically dispersed and sometimes fall in stressed regions, the authors argue for integrated measurement, better cooling, and different siting practices. Sections 2–4 develop the physical mechanisms and geographic concerns at length; Section 5 supplies the simulation that is supposed to convert them into policy-relevant forecasts. The abstract announces the sevenfold-growth warning before this work begins, while the closing sections repeatedly return to it. That structure gives a conditional scenario the rhetorical force of an established outlook.

The three-component accounting is persuasive because it makes displacement visible. A facility can reduce on-site water consumption while increasing electricity demand and therefore water consumption elsewhere. Section 3.1’s comparison of air and evaporative cooling explicitly recognises that tradeoff. Equations (1)–(3), separating on-site use from electricity-related use, give the concern an intelligible analytical basis. Consequently, the recommendation to assess water alongside energy and emissions follows reasonably well. It does not require the particular 2050 numbers to be correct. However, calling the boundary consumptive blue water creates an obligation to distinguish consumption from withdrawal or discharge. Section 3.2 treats blowdown as permanently removed from the local water cycle without specifying where it goes. Discharge alone does not establish loss from the originating basin under the paper’s own definition. That uncertainty matters to the consistency of the proposed accounting.

The most consequential weakness is the interpretation of scenario differences as intervention benefits. Section 5.1 fixes annual compute growth at 10%, 5%, and zero. Algorithm 1 applies these rates directly to server electricity consumption. This assumes a relationship between compute and energy that the article does not separately model through changing processor efficiency or workload composition. Historical increases in frontier training compute motivate concern, but they do not establish these particular trajectories for worldwide server electricity. The article also moves between AI infrastructure and data centres generally without clearly establishing how its baseline isolates AI’s share. The baseline is attributed to an earlier data-centre energy study, rather than derived in the article from an explicit AI allocation.

These choices are legitimate ingredients for exploratory scenarios, but they limit the conclusions. Table 2’s sustainable outcome of 1.38 billion litres per day in 2050 is about 95% below BAU’s 28.11. The conclusion attributes a reduction of that size to cooling, electricity sourcing, and hardware lifecycle management. Yet the comparison also changes compute growth from 10% to zero for twenty-five years. With otherwise identical assumptions, that growth difference alone makes the growing energy component about eleven times larger by 2050. The scenario therefore cannot identify a 95% technological mitigation potential. Holding demand growth constant while changing efficiency would be needed to distinguish those effects. Likewise, Section 5.2’s suggestion that systemic improvements can decouple AI adoption from rising consumption is permitted by the model only under additional assumptions about adoption expanding without compute growth. The simulation does not demonstrate that relationship.

This does not make a stable-compute scenario worthless. It asks a useful question about sufficiency and demand management. But the authors give little attention to what stabilisation would require or which uses would be limited. Calling that pathway sustainable embeds a value judgment: a smaller resource footprint is treated as the principal criterion, while forgone services, implementation costs, and distributional consequences remain outside the comparison. The acknowledgement of environmentally beneficial AI in Section 6.3 is welcome, but no net-benefit assessment connects those benefits to the chosen growth limits. The evidence could also support maintaining some expansion while directing it toward higher-value uses and less vulnerable locations.

The probability language compounds this interpretive problem. The authors explicitly acknowledge that they do not perform full Bayesian inference. Their algorithm samples baseline electricity consumption from a normal distribution, then applies fixed growth and efficiency trajectories. That is a defensible uncertainty-propagation exercise. It does not, however, quantify uncertainty about whether those trajectories will occur. Increasing the number of Monte Carlo draws to 10,000 makes the simulation more stable; it does not supply evidence for the growth rates or capture technological surprises. The reported 90% intervals describe variability under the stipulated model. They should not be read as comprehensive uncertainty bounds on future demand. The paper’s conditional wording partly recognises this, but the repeated presentation of probabilistic forecasting as a remedy for scarce evidence encourages more confidence than the specified stochastic inputs warrant.

There is also an internal problem with the evidence used to rank interventions. In the printed equations, PUE and electricity water intensity multiply the same off-site term. Changing either alone by 20%, with everything else fixed, must therefore change total consumption by the same amount. Appendix Table A.3 instead reports different effects: 14.3% for PUE and 15.1% for EWIF. Moreover, a 20% WUE change producing a 16.7% total change implies that on-site consumption accounts for 83.5% of the baseline. The reported 15.1% EWIF response implies an off-site share of 75.5%. Those shares cannot coexist in an additive model with nonnegative embodied consumption. This is more than uncertainty about future inputs: the reported sensitivity results do not reconcile with the stated equations. An unreported procedure might explain the discrepancy, but then the article has not supplied the explanation needed to evaluate its central leverage ranking. Providing a repository link is useful, yet the printed argument must still be internally interpretable.

The cooling recommendations deserve scrutiny independently of those numbers. The article explains why liquid cooling can efficiently remove heat from chips, and it acknowledges retrofit costs and compatibility constraints. Nevertheless, Table 1’s qualitative designation of direct liquid cooling as low-water does not establish the consumption of the full heat-rejection system. A recirculating coolant loop does not by itself specify how heat ultimately leaves the facility. The paper’s own careful discussion of air cooling’s energy penalty should have prompted an equally explicit comparison of the conditions under which preferred liquid systems save total water. Corporate replenishment commitments and market-growth forecasts provide context, but they do not establish comparative performance across climates or the feasibility of rapid deployment.

The geographic argument is stronger as a reason to investigate sites than as evidence of quantified harm. Figure 5 combines national stress categories with facility counts; it does not connect each facility’s consumption to its actual watershed, seasonal availability, or competing demands. A national pattern cannot establish the local burden of a particular cluster. The global simulation likewise cannot determine where an additional litre does most damage. The authors recognise regionalisation as future work, which is an important concession. It means disclosure and local hydrological assessment are well-supported recommendations now, while specific siting restrictions or technology mandates require further evidence. Alternative water sources also need assessment of their existing uses and return flows, rather than an assumption that replacing potable supply eliminates the relevant burden.

The authors’ engineering and water-resource affiliations help explain their attention to measurable efficiency, infrastructure, and governance. Their declared absence of competing interests gives no basis for alleging financial distortion. The more visible bias is a solution-oriented sustainability framing that develops technological mitigation more fully than the social choices behind demand stabilisation. My judgment is shaped by a preference for separating exploratory calculations from demonstrated intervention effects. That preference could undervalue scenarios whose purpose is to motivate preparation before precise data exist. Even allowing for that purpose, the numerical inconsistencies and attribution problem remain consequential. I accept the article’s case for integrated water accounting, disclosure, and locally informed planning. I would use its scenarios to formulate questions, but would withhold confidence in the headline forecast and 95% mitigation claim until their assumptions, calculations, and causal interpretation are reconciled.
