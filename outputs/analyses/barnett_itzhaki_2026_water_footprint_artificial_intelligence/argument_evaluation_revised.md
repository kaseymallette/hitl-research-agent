Source title: The water footprint of artificial intelligence: Emerging solutions and governance imperatives  
Source type: other  
Publisher: Elsevier  
DOI: 10.1016/j.watres.2026.125866

Barnett-Itzhaki makes a persuasive case for treating data center water use as a local allocation problem. The paper is less persuasive when it asks water governance to judge which AI applications deserve to run. Published in Elsevier’s *Water Research* as a “Making Waves” perspective, it reads as an attempt to put computing infrastructure on the agenda of water researchers, engineers, and the people who make siting and allocation decisions. The highlights and graphical abstract announce the policy program before the evidence is presented: change where data centers go, require disclosure, and ask what their workloads are worth. The targeted review in §2 explains the kinds of sources consulted but gives little basis for judging how representative the cases are. I would read the article as an argument that identifies problems and proposes responses, with different levels of evidence for each.

The opening numbers attract attention, but they cannot carry the allocation argument. The introduction moves from an estimated bottle of water for a range of chatbot responses to a global AI projection of 4.2–6.6 billion cubic meters a year by 2027, then to a projection for U.S. data centers. These figures describe different populations and scales. The bottle is memorable precisely because it makes a conditional estimate sound like a property of the response itself. Later sections show why that intuition misleads: the same workload can impose different burdens depending on how and where its heat is rejected. The paper explicitly distinguishes withdrawal from consumption in §2 and acknowledges that many cited figures are withdrawals, even though the review focuses on direct freshwater consumption. Yet §3 compares U.S. data center consumption with global withdrawals to establish that the sector is small in aggregate. The comparison mixes boundaries in both geography and accounting. The better argument survives without it: a global total cannot tell a community how much water will remain available when its watershed is under stress.

The paper earns that local argument in §3.2, though it sometimes asks the evidence to explain too much. The two-thirds figure in the highlights refers in the body to centers built **or planned** since 2022 in the cited analysis. A count of sites is not a measure of the water they consume or the pressure they add at the driest time of year. It also does not establish the paper’s claim of systematic disregard for water availability: to distinguish disregard from a trade-off that decision makers considered and accepted, I would need to see the feasible alternative sites and how water figured in the choices. Colón is more revealing. In the account cited by the paper, industrial cooling continued under existing allocations while drought left agricultural communities relying on delivered water for basic needs. That juxtaposition makes drought priorities a legitimate subject of inquiry even without showing how much the campus contributed to reservoir depletion. It supports asking what happens to permits when supply contracts, and whose needs are protected first. It does not establish how widespread the same failure is or whether cheap water was decisive in this siting decision.

The engineering survey does the most to earn the move toward public oversight. Section 4 shows why a facility can improve its reported site water efficiency while moving costs elsewhere. Dry heat rejection may shift some water use to electricity generation; a lake can receive most of the water back and still bear the burden of warmer discharge. Reclaimed water may ease pressure on potable supplies while competing with another use of the same reclaimed supply. The examples establish that alternatives exist, with different consequences. They cannot rank the options on a common basis. Table 1, for instance, presents an approximate per-megawatt figure for free-air cooling at Luleå, but its own footnote says the facility’s IT capacity is undisclosed, and the reported water volume is withdrawal. The conclusion can cite Luleå as an example of a different cooling design; the disclosed volume alone cannot establish comparative consumption for equivalent computing service. The same caution applies to the author’s preferred incentives. The pool and district heating examples in §4.5 show that someone can use server heat. Whether rewarding heat recovery saves water depends on which heat source it actually displaces, the extra electricity needed to deliver it, and whether demand coincides with supply. Section 6.2 recommends incentives more confidently than §4.5’s evidence warrants.

Of the proposed reforms, mandatory facility reporting is the best supported. Section 5.2 identifies a specific problem with corporate replenishment pledges: an annual company balance does not tell a watershed whether water was restored where and when it was needed. A credible local replenishment project could still help; its benefit has to be assessed against the timing and location of the loss. The same logic applies to disclosure, and here §6.2 goes most of the way: it would extend WUE reporting to on-site consumption, water embedded in electricity supply, and temporal variation. What it leaves unstated is that a ratio of site water to IT energy, even a seasonal one, is not a volume. A watershed manager needs absolute volumes by source, with withdrawals and consumption distinguished. Reporting enables scrutiny; a drought rule or permit condition is what changes allocation. Cumulative review and enforceable drought conditions follow more directly from the Colón example than §6.2's restriction on new construction in "severely-stressed watersheds." That restriction could be justified where a basin has no room for added consumptive demand, but the paper does not define "severely" or say when that threshold is met. Because the rule targets construction rather than consumption, it also leaves unclear how it applies to a proposal that would draw little or no freshwater from the basin, although the preferential permitting for water-efficient cooling in §5.1 points toward an answer.

The most ambitious part of digital water sobriety, in §5.1, starts from a premise I share: when freshwater is scarce, the benefit of a use matters, and ability to pay does not settle who should get it. The paper sensibly proposes a spectrum rather than a ban on whole categories. Even so, contrasting medical diagnosis with recreational imagery makes the judgment easier than it would be in practice. A medical project may duplicate a cheaper, less water-intensive tool; an apparently trivial query may run on a shared model whose marginal water cost is hard to isolate. Section 6.1 concedes that existing facility totals cannot attribute water to particular models or training runs. Even with better attribution, the paper leaves open who judges benefit, against what alternative, and how they handle uncertainty or competing accounts of public value. The evidence better supports the narrower instruments the paper itself lists in §5.1: facility-level water budgets tied to real-time watershed conditions, mandatory water impact assessments for training runs above computational thresholds, and preferential permitting for water-efficient cooling. Those rest on measurable resource costs. Application-level review can be tested where both the resource cost and the claimed benefit can be evaluated. The step §6.2 takes beyond them, tiered governance linking deployment decisions to demonstrated societal benefit, remains a proposal to test.

The author’s affiliations in engineering and sustainability help explain why the paper moves readily from cooling design to resource justice. The water journal setting also makes watershed impacts more visible than they would be in a discussion organized around computing performance. I bring my own preference for rules that can be measured and audited, which makes facility limits look more tractable to me than judgments about social value. That preference can hide unfairness: a measurable limit can still leave essential users with too little if existing allocations are taken for granted. The paper changed my view most by making that distributional question unavoidable. It establishes a strong case for putting local water availability and drought priorities into infrastructure decisions now; it makes a plausible case for testing ways to judge the value of particular workloads, but offers too little evidence to make those judgments the basis of the tiered governance that §6.2 proposes.
