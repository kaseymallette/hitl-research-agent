The strongest case Alissa and colleagues make is for bringing life cycle assessment into cooling design before the layout, equipment and power supply are settled. They show why a more efficient cooling system cannot be judged by the electricity it saves at the rack: it can also change how many servers fit, how much work those servers can do, and what has to be built and replaced. The weaker claim is the one a reader could take from the opening percentages: that installing liquid cooling will cut a data centre’s environmental footprint by roughly those amounts. The evidence supports lower impacts per unit of modeled computing capacity under particular conditions. That is useful enough to change a design decision, but it leaves open what happens to the footprint of the whole facility.

This is an *Analysis* in *Nature*, written by current or former employees of Microsoft and WSP. It speaks to a broad scientific readership while giving engineers and operators a method they can use. The opening makes climate action urgent, treats continued cloud expansion as a given, and turns Microsoft’s own sustainability commitments into a reason to compare cooling designs. The discussion of rising chip power and heat flux (p. 332) gives a good reason to take liquid cooling seriously even apart from the environmental targets. It establishes a thermal problem, though it does not establish that the same solution is needed at every site. Figure 2 shows that Microsoft built and tested prototypes; that makes the designs credible candidates. The environmental percentages, announced in the opening and repeated before the model is laid out, are earned only later, when Tables 3 and 4 and Figure 4 show what configurations, metrics and baselines the authors compared.

The central choice is to compare annual impacts per virtual core (Vcore). For a provider selling general-purpose cloud capacity, this makes more sense than comparing buildings or racks as if each offered the same service. But the denominator is also part of the proposed benefit. Table 3 credits the liquid designs with 25–36% more Vcores than air cooling, reflecting both the additional servers and the capacity attributed to higher clock speeds. The comparison is between whole designs that take advantage of cooling, so the extra capacity belongs in the calculation if customers can use it for comparable work. The article describes the conversion from hardware to virtual capacity and assumes a utilization rate in the operating model. It does not show that the extra capacity translates into a matching increase in useful work across workloads. I would therefore use the results to compare capacity for the kind of cloud the authors modeled, and ask for measured work at comparable service quality before applying the percentages to a different workload.

The difference between capacity and facility impact is visible in the paper’s own numbers. The conclusion’s 10 GW example (p. 337) applies the per-Vcore reductions to an unchanged Vcore count. That arithmetic works for a fixed quantity of comparable service; holding IT capacity fixed does not by itself hold service capacity fixed. If the modeled two-phase centre provides 36% more Vcores (Table 3) and emits 21% less per Vcore on grid power (Table 4), multiplying the two rounded ratios gives 1.36 × 0.79 ≈ 1.07. For the two modeled designs at their respective Vcore counts, that implies about 7% more greenhouse gas emissions in total. This is an implication of the modeled ratios, not an observed increase or a prediction about customer demand. It shows why I would put absolute facility impacts next to impacts per unit of service, and why I would not read the introductory sector emissions target as something the study shows these designs will help meet in absolute terms.

The power comparison makes the argument for early assessment especially well. Figure 4 puts every bar on the same scale: grid-powered air cooling is 100 for each metric. On that scale, renewable air cooling takes the greenhouse gas index from 100 to 11, while switching to any liquid design on the grid takes it to 79–85. The modeled change in power supply matters far more to emissions than the choice among coolants, and server manufacturing becomes more prominent once operating emissions fall. The paper earns that insight by changing a premise and tracing what follows. It also shows why a ranking is fragile: one-phase immersion has a small greenhouse gas advantage over cold plates on grid power, and cold plates lead that pair in the renewable case (Table 4). The authors qualify the ranking, appropriately. I would extend that caution to their confidence in the savings themselves. The pedigree matrix tells readers how well inputs fit the system; even good operating data do not tell us how much the result moves if overclocking, utilization or equipment life changes. The Methods average air and two-phase values to get cold-plate server power and power usage effectiveness, and give both immersion designs the same values for those parameters. These are reasonable starting assumptions for a design study. A sensitivity test could show how much of the comparison survives when they move.

Water and fluid hazards are where the choice of what to count matters most. The opening’s blue water range ends at 52%, but Table 4 gives grid-case reductions of 31%, 45% and 48%; Figure 4 shows 52 as the two-phase impact *remaining* against the grid-air baseline of 100. The table is the clearer guide. Even its water totals cannot tell a siting team how much pressure the facility puts on the local watershed: they include water consumed in power generation as well as at the site, and the Methods make wet cooling depend on outdoor temperature. A similar limit applies to the two-phase fluid. The modeled fluid contribution to greenhouse gas emissions is small, with production and disposal estimates supplied by the manufacturer. That says little about the consequences of a leak or long-term exposure to persistent chemicals. The authors’ own recommendation to screen for health and safety first is better supported by their discussion than their suggestion that broad PFAS restrictions might be impractical. Their model gives regulators a benefit to weigh; it does not show that containment would work reliably at scale or that the benefit warrants a particular restriction or exemption.

Microsoft’s role gives the study access to actual designs, equipment inventories and engineering judgment. It also helps explain why the question is framed as how to accommodate cloud growth efficiently, with less attention to whether existing capacity can meet demand or whether replacing a functioning air-cooled installation is worth the disruption and embodied impacts. The paper acknowledges the latter advantage of air cooling (p. 336) without evaluating a decision about when to retrofit. My own preference is to ask first about absolute emissions and local water use, which can make me underrate a measure that matters to designers choosing how to serve a fixed amount of demand. I would keep both questions in view. The paper makes a convincing case for modeling liquid cooling early, comparing it with an air-cooled baseline that matches the actual decision, and examining power supply and server manufacture alongside cooling. It gives a shortlist of plausible designs. The choice among them still requires evidence about delivered work, site conditions and fluid hazards that the headline percentages cannot supply.
