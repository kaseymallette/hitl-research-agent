Source title: The water use of data center workloads: A review and assessment of key determinants
Source type: peer_reviewed_paper
Publisher: Elsevier
DOI: 10.1016/j.resconrec.2025.108310

Lei and colleagues make a convincing argument that water efficiency cannot be judged from water per unit of electricity alone. They make a less complete argument that their workload metric provides impartial comparisons or establishes priorities for water conservation. Published in Resources, Conservation and Recycling, the paper addresses an audience spanning environmental researchers, engineers, operators, and policymakers. It combines a review with modeling to redirect attention toward computing efficiency and integrated system design. Its purpose is both explanatory and persuasive: to change what practitioners measure, then influence what they improve. The first ambition is substantially achieved; the second requires distinctions that the paper sometimes compresses.

The argument's strongest premise is earned early. Equation (4), on manuscript page 7, expresses overall water per workload as server electricity per workload multiplied by the sum of site WUE and PUE times the electricity water consumption factor. The reasoning follows: equal water per kWh need not mean equal water per service when servers deliver different amounts of work per kWh. Figure 3 makes the point through two configurations with equal facility water per workload despite markedly different site WUE. This counterexample is sufficient to defeat an inference from lower WUE to greater workload efficiency. It does not require the modeled configurations to be common. The authors also avoid simply replacing one isolated indicator with another: they retain distinctions between onsite water, electricity, and overall water throughout the decomposition.

Much of the results section elaborates this accounting logic. The successive plots first introduce server performance, then cooling and climate, then electricity supply. Their repeated examples of equal outcomes from different configurations make the reasoning accessible, but their accumulation should not be mistaken for repeated independent validation. Several results are consequences of the same equation applied to modeled inputs. The numerical breadth depends on those inputs: SPECpower server observations, fitted performance surfaces, cooling simulations, and generation-weighted U.S. electricity factors. Thus the abstract's more-than-10,000-fold variation is best understood as a span across constructed possibilities. It does not establish the distribution of water intensity among operating data centers, the frequency of the extremes, or the savings available to a typical facility. The headline appears before the reader learns these qualifications, giving the numerical span greater apparent generality than its evidential basis warrants.

The modeling nevertheless contributes something beyond algebra. Jointly simulating PUE and site WUE acknowledges that cooling choices change electricity and water together. Figure 4 and the cooling descriptions in SI Section S3 explain why evaporative heat rejection can save electricity while consuming water, and why liquid cooling's ultimate water requirements depend on the heat rejection system. This is sound grounds for rejecting technology labels as sufficient evidence of environmental performance. The authors' preferred economizers, adiabatic systems, and liquid cooling deserve the same conditional treatment. Their favorable simulated regions demonstrate opportunities under specified temperatures, weather, and operating practices; they do not establish that every installation can reach those regions. SI Section S6 itself attributes very low water use in one adiabatic configuration partly to advanced operating practices associated with major internet operators. That qualification makes replication of the operating conditions part of the proposal, rather than an automatic benefit of selecting the equipment.

The proposed denominator also needs more scrutiny than it receives. SPECpower provides a standardized basis for comparing volume servers, making it a reasonable demonstration tool. Standardizing a benchmark, however, does not establish equivalence among the services delivered by different facilities. A benchmark operation is not automatically interchangeable with a production transaction, stored information, or an AI inference at a specified quality and latency. The authors explicitly recognize the need for other benchmarks and acknowledge missing GPU, storage, and network loads on manuscript page 27. Those concessions support the portability of the accounting framework, while limiting the present numerical results. They do not justify the stronger language of impartial comparisons on page 26. Fair comparisons require equivalent service definitions and system boundaries as well as an improved resource denominator. The paper establishes a necessary improvement in measurement without establishing a universal comparison standard.

The sensitivity ranking is particularly vulnerable to being read as more decisive than it is. Figure 8 identifies server efficiency, electricity WCF, and utilization as the largest modeled contributors, but their 95% confidence intervals overlap substantially. The plot supports treating them as a leading group more securely than insisting on their exact order. More fundamentally, variance contributions depend on the ranges and distributions assigned to inputs. The study contrasts hardware efficiency quantiles and broad electricity-source differences; SI Section S5 spans utilization from 10% to 80%. Those are useful exploratory choices, but they do not measure the changes available at a particular site. A large contribution to modeled variance does not establish the cheapest intervention, the greatest feasible saving, or the strongest policy incentive. The case for considering these levers is persuasive; a universal order of action is not earned.

The electricity comparison illustrates both the benefit and the limit of broad boundaries. Including upstream and generation-stage water prevents onsite savings from concealing consumption elsewhere. But a U.S. technology-average factor is not a site-specific account of the electricity displaced by an intervention. The authors acknowledge regional and technological variation in Section 2.4, yet recommendations to use wind or solar move quickly past questions of availability and actual supply substitution. There is also a concrete numerical inconsistency: Figure 6(b) gives wind's WCF as 0.011 L/kWh, whereas SI Table S4.1 gives a total of 0.0148 after adding generation-stage consumption. This does not reverse wind's favorable position relative to the high-WCF sources. It does make the exact low-end bounds less secure and reinforces the need to distinguish robust qualitative comparisons from precise numerical claims.

The largest policy gap concerns what counts as conservation success. The introduction motivates the study through local water stress, including The Dalles, while the model adds liters consumed in different places and divides by work. Equal totals need not impose equal pressure on affected communities. A configuration with lower overall consumption could still consume more onsite during a locally critical period. Annual average results based on representative-city typical weather, as described in SI Section S3, cannot resolve that question. Recognizing omitted withdrawals on manuscript pages 27-28 is helpful, but adding withdrawals alone would not supply the missing location, timing, and scarcity judgments. The same evidence could reasonably support local consumption constraints alongside workload efficiency targets, even where that combination does not minimize the unweighted total.

Similarly, the claim that these measures can reduce national water use while maximizing service output requires an assumption about demand. Lower liters per workload reduces total liters at a fixed workload; it does not ensure lower totals when workloads grow. This matters because digitalization and AI growth motivate the paper from its opening paragraph. The proposed co-benefits are plausible, but the model does not evaluate demand responses, implementation costs, or the manufacturing consequences of faster server replacement. These omissions do not invalidate operational efficiency improvements. They limit the inference from operational intensity savings to an overall conservation policy, particularly when alternative measures such as workload restraint or local limits receive little attention.

The authors' institutional setting helps explain this emphasis. Their energy, engineering, environmental management, and statistical affiliations favor a tractable systems model and technical improvement. Their stated public and philanthropic support gives no basis for inferring an undisclosed commercial motive. The framing nevertheless treats digital service provision as the objective to preserve and resource intensity as the variable to optimize. My own inclination is to demand evidence about local consequences and implementation before accepting policy prescriptions; that standard is broader than a determinant study must satisfy. On its stated analytical task, the paper succeeds: it shows why computing performance belongs in water accounting and why several interacting determinants must be considered together. Its recommendations are credible candidates for site-specific evaluation, but the model does not establish a general ranking of interventions or make workload efficiency sufficient evidence of water sustainability.
